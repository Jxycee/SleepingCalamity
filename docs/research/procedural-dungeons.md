# Procedural dungeons that feel different every visit but familiar

Research for the Undercroft Cellars floors (PHASE1 7.2). Written 2026-10-31 in our own words: mechanics and numbers only, no code copied. Worry being answered: players feel the same path every time even when the layout changes.

Reading method (second pass, 2026-10-31): sources were downloaded as raw text into a scratchpad directory outside the repo (nothing built or run) and read after stripping markup; Spelunky Classic's GameMaker project was unpacked with a Python script that only decompresses text blocks. First-pass items read through a summarising fetch tool were re-read raw where marked. Tags: [P] = primary (source code, developer file, or developer/community symbol documentation read directly); "read raw" = a third party's or developer's page read as raw text; "secondary" = a description by someone else or a search-engine summary. Licences recorded per source; no code or text is copied.

## 1. Per source

### 1.1 Brogue Community Edition (primary, source code)
- URL: https://github.com/tmewett/BrogueCE (files `src/brogue/Architect.c`, `src/variants/GlobalsBrogue.c`). Licence: AGPL-3.0 (copyleft: we must never copy code from it; ideas and numbers only).
- Generator steps, in order:
  1. Carve the base map: a first room, then repeat "design a random room in empty space, slide it around the map in shuffled order until one of its door sites touches an existing wall, then attach". Room kinds: cave blob, cross, symmetrical cross, small box, circle, chunky (blobby box); a hallway can be prepended to a room. Room-kind frequencies and the corridor chance shift with depth (more caves and long corridors up top, more of one other kind deeper).
  2. Add loops: for every wall cell with floor on both sides, make a door if the walking distance between the two sides is above 20 steps. This is the "secondary connection" pass. Loops come from a rule on shortcut value, not a fixed count.
  3. Lakes (water, lava, chasm, brimstone): blob shapes tried large to small (30x15 shrinking by 2x1 to 20x10, minimum 4x4), accepted only if a flood fill proves the level stays connected (10 placement tries each). Lake wreaths (shallow edges) are added.
  4. Plain "autogenerators": a table row says terrain or decoration, which depth range, a percent chance, a count formula (intercept plus slope times depth, capped). Examples: granite columns (depth 1+, 60%, max 4), grass thinning out by depth 10, dead grass depth 4-14, bones from 12, torches in walls from 6 (5%, max 12), statues, luminescent fungus from 7, pre-revealed traps at 20% each in a depth band, hidden traps later. Sunlight patches (0-5) and darkness patches (1-15) are autogenerators too, which is a ready-made per-floor light variant.
  5. Machines (the interesting part, below).
  6. Bridges across chasms and lakes, only where they shorten a long detour.
  7. Doors: about 60% of door sites become real doors; some become secret doors with depth.
- Machines ("blueprints"): a table of about 80 hand-written recipes. A recipe gives a depth range, a room size range, a frequency weight, flags and a list of features (each feature: how many instances, where in the room, what item or monster, constraints such as "far from the door" or "in the walls"). Four families:
  - Reward rooms (item library choose-one-of-many 30 weight at depth 1-12, single-category library 15, pedestal with a guaranteed good item 5-16, consumable pedestals 10+, commutation altars and resurrection altar 13+, allies in cages or chains 5+, legendary ally 8+, goblin warren 5-15 and sentinel sanctuary 10-23 as big set-pieces). Frequency weights run from 5 to 50, depth gates run from 1 to 13.
  - Vestibules (the "door" to a reward room): locked door with the key held by a key-holder machine (weight 100, the default), secret door (weight 1), lever + portcullis (8, from depth 4), flammable barricade (10, depth 1-6), statue to shatter (6), statue bursting into a monster (6, depth 5+), pit-trap field (8), mirror totem (8, depth 5+), guardian-on-glyph (8, depth 6+), throwing tutorial with a pressure plate (8, depth 1-4 only).
  - Key holders (where the key to a locked vestibule lives): about 30 puzzle rooms, each weighted 1-35 and depth gated: collapsing floor, rat trap, flood room, fire-trap room, levitation over a pit, lava moat, poison gas, burning grass, guardian water puzzle, guardian gauntlet and corridor, sacrifice altar, electric crystals, zombie crypt (12+), haunted house (16+), boss with the key (18), plus a "nested item library" (weight 35) where one of the choices is the key to another vault. A key holder is also a plain secret room (weight 1).
  - Flavour machines (no reward, pure scenery): bloodwort plant, abandoned shrine, idyll with ponds, swamp, camp, remnant, dismal (blood and bones), chasm catwalk, lake walk, paralysis-trap area, statue that comes alive, worms in walls, sentinels. Each has its own chance row in the autogenerator table (for example shrine 7% from depth 5, bloodwort 25%, idyll 15% depth 1-5, remnant 15% from depth 10), capped at 1-5 per level.
- Rarity budget for reward machines: a level is owed a reward room when the count generated so far, scaled, falls behind the depth reached (the shipped constants amount to roughly one per three to four levels, with the multiplier 4, offset 2 and factor 1). On top of that comes a random extra: 40% on the first two levels when none is owed, otherwise 15% per extra, repeatedly. That is a pity timer plus random bonus: a long dry spell raises the count owed, so rewards never go missing for long, but the exact floors vary.
- Item-key link: the reward room "outsources" its key to a second machine, and key-holder machines can themselves be vaults needing a key, so chains of 2-3 steps appear.
- What makes it feel varied: nearly everything is data rows with a depth window and a weight, so the combination on any level is different, and each machine is a mini-puzzle with its own rules (so two visits differ in what you must do, not only in what the walls look like). What keeps it familiar: the same recipes recur and are learnable; the vault's door is always a recognisable "kind of obstacle".
- Fixed vs procedural: depth-1 start room is fixed in kind (the entrance room); the amulet floor and its holder machine are forced. Everything else is procedural.

### 1.2 Spelunky Classic 1.1 (primary, developer's own released project)
- Source: official site https://spelunkyworld.com/original.html links the source zip `http://www.derekyu.com/games/spelunky_1_1_src.zip` (680 KB, two GameMaker project files, `spelunky.gmk` and `config.gmk`). The scripts are zlib-compressed text blocks inside the file; I decompressed them with a Python script and read the level scripts as text (nothing run, nothing built). Licence: "Spelunky User License v1.1b" (free to read, modify and share for learning; may not be sold; no copy in the repo). Kazemi's blog (https://tinysubversions.com/spelunkyGen/ and /spelunkyGen2/, secondary, no licence) describes the same code.
- Level = 4x4 rooms, each 10x8 tiles [P]. Path: start room in a random top-row column; each step rolls 1-5 (1-2 left, 3-4 right, 5 down); at the left edge it rolls 3-5, at the right edge 5-7 (so down is more likely at the edges) [P]. Going down marks the room left as a "drop" room (type 2) and the room entered as a "landing" (type 3). Leaving the bottom row sets the exit room. Room-path types in the script: 0 side, 1 left-right, 2 drop, 3 landing, 4/5 shop (shop door on the left/right), 6 special (Moai or City of Gold), 7/8/9 top/middle/bottom of a tall special shaft (snake pit, sacrifice pit, alien craft, lake) [P].
- Template counts, Mines script only (`scrRoomGen`; the jungle, ice and temple scripts have their own tables with different counts, for example jungle has 10 main, 7 landing, 6 drops) [P]: start room 8 (4 normal + 4 with a hole, chosen 1-4 or 5-8 depending on whether the start is a drop), exit room 6, side rooms 9 + 1 idol room + 1 altar room, main left-right rooms 12, landing rooms 8, drop rooms 12 (if the room above is also a drop only the first 8 are allowed; in a snake-pit shaft 4-12), shop 7 flavours (general, bomb, weapon, rare, clothing, craps, kissing), snake pit 1 top + 1 bottom. So a normal Mines room is one of only 8-12 hand-drawn templates [P]. Several templates are written in mirrored pairs, picked 50/50 (ladder rooms, "treasure below"), but I found no generic left-right flip in the Classic room script; the "rooms are flipped" remark in the old note comes from blog descriptions of later versions and is not primary-verified.
- Layers inside a room [P]: a template is an 80-character string. Tile `1` solid (10% become a harder block variant), `2` solid half the time, `7` spikes 1 in 3, `4` push block 1 in 4. Three marker tiles are replaced by hand-drawn 5x3 "obstacle" chunks: ground marker (16 variants), air marker (10 variants), door/entrance marker (8 variants). So each room has a template roll, 0-3 chunk rolls and per-tile coin flips.
- Rarity table (probabilities are "1 in N", set at run start) [P]: dark level 1/12 (levels past the first only, once per run, suppressed when the run clock's seconds field is 20 or less, which the author comments as a guard against speedrunners), snake pit 1/8 per candidate shaft in the top two rows, cemetery 1/10, lake 1/10, yeti lair 1/6, alien craft 1/10, sacrifice pit 1/8, idol: one side-room roll in ten per room until one exists, altar 1/16 per side room from level 2 until one exists. Shop: rolls "1..level <= 2" so level 2 always, level 3 two-in-three, level 4 one-in-two, and so on down; it goes in an empty cell beside the path. Moai is a guaranteed escalation: level 9 1/4, level 10 1/3, level 11 1/2, level 12 certain, unless already made [P].
- Pity counters in the entity pass [P]: the market-entrance and gold-door placements roll "1 in counter" at each candidate wall, and on every miss the counter drops by one, starting at 160 for both. The chance climbs each miss, so once a level is flagged to have one it nearly always places one, and which wall holds it is random. This is the same idea as Brogue's owed counter, in two lines of code.
- What makes it varied: a macro path snake, a template per room, chunks per template, tile coin flips and rare shafts. What keeps it familiar: always 4x4, the same 8-12 templates per type, and exits in a predictable direction (down).
- Spelunky 2 (secondary tooling docs plus the Overlunky API listing, MIT): the room-template vocabulary is a flat list of about 140 names: path normal / drop / no-top / drop-no-top, ground, air and door chunks, passage rooms, big, wide and tall "machine" rooms with path and side variants and a key room plus reward room pair, vault, shops (dice, curio, ghist), challenge rooms, idol and altar, coffins, and "feeling" levels (factory, prison, tomb). Levels may be up to 8 wide by 15 tall (CustomLevels README). Hand-set rooms use names like setroom0-0, and procedural spawn odds are set per level as `inverse_chance` (a 1-in-N number) with about 100 chance ids (traps such as spike ball, crusher, snap trap; every enemy kind; critters; gold). The actual .lvl contents and numbers are not published (see section 4). URLs: https://github.com/spelunky-fyi/overlunky (`docs/game_data/spel2.lua`), https://github.com/jaythebusinessgoose/CustomLevels, https://github.com/spelunky-fyi/modlunky2.

### 1.3 DevilutionX, Diablo 1 level generators (primary, source code)
- URL: https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX (`Source/levels/drlg_l1..l4.cpp`, `themes.cpp`). Licence: GitHub reports "other" (the repository's Sustainable Use License; source-available, restricts commercial use): read-only reference, nothing copied. It rebuilds the original game around data files we do not own.
- Cathedral (level type 1): as in the previous note (3 chambers of 10x10 on a 40x40 mega-tile area with a 6-wide hall, regenerate until a minimum area).
- Catacombs (l2) [P]: place a first room, then recursively split the free space and add rooms of random size 4-10 tiles per side (hard cap 80 rooms per level), joining each new room to its neighbour with a hall that bends at random; the whole thing re-rolls until stairs fit; a quest set-piece (one of three hand-drawn blocks, chosen by which quest is active) is stamped into a reserved room; then theme rooms (size 6-10), then roughly 130 "mini-set" passes that each drop a small hand-drawn feature (door types, arches, columns) at a given percentage, from 100% for the arches and doors down to 1% for the rarest decorations, with a few at 3% and 20%.
- Caves (l3) [P]: grow outward from a small seed block by random fill of diagonals, straights and singles; reject unless the floor area is big enough; then optional "pools": slime/lava pools are flood-filled into closed regions of at most 40 tiles, each accepted with a 25% roll and only if the region is larger than 4 tiles. A separate 4-pattern "slime pool" pass accepts the level only if at least 3 of its 4 patterns (30%, 40%, 50%, 60%) placed. Quest set-piece rooms exist here too (anvil, nest).
- Hell (l4) [P]: build only one quarter of the map (a first room 2-6 tiles per side, 14x14 on the last level, then recursive splits with a 1 in 4 chance of flipping orientation) and mirror it into the other three quadrants; the quarter is rejected until the total area reaches a minimum (692 tiles in the quarter units). The quest set-piece sits in the reserved first room.
- Theme rooms (`themes.cpp`) [P], confirming the earlier claim: after layout, enclosed rooms of fitting size are found; a "good" subset of four themes (goat shrine, shrine, skeleton room, library) is tried first, falling back to any of 17; fountains (four kinds), cauldron (hell only), armour stand, weapon rack and treasure room are once-per-level flags, so two never appear on the same floor.
- Set-pieces: quest blocks are stamped after generation into a reserved room, which is the model for a fixed chamber inside a procedural floor. Boris the Brave's full write-up (https://www.boristhebrave.com/2019/07/14/dungeon-generation-in-diablo-1/, secondary, read raw) agrees with the source.

### 1.4 Pokemon Mystery Dungeon, Explorers of Sky floor generation (primary: pmdsky-debug symbol descriptions)
- URL: https://github.com/UsernameFodder/pmdsky-debug (`symbols/overlay29.yml`, `headers/types/dungeon_mode/dungeon_mode.h`). Licence: repository GPL-3.0; `symbols/` directory MIT. These are a community's own descriptions of the game's code, written from reverse engineering, so they carry no game assets; mechanics only. The pret decomp (https://github.com/pret/pmd-sky) has only one generator file in C (secondary-terrain rivers and lakes); the rest of generation is still assembly there, so the yml descriptions are the readable source.
- Floor settings (a 32-byte record per floor) [P]: layout (12 kinds: large, small, medium, one-room monster house, outer ring, crossroads, two rooms with a monster house, line, cross, beetle, outer rooms), room density (a positive value gets a random -2..+1 jitter, at least 1; negative means exact), connectivity, enemy density, Kecleon shop chance 0-100, monster house chance 0-100, maze room chance 0-100, "itemless monster house" chance, whether dead ends are allowed, max secondary structures, flags for secondary structures and ragged rooms, extra hallways count, item/trap/buried-item density, secondary terrain density, visibility range (non-zero turns on darkness), hidden-stairs type and chance, fixed room id. Nearly every "dice" in the generator is a per-floor percentage in data, which is the lesson: variety is data rows, not code.
- Standard floor [P]: grid up to 15x15 cells (floor map 56x32 tiles); between 2 and 36 cells become rooms; the rest become one-tile hallway anchors; connections come from a random walk with 50% momentum, run "connectivity" times; connections are hallways or, 9.75% of the time, two rooms merge into one; 40% of rooms (where allowed) get ragged walls; extra hallways are random-walk tunnels. Then special features in this order: maze room (needs odd dimensions), Kecleon shop (a room at least 5x4, fills the interior leaving a 1 tile border), monster house (any connected, non-merged, non-maze room), extra hallways, imperfections, secondary structures (dice 0-5: none, maze, checkerboard of water/lava, central pool, island with items and warp tile inside a moat, divider). Other layouts are small hand-coded footprints (outer ring 4x2 rooms, cross of 5 rooms, line of 5, beetle 3x3 with a merged centre column).
- Failure handling [P]: a floor is re-rolled if the layout is invalid or entity spawning fails; after 10 failures it falls back to a single-room monster house, which the designers also use as a layout in its own right.
- Fixed rooms [P]: over a hundred named fixed-room ids (boss chambers, story floors, secret room 113, "substitute" room); a revisit-override table swaps the fixed room used on a floor once the dungeon has been cleared before (so a story floor plays differently the second time). Monster-spawn lists are capped at 14 species per floor and the species are chosen randomly from the floor's list (Kecleon and a decoy always included).
- What makes it varied: the layout kind and count of rooms per floor, merges and secondary structures, and the per-floor ambush chance. Familiar: the room grid skeleton. Secondary: Rust reimplementation docs (https://eosrs.pmdcollab.org/) repeat the same numbers.
- Not obtainable: the per-dungeon floor values (which floors have which percentages) sit in game ROM data, not in the symbol files.

### 1.6 Developer write-ups
- **Dead Cells** (lead designer Sebastien Benard, Game Developer, 29 March 2017, "Building the Level Design of a procedurally generated Metroidvania: a hybrid approach", https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/building-the-level-design-of-a-procedurally-generated-metroidvania-a-hybrid-approach-, read raw; same text as the earlier deepnight.net copy). Six steps: fixed world map; hand-built room tiles in CastleDB, each tagged with a biome, entrance/exit counts and purpose; a concept graph per biome (length, special room count, how labyrinthine, entrance-to-exit distance); the generator tries random rooms from the biome until one matches each node; monster count is set by total combat-room length (the article's "250 tiles, 1 per 5, 50 monsters" are illustrative numbers, not shipped ones) with per-kind spacing and weights; loot last (kept secret). The article gives no room counts and says nothing about secret rooms; their "pacing peaks and breathers" idea came from Left 4 Dead's director. They credit Spelunky for the hybrid approach.
- **Enter the Gungeon** (Boris the Brave, https://www.boristhebrave.com/2019/07/28/dungeon-generation-in-enter-the-gungeon/, read raw; secondary, analysis of the game's files). Correction to the first note: the stage with the fewest hand-authored flows is the first, the Hollow, with 4, and the Gungeon Proper has the most, 8; each flow is built around one signature feature (huge loop, multi-way fork, forced pass through the shop). A flow is a directed graph (tree plus extra edges for loops). Transformations: some rooms become random-length chains (later stages only), alternate paths (used twice). "Injection" adds special nodes with spawn probability and preconditions; secret rooms prefer dead ends but have a 1 in 5 chance to attach to any room, 90% chance to appear. Rooms are picked per node from a pool (nearly 300 in stage one) without repeats. Layout: break the flow into loops and trees, lay out each separately by matching exits (up to 3 retries per room choice), close each loop with a small room or a pathfound corridor 4-30 units long (up to 50 in the mines), then glue composites together starting from the most connected room. Boris notes the generator does the hardest, most central parts first. A developer comment under the post says the goal was to approximate a Zelda dungeon each time, and that most chests sit at the end of a one-way loop (risk for reward).
- **Unexplored** (https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/04/10/dungeon-generation-in-unexplored/, read raw). Confirmed: 20 level requests per game, a 5x5 node grid, a start, a goal and a rough circle, 24 major cycle types, minor cycles, dead ends, about 5,000 rewrite rules in about 50 modules, themes ("biomes") that steer many independent systems at once, grid resolution raised stepwise (x2, then x5) before the tile map. Lock-and-key placeholders keep the pair valid across rewrites (https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/02/27/lock-and-key-dungeons/ explains mission graphs; hard locks versus soft locks that allow a skilled bypass).
- **Binding of Isaac** (https://www.boristhebrave.com/2020/09/12/dungeon-generation-in-binding-of-isaac/, read raw, with input from the developers; new). 9x8 grid, rooms per floor = random(2) + 5 + 2.6 x floor (7-8 on floor 1, plus 2-3 each floor); grow by breadth-first from the start, skipping any cell that already has two neighbours (so no loops) with a 50% give-up per direction; the boss goes in the last dead end (always among the farthest from start, and never adjacent to it); the secret room is wedged where at least three rooms meet, away from dead ends; other special rooms take random dead ends with conditional odds (a sacrifice room about 1 in 7, rising to about 1 in 3 when the player is at full health); room contents come from three pools (easy, medium, hard; stage one of a chapter uses easy+medium, stage two medium+hard; 174 normal rooms in the first chapter); a rare double-size "labyrinth" floor has 80% more rooms (max 45) and special rooms restricted to the 6 farthest dead ends. A whole game generated from a simple growth rule, hand-drawn room pools and conditional specials.
- **Cogmind** (Josh Ge, Grid Sage Games; read through the ModDB mirrors, original posts at https://www.gridsagegames.com/blog/2015/05/map-composition etc., a custom blog with no stated licence). About two dozen map types fed by four sources: a tunneling room-and-corridor generator driven by dozens of parameters (so one algorithm gives layouts that look different yet theme-consistent), cellular automata caves (mines), encounters, and prefabs. Content on tunneler maps is weighed by depth and map type; automata maps are filled only by encounters. Encounters come in four categories: fluff (atmosphere), reward (free), risk-versus-reward (obstacle then prize), danger (hostile, no prize); the unusual ones are kept to optional branch maps so the main route stays predictable and fair; prefabs may rotate and shift their position. His layout lesson for balance: exits are deliberately placed far from entrances through pre-drawn orientation templates (several per map type, randomly one per map), with optional barrier lines to make routes circuitous, tested by watching generation previews for around 30 minutes per map type. Also stresses loops, openness and room-size consistency as tuned parameters (https://www.moddb.com/games/cogmind/news/roguelike-level-design-addendum-procedural-layouts).
- **DunGen** (https://dungen-docs.aegongames.com/2.18/, official documentation of a commercial Unity asset; proprietary; read raw). Dungeon flow: a main-path length range, a doorway "connection chance" (a probability that two touching but unconnected doors are joined, optionally restricted to the same section so loops cannot skip a gate), straightening chance, branch mode (per-tile, global or per-section), branch counts and depth ranges per archetype, and prune tags that forbid dead ends ending in a corridor. Weights have a main-path weight, a branch weight and a depth curve (0 at the start to 1 at the goal) so a tile can be "common early, rare late" or "branch only". Props: local prop set keeps a random min-max subset of pre-placed children; random prefab picks one weighted prefab at a marker; global props cap a category across the whole dungeon by priority (it never adds missing ones). Injection: rules add a required or optional tile from a tile set at a normalised depth range on the main path or branches, optionally locked; keys are guaranteed reachable before their lock on the main path. Regeneration retries until constraints hold.
- **Lethal Company and DunGen** (search-engine summaries of mod pages, not read raw except the LethalLevelLoader README, which only describes an API for loading custom dungeons and levels, MIT licence). Lethal Company uses DunGen dungeon flows and tile sets; LethalLevelLoader (https://github.com/IAmBatby/LethalLevelLoader) injects extra flows, archetypes and tiles, and a search summary says the host alone picks the flow and dungeon size and sends them to clients to prevent desync (not confirmed from the README). Mod pages mention dungeon mods with "30+ tiles" and "21 tiles", i.e. content packs of that size. Vanilla flow data was not found.

## 2. Synthesis: the layers of variety

Ranked by perceived variety per unit of authoring cost (best first). Items marked [P] now rest on primary reading.

1. **Rare vignettes and set-pieces with a rarity budget** (Brogue machines and flavour rows [P], Diablo quest blocks and theme rooms [P], PMD monster house [P], Gungeon special rooms, Spelunky shafts [P], Cogmind encounters). One authored scene, 1 floor in 4-10, beats any layout change. Rarity is always a "1 in N" or a percentage, and the better games soften the dry-spell luck: Brogue's owed counter, Spelunky's falling counter for market and gold entrances [P], Spelunky's Moai escalation 1/4, 1/3, 1/2, certain [P]. Tag each vignette fluff / reward / risk-reward / danger (Cogmind) and keep a mix.
2. **Optional dressing with a fill probability** (Brogue autogenerators [P], Spelunky tile coin flips [P], DunGen local prop sets, Diablo mini-sets at 1-100% [P]). Costs nothing per item.
3. **Encounter and event variety** (PMD monster house per-floor percent [P], Dead Cells budget by combat length, Isaac conditional specials [P], Hades reward door as secondary). Reuses existing enemies. PMD also caps and randomly selects which species can appear on a floor (14 of the list [P]), an easy way to make two floors of one theme feel different.
4. **Per-visit lighting and atmosphere variants** (Brogue sunlight and darkness patches [P], Spelunky's 1/12 dark level [P], PMD per-floor visibility range [P], Unexplored themes). One knob changes every room at once.
5. **In-room chunks and obstacle blocks** (Spelunky 5x3 chunks with 8 to 16 variants per marker kind [P]). Several swappable regions in one room multiply that room's looks.
6. **Macro graph variants** (PMD 12 layout kinds [P], Gungeon 4-8 flows per stage, Unexplored 24 cycle types [P], Brogue lakes, Cogmind's several orientation templates per map type). A floor with a ring plays differently from a long spine. Cheap form: a small enum of layout presets plus a start/exit distance bound.
7. **Room-template pool size** (Gungeon about 300 for one stage, Isaac 174 for chapter one [P], Spelunky 8-12 per type [P], ours 21). Linear in cost; Spelunky shows that a tiny pool survives when chunks and tile flips add noise.
8. **Fixed anchors** (entry, exit, checkpoint, boss floor, Dead Cells world map, Diablo's reserved set-piece room [P], PMD's fixed rooms and revisit override [P]). They are the "familiar". Keep few; vary what surrounds them.

Why a player feels "same dungeon path" (observed across sources): (a) same start-to-exit distance and direction (Isaac forces the boss to a far dead end [P]; Cogmind places exits deliberately far and picks a random orientation template [P]; Gungeon flows differ in one noticeable feature), (b) same room-kind sequence, (c) rooms that look alike once walls are ignored, and (d) no unpredictable event.

## 3. Recommendations mapped onto our system

Our system (re-checked against `Core/Cellars/` on main at the M5 merge): `CellarRoom` data grids with doors, dressing sockets (2-3 variants), spawns, chests; `CellarRoomPool` 21 rooms (12 combat, 3 sub-themes); `CellarFloorGenerator` on a 14x14 grid of 4 m cells, 4-6 rooms, link to a random earlier room, 1-3 loops, 1-3 dead ends, a 50% locked leaf room with its key chest, 6-8 chests per floor, retries then a minimal-plan fallback. Rooms rotate four ways.

Labels: **Phase 1 cheap**, **later**, **big**. "[changed]" marks a recommendation altered by the second pass.

1. **Optional sockets with a fill chance (Phase 1 cheap).** Unchanged: `Chance` 0-100 on `SocketSpec`/`DressingSocket`, default 100, floor RNG; about a third of sockets at 50-70%; an "empty" variant allowed. Spelunky's per-tile flips (25-50%) and DunGen's min-max subset are the references [P].
2. **Socket placement jitter (Phase 1 cheap).** Unchanged: 2-3 alternative cells along a wall per socket group.
3. **Per-visit atmosphere variant (Phase 1 cheap) [changed].** One roll per floor from a table (lantern density, fog tint, dust, a darker floor). Add the Spelunky-style rare dark floor: about 1 in 12, not on floor 1, never twice in a dive, and the same condition that skips it for a rushed player is optional. Derive from the floor seed so clients agree.
4. **Vignette system with rarity budget (Phase 1 cheap for 4-6) [changed].** Replace the single "owed counter forces one at 3" with two cheaper-to-reason-about mechanisms: (a) per-vignette rarity as a 1-in-N number with a falling counter like Spelunky's market and gold doors (each miss lowers N by one, so it almost certainly fires within a handful of floors), and (b) a floor-gated escalation for any "must see once" vignette (1/4, 1/3, 1/2, certain over four floors, the Moai pattern). Keep Brogue's owed idea as the world-save field (counters in the save section, with a note under `sc-netcode`). Add "once per floor" flags (Diablo) so two vignettes of one kind never share a floor, and tag each as fluff / reward / risk-reward / danger; floor roll should try to include one of each at most. Also allow odds conditioned on player state (Isaac's sacrifice room is likelier at full health): for us, a rest or healing vignette is likelier when the party is hurt.

Loops must not bypass the spine: DunGen's "restrict connections to the same section" suggests only joining rooms in the same half of the path.
8. **Cyclic lock-and-key option (later).** Unchanged. Add Boris's hard-versus-soft lock split: a soft lock can be a shortcut door or an optional hazard. Store the lock-to-key relation so the validator can prove solvability; DunGen guarantees keys sit before their lock on the main path.

Ambush odds as a plain per-floor percentage in data, like PMD's monster-house chance [P].
10. **Doorway variants as vestibules (later).** Unchanged.
11. **Fixed floor-4 set-piece (keep) [changed].** Stay fixed, but model PMD's fixed-room revisit override: after the first clear, floor 4 may swap in an alternate dressing set or a changed encounter, so the single fixed room does not become a rerun. Vary entry-side dressing and atmosphere each dive (items 1-3).

## Addendum: graph rewriting (Unexplored) and NetHack's Gnomish Mines

Sources, all read as raw text on 2026-10-31 and restated in our own words (mechanics and numbers only): (A) https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/04/02/graph-rewriting/, (B) https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/04/02/phantomgrammar-and-ludoscope/, (C) https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/04/10/dungeon-generation-in-unexplored/ (the follow-up that shows how the rules are put together; first read in the earlier pass, re-read here for the rule chaining), (D) https://www.boristhebrave.com/2026/10/18/dungeon-generation-in-the-gnomish-mines-of-nethack/. All are secondary (an analyst working from the tools and developer help), not source code. One quote, from (C), quoting the developer: "so much more interesting to generate small levels".

### How graph rewriting works (A)
- The level is a graph: nodes carry data (type, tags), edges link them. A rule has a find side (a small pattern of typed nodes and edges) and a replace side. Nodes are numbered so the rule can say "this one stays, changes type, or is new/deleted"; edges to the rest of the graph that the pattern does not mention are kept. Applying a rule edits only the matched part, and the result can match again, so a rule can grow a structure each time it fires.
- It can make chains, forks, hubs and cycles from a handful of rules. Few shipped games use it in full: Gungeon only rewrites single-room patterns; the Dungeon Architect asset supports multi-node patterns but not loops.
- The lock-and-key use (from the Dormans and Bakkes mission paper): a rule that turns a straight line of tasks into a branch; a rule that inserts a lock plus its key; a rule that moves a lock one step forward; a rule that moves a key one step back. Composed, they shuffle a valid mission into many other valid missions.

### What PhantomGrammar and Ludoscope add (B)
- Nodes and edges hold arbitrary attributes; patterns can match on attribute values and on conditions (for example, incoming edge count); replacements can pick among random alternatives, run code, and randomise values. The same engine also rewrites strings and tile maps.
- Rules never run all at once. A module has a recipe: a sequence of steps, each either iterating a rule set once (one random rule, once) or executing it (repeat a fixed count, or until nothing matches). Three modes decide what happens with several matches: normal (one random match), L-system (visit matches in fixed order and apply to every one, so earlier edits are visible to later matches) and cellular (find all matches first, apply after, so the rules must not conflict). Worked on the string AAAAAA with find AA, replace AB: normal gives one change, L-system gives ABABAB, cellular gives ABBBBB.
- A rule can carry its own sub-recipe that runs after it fires, which gives a call stack without general control flow.
- Limits: a rule always matches a fixed node count, so variable-size things need a seed rule, a grow rule and a cleanup rule (the circle below took about 40 rules); simple queries such as "farthest node from X" need several steps (label distances, find max, pick); there is no layout, so a graph with no positions cannot be placed on a map.

### How Unexplored chains the rules (C)
- Scale: about 5,000 rules in about 50 modules across two generators (whole-dungeon with 20 level requests, and per-level floorplan). Varied output comes largely from the sheer number of hand-written cases, not from a clever core.
- Pipeline for one floor: (1) a 5x5 grid of graph nodes that are never moved or deleted, only tagged, so the later map conversion is trivial and the graph keeps a 2D shape (this solves the "no layout" limit); (2) draw a start, a goal and one big rough circle through the grid; (3) pick one of 24 major cycle types and apply it to the two arcs between entrance and goal; (4) add minor cycles (short detours, often with more keys), lengthen cycles, add dead ends, repeat until the floor reaches target size; (5) resolve abstractions stepwise; (6) grid x2 (corridor cells, marked barrier or door) then x5 (tiles), rooms grown to rectangles, caves roughed by small cellular automata; (7) set pieces and items placed by pattern (a bookshelf looks for a wall spot that does not block a door), terrain noise, smoothing and vegetation.
- Cycles: the two arcs let a level be two alternative routes, a one-way valve that avoids backtracking, a goal locked behind three keys scattered around with a guardian (the ThreeLeverHub type), a hub, or a lake ring. Cycle types only fix the main flow; arc obstacle kinds and key positions are randomised inside them.
- Lock and key: an early abstract "Lock" node and "Key" node, plus a special edge from each key to its lock. Because the pair is linked, every middle rule can insert, shuffle or move either one and the pair stays consistent. Locks and keys stand for anything (item, switch, knowledge); hard (key required) and soft (key only helps) use the same structure. The same trick is used for "Obstacle" placeholders (enemy, puzzle or trap decided late, using the theme and the arc's role) and for hints and patrol assignments. The relations are kept in the output, so the game can check solvability and offer a "pray for help" fix.
- Themes ("biomes"): level-wide tags chosen first (fire, wood, caves, plus rare minor ones) that many independent later steps read to turn content on or off: enemy pool, terrain tile set, which hazards are banned.

### Gnomish Mines method (D)
- Grid of floor/rock; start with 40% of cells floor at random, then four cellular-automaton passes over the 8 neighbours, then connect stray caves with corridors. Pass 1 (done in place, cell by cell): 0-2 floor neighbours becomes rock, 3-4 unchanged, 5-8 becomes floor (a clustering pass). Pass 2 (buffered, whole grid at once): a cell with exactly 5 floor neighbours becomes rock, otherwise unchanged (the author could not explain its purpose). Passes 3 and 4 (buffered): 0-2 floor neighbours becomes rock, else unchanged (erosion that removes specks and corners). Most of NetHack is rectangular rooms; only the Mines use this, to feel organic.

### Takeaways for the Cellars generator
Context: `Core/Cellars/CellarFloorGenerator.cs` places hand-built rooms (shuffled, each linked to an earlier room), then 1-3 extra loop corridors and 1-3 dead ends, with a locked leaf room on 50% of floors.
1. **Phase 1 cheap: keep the lock and key as a linked pair and validate it.** The plan already stores `LockedRoom` and `KeyRoom`; add a validator that walks the final graph and proves the key room is reachable without passing the locked leaf, and expose the lock-to-key relation in the layout for debug overlays. This is Unexplored's consistency edge, scaled to one pair.
2. **Phase 1 cheap: make the loop a chosen shape, not just "1-3 extra corridors".** Unexplored's backbone is one entrance-to-goal loop with two arcs. Pick entry and exit on a ring of rooms first, then give each arc a role (for example arc A holds the key chest, arc B holds the approach to the locked room or a one-way shortcut door). Cheap form: a three-value cycle preset (plain two-route ring, key-on-one-arc, shortcut-back) chosen per floor, with the exit-distance bound from section 3.
3. **Phase 1 cheap: late-resolved placeholders.** Place "Obstacle" and "Reward" slots in the room graph first; resolve them to roster, trap or vignette after the layout and the floor sub-theme are fixed. One theme tag then steers rosters, tiles and hazards, and the sameness metrics in 3.1 can be measured at the abstract level.
4. **Later: a small rewriting pass over the room graph.** Not 5,000 rules; 5 to 10 (add a dead end, lengthen an arc, add a minor loop, move the key one room back, move the lock one room forward) run in iterate-N or L-system style, replacing the fixed "loops then dead ends" order. Worth it only once there is more than one lock or key type. For caves, the Mines recipe (40% fill, four passes, connect pockets, about 40 lines in Core) could give an organic sub-theme, but our room pool and prop placement assume rectangular rooms.
5. **Big: a full graph-rewriting macro generator with a cycle library.** Needs a rule engine, an editor and tests; Unexplored's author spent years on the tooling. Not for Phase 1 (matches item 12 in section 3).

## Addendum: Hades room and encounter variety (fandom wiki, via API)

Secondary source: community wiki text (CC BY-SA), read 2026-10-31 through `https://hades.fandom.com/api.php?action=parse&prop=wikitext`, not the game files. Pages read (all under `https://hades.fandom.com/wiki/<Title>`): Chambers_and_Encounters, Chamber_Reward, Chaos_Gate, Infernal_Trove, Infernal_Gate, Erebus, Tartarus, Asphodel, Elysium, Temple_of_Styx, Well_of_Charon, Shrine_of_Hermes. Caveats: the wiki mixes Hades 1 and Hades 2 text on some pages (Erebus and Shrine of Hermes are mostly Hades 2 and used only as context); some numbers are player-observed. The wiki gives no pool sizes, no per-room weights and no repeat rule, so those stay unknown (the game's Lua scripts are needed).

### Run shape and counts
- A full run touches at most 73 chambers. Boss chamber numbers (not counting Chaos rooms and troves): Tartarus 14, Asphodel 24, Elysium 36.
- Tartarus is 15 chambers by default (boss at 14, then a stairway) and can be stretched by taking a Chaos gate in chamber 12. Asphodel is always 10 chambers (rooms 16-25, the last is the stairway). Elysium is always 11 chambers per its page (the stairway is room 37). The Styx temple is built differently: a hub room plus five wings of small chambers, 33 chambers on a full clear, with a reward only in the last room of each wing.
- Tartarus has one boss chosen at random per run from three (a new player sees only the first until later runs unlock the others). Asphodel and Elysium have one fixed boss each, in its own arena.

### How chambers are drawn
- The wiki states no pool or repeat rules. What it does say: normal combat chambers come in layout variants (the Tartarus "Bloodless" mini-boss fight happens in one of two rooms: a small one with four breakable pillars, or a large ring room with traps that only appears when an optional difficulty setting is on). Mini-boss, boss and story rooms are fixed, hand-built arenas.
- Fixed structure around the random picks: the first Tartarus room is a combat room; each later biome opens with an enemy-free room that has no reward, only a passage on; a boss is always followed by a stairway room (healing fountain, then a shop well and a keepsake cabinet); the chamber before a boss offers Charon's shop or, as the alternative door, a free room holding that room's reward.
- Unique rooms are capped per run. Each biome has one story-character room (Tartarus, Asphodel, Elysium one each); each can appear once per run and not every run. Each biome's mini-boss list is "one of N, at most one per run, possibly none": Tartarus 3 choices, Asphodel 3, Elysium 2, Styx 4 (up to two per run). The door does not say which mini-boss it is, only the skull.
- Avoiding repeats: stated only for specials (story rooms once per run, the death-god helper room once per run in any non-unique chamber, spacing rules for side rooms below). Nothing on the pages says normal rooms avoid repeating.

### Encounter types
- Wave rooms are the default: kill all waves, then the reward appears and the exits unlock.
- Skull rooms: harder variants with doubled persistent rewards and more likely higher-rarity boons. This covers mini-boss rooms (reward fixed to a boon), the two-god "trial" room (choose one of two boons, the spurned god sends waves), and boss rooms (reward is a one-time bounty, then a currency drop of 100 / 150 / 250 by biome).
- Mini-boss shapes: two armoured elites; a single teleporting enemy that summons reinforcements if slow; a floating beam enemy; a barge crossing in three waves protected by shield-casting support; a big caster plus an elite bruiser; four elite casters around a magma moat; an elite summoner plus capped reinforcements that re-arm from weapons on the floor; a duel that ends at 20% health instead of death. Styx mini-bosses sit only in the last room of a harder wing, spawn reinforcements up to a cap, and end when everything is dead.
- Survival room (Tartarus only): occasionally a standard encounter is swapped for a 45-second hold-out against continuous spawns, after which every enemy is wiped; killing is optional. A difficulty setting turns it off.
- Perfect-clear room (Erebus, entered through an Infernal gate): waves in a unique room; with no damage taken the reward is an amplified version (double currency or heart, higher-rarity boon, level-2 upgrade), otherwise a token 1-heal item. Three exits return to the same biome.
- Trap rooms are an environment layer per biome, not an encounter type: floor spikes, dart cannons on pressure plates and shoot-to-detonate barrels in Tartarus; magma and smoke vents in Asphodel; regrowing explosive eggs, crossbows and spear statues in Elysium; guillotines, saw launchers and poison vents with cure pools in Styx. Enemies trigger them too.
- Non-combat rooms (rare): Charon's shop (always three random rewards), free room (reward lying in the middle), fountain room (take the reward, then drink to open the exits), story-character rooms, stairway rooms. They do not count as encounters for timed buffs and cannot spawn the side-room gates below.

### Rewards on doors and how they are chosen
- Every door shows its reward before entry, framed by a blue laurel (persistent currency) or a gold laurel (this run only: boon, heart, coin pile, hammer, power-up), with a skull under it for hard rooms; shops have a bag symbol. The player chooses the next room by choosing a door; usually two doors, sometimes one.
- The persistent-versus-run split is a feedback rule: chance of persistent = target + 10 x (target minus the current share of rooms that gave one). Targets: Tartarus 0.45, Asphodel 0.4, Elysium 0.33, none in Styx wings. With a factor of 10 the result is almost forced: under target it is nearly always persistent, over target nearly always run-type (the page lists one witnessed exception). The first room's reward is forced to a boon or hammer. Boss reward is a bounty or currency drop; mini-boss reward is a boon.
- A meta-upgrade reroll swaps the reward behind a door for another from the same pool; it cannot touch shops, story rooms or bosses, and a mini-boss room keeps its fight when only the boon changes.
- Styx wings, with no rerolls, always offer two or three boons, a hammer if only one was offered earlier, and a mix of coins, power-ups and hearts for the rest.

### Side rooms and add-ons (rarity and conditions)
- Gates and chests appear only inside a normal combat room, and open once its reward is collected.
- Chaos gate (a health-paid side room): only after 6 earlier runs; not in shops, boss rooms or the room after a boss; no two within 8 chambers; rarer in each later biome; none in Styx; a bought item can force one.
- Infernal trove (a timed chest whose loot decays while its guards live): not in a biome's first room, boss rooms or the room after a boss; never in two consecutive rooms; none in Styx; a bought item can force one; 5% extra nectar drop.
- Erebus gate (the perfect-clear room): unlocked through the difficulty system at 5, 10 or 15 total heat for Tartarus, Asphodel, Elysium; needs a one-time upgrade purchase; not in boss rooms, the room after a boss, or within 8 chambers of another; cannot be forced.
- Well of Charon (a small buff shop): not on the first run of a save, not in the first four Tartarus chambers, not within 2 chambers of another (3 in Tartarus); guaranteed once in every stairway; reduced rate in Styx. Wares are temporary buffs (3 to 15 encounters) at 10 to 75 coins, instant effects at 40 to 200, plus "ensure the next trove / chaos gate / fishing spot appears" items.
- Pool of Purging (removes a boon for a refund) shares the same eligibility as the other side rooms.
- The sequel's delivery shrine uses plain spacing (not within 2 chambers of another, not in boss or guardian rooms); noted only as a pattern.

### Takeaways for the Cellars
1. **Phase 1 cheap: show the reward on the door and let the exits differ.** Each exit displays its reward icon (loot piece, heal, weapon-growth item, shop) before you enter, with a marker for the harder variant. This turns a hand-authored room pool into a choice every room at almost no code cost; the visible information is the variety, not the geometry.
2. **Phase 1 cheap: a feedback rule for the reward mix instead of a flat roll.** Hades steers the persistent-versus-run split toward a target share with a strong correction. For us: keep a running count of town-piece rewards per dive and steer toward a target per floor, so a floor never gives all or none. A few lines of Core code plus a unit test.
3. **Phase 1 cheap: one special per floor, with a hard cap.** A mini-boss or story room as "one of N, at most one per run, may be none" (Tartarus does this with 3 mini-bosses), plus once-per-run flags for story rooms. With 4-6 rooms per floor, pick the floor's single special from a short list so two floors of one theme differ without new room art.
4. **later: optional detour rooms with spacing rules.** Trove or gate style side rooms that open after a cleared room, using the wiki's eligibility rules: not the first room, not a boss, not the room after a boss, not twice in a row, minimum gap. Needs a decision on the cost or risk they trade.
5. **big: per-theme trap kits and elite-plus-arena mini-bosses.** Hades gets its mini-boss variety by pairing an elite with a fixed arena and a biome trap kit. Doing that per Cellars theme means authoring enemy groups and trap sets per room; hold until the room pool exists. Still unknown: Hades' real repeat avoidance and room weights, which sit in unread game scripts.
