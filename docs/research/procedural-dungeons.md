# Procedural dungeons that feel different every visit but familiar

Research for the Undercroft Cellars floors (PHASE1 7.2). Written 2026-10-31 in our own words: mechanics and numbers only, no code copied. Worry being answered: players feel the same path every time even when the layout changes.

Reading method: Brogue CE and DevilutionX were cloned shallowly into a scratchpad directory outside the repo and read as text only (nothing built or run). Web pages were read through a fetch tool that returns a summary of the page, so numbers from web sources are as the summariser reported them. "Primary" below means read from source code or the developer's own page. "Secondary" means a third party's description.

## 1. Per source

### 1.1 Brogue Community Edition (primary, source code)
- URL: https://github.com/tmewett/BrogueCE (files `src/brogue/Architect.c`, `src/variants/GlobalsBrogue.c`). Licence: AGPL-3.0 (copyleft: we must never copy code from it; ideas and numbers only).
- Generator steps, in order:
  1. Carve the base map: a first room, then repeat "design a random room in empty space, slide it around the map in shuffled order until one of its door sites touches an existing wall, then attach". Room kinds: cave blob, cross, symmetrical cross, small box, circle, chunky (blobby box); a hallway can be prepended to a room. Room-kind frequencies and the corridor chance shift with depth (more caves and long corridors up top, more of one other kind deeper).
  2. Add loops: for every wall cell with floor on both sides, make a door if the walking distance between the two sides is above 20 steps. This is the "secondary connection" pass. Loops come from a rule on shortcut value, not a fixed count.
  3. Lakes (water, lava, chasm, brimstone): blob shapes tried large to small (30x15 shrinking by 2x1 to 20x10, minimum 4x4), accepted only if a flood fill proves the level stays connected (10 placement tries each). Lake wreaths (shallow edges) are added.
  4. Plain "autogenerators": a table row says terrain or decoration, which depth range, a percent chance, a count formula (intercept plus slope times depth, capped). Examples: granite columns (depth 1+, 60%, max 4), grass thinning out by depth 10, dead grass depth 4-14, bones from 12, torches in walls from 6 (5%, max 12), statues, luminescent fungus from 7, pre-revealed traps at 20% each in a depth band, hidden traps later. Sunlight patches (0-5) and darkness patches (1-15) are autogenerators too, which is a ready-made per-floor light variant.
  5. Machines (the interesting part, below).
  6. Bridges across chasms and lakes, only where they shorten a long detour.
  7. Doors: about 60% of door sites become real doors; some become secret doors with depth.
- Machines ("blueprints"): a table of about 80 hand-written recipes. A recipe gives a depth range, a room size range, a frequency weight, flags and a list of features (each feature: how many instances, where in the room, what item or monster, constraints such as "far from the door" or "in the walls"). Four families:
  - Reward rooms (item library choose-one-of-many 30 weight at depth 1-12, single-category library 15, pedestal with a guaranteed good item 5-16, consumable pedestals 10+, commutation altars and resurrection altar 13+, allies in cages or chains 5+, legendary ally 8+, goblin warren 5-15 and sentinel sanctuary 10-23 as big set-pieces). Frequency weights run from 5 to 50, depth gates run from 1 to 13.
  - Vestibules (the "door" to a reward room): locked door with the key held by a key-holder machine (weight 100, the default), secret door (weight 1), lever + portcullis (8, from depth 4), flammable barricade (10, depth 1-6), statue to shatter (6), statue bursting into a monster (6, depth 5+), pit-trap field (8), mirror totem (8, depth 5+), guardian-on-glyph (8, depth 6+), throwing tutorial with a pressure plate (8, depth 1-4 only).
  - Key holders (where the key to a locked vestibule lives): about 30 puzzle rooms, each weighted 1-35 and depth gated: collapsing floor, rat trap, flood room, fire-trap room, levitation over a pit, lava moat, poison gas, burning grass, guardian water puzzle, guardian gauntlet and corridor, sacrifice altar, electric crystals, zombie crypt (12+), haunted house (16+), boss with the key (18), plus a "nested item library" (weight 35) where one of the choices is the key to another vault. A key holder is also a plain secret room (weight 1).
  - Flavour machines (no reward, pure scenery): bloodwort plant, abandoned shrine, idyll with ponds, swamp, camp, remnant, dismal (blood and bones), chasm catwalk, lake walk, paralysis-trap area, statue that comes alive, worms in walls, sentinels. Each has its own chance row in the autogenerator table (for example shrine 7% from depth 5, bloodwort 25%, idyll 15% depth 1-5, remnant 15% from depth 10), capped at 1-5 per level.
- Rarity budget for reward machines: a level is owed a reward room when the count generated so far, scaled, falls behind the depth reached (the shipped constants amount to roughly one per three to four levels, with the multiplier 4, offset 2 and factor 1). On top of that comes a random extra: 40% on the first two levels when none is owed, otherwise 15% per extra, repeatedly. That is a pity timer plus random bonus: a long dry spell raises the count owed, so rewards never go missing for long, but the exact floors vary.
- Item-key link: the reward room "outsources" its key to a second machine, and key-holder machines can themselves be vaults needing a key, so chains of 2-3 steps appear.
- What makes it feel varied: nearly everything is data rows with a depth window and a weight, so the combination on any level is different, and each machine is a mini-puzzle with its own rules (so two visits differ in what you must do, not only in what the walls look like). What keeps it familiar: the same recipes recur and are learnable; the vault's door is always a recognisable "kind of obstacle".
- Fixed vs procedural: depth-1 start room is fixed in kind (the entrance room); the amulet floor and its holder machine are forced. Everything else is procedural.

### 1.2 Spelunky (secondary for Spelunky 1/2 behaviour, primary developer blog for the 1 generator)
- URLs: https://tinysubversions.com/spelunkyGen/ (part 1, solution path) and https://tinysubversions.com/spelunkyGen2/ (part 2, rooms), by Darius Kazemi; the underlying design is Derek Yu's. No stated licence (a personal blog, text is copyrighted; we only record numbers).
- Level = 4x4 grid of rooms, each room 10x8 tiles (80 tiles). Steps:
  1. A start room in the top row; walk to the exit with a 5-way roll (two parts left, two parts right, one part down); a drop forces the room to have a bottom opening and the room below to have a top opening. The path passes through 4 room types: 0 side room (no guaranteed exits), 1 left-right, 2 left-right-down (+up if stacked), 3 left-right-up. Reaching the bottom row's end places the exit.
  2. Every cell off the path gets a type-0 filler room (may be walled off), and a vertical stack of 3-4 filler rooms can become a snake pit.
  3. Each room picks one template at random from a set belonging to its type and position; each type has about 8-16 templates.
  4. Inside a template: fixed tiles, probabilistic tiles (a spike 33%, a pushblock 25%, a coin-flip block 50%) and "obstacle blocks": marker tiles that are replaced by a random 5x3 hand-built structure (one marker for ground, one for air).
  5. Rooms are randomly mirrored left-right.
  6. Entities (treasure, enemies, traps) are placed in a later pass with their own odds.
- Spelunky 2 keeps the same "guaranteed safe path out of templates" idea (developer interview, secondary); its level files, special rooms and rarity tables could not be read (see section 4).
- What makes it varied: three small random layers stacked in each cell (template choice, obstacle blocks, probabilistic tiles) multiply to a huge number of surface looks from a few hundred authored pieces. What keeps it familiar: the room grammar (always a 4x4, always a left-to-right snake) and the small template set; the developer himself says the result does not look like natural caves and players learn the repeats.
- Fixed vs procedural: nothing fixed in a normal level; fixed layouts only for special levels.

### 1.3 DevilutionX, Diablo 1 level generators (primary, source code)
- URL: https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX (`Source/levels/drlg_l1..l4.cpp`, `themes.cpp`, `drlg_quests.cpp`). Licence: the repository states the "Sustainable Use License 1.0" (source-available, restricts commercial use; read-only reference for us, nothing copied). It is a community engine rebuilt around the original game's data; we own no game files.
- Cathedral (level type 1): a fixed template of a 40x40 mega-tile area: up to three 10x10 chambers (each present with 50% chance, but at least the middle one if an end one is missing) joined by a hallway 6 wide, with a random choice of horizontal or vertical orientation. Then random walls are added to split the space and the layout is regenerated until a minimum area is met (about 533 mega-tiles on level 1, 693 on level 2, 761 beyond). Stairs placed last, retry the whole level if they do not fit.
- Other level types (catacombs, caves, hell) use different recursive-division, drunk-fill and room-pair routines; not read in depth.
- Set-pieces: "quest" maps (hand-authored small tile blocks, for example the butcher's room, a skeleton king's chamber, a banner room) are stamped into a chosen chamber of the generated level after generation, and the rest of the level routes around it. The chosen quest depends on which quests are active. The chamber chosen is random, so the set-piece sits in a different place each visit but its inside is identical.
- Theme rooms: after layout, enclosed rooms of the right size are scanned; each becomes a themed room (shrine, skeleton room, library, treasure, fountains of four kinds, cauldron, torture, decapitated, armor stand, weapon rack, burning cross, goat shrine, monster pit, barrel room). Selection: a "good" subset of four themes (goat shrine, shrine, skeleton room, library) is rolled first; if it does not fit that room, any of 17 themes are tried until one fits. Some themes (fountains, cauldron, armor, weapon, treasure) are once-per-level flags, so a level can never show two of them. Inside a theme, each object spot fires on a per-theme probability, so two uses of the same theme look similar but not identical.
- What makes it varied: large reshuffle of the chamber layout plus themed rooms plus the quest set-piece; what keeps it familiar: the same four or five chamber footprints.
- Fixed vs procedural: town and quest-only levels are fixed; dungeon levels are generated except the stamped set-piece.

### 1.4 Pokemon Mystery Dungeon, Explorers of Sky floor generation (secondary)
- URLs: https://eosrs.pmdcollab.org/armv5te-none-ndseoseabi-na/doc/eos_rs/api/dungeon_mode/dungeon_generator/game_builtin/struct.DungeonGridMutator.html (a Rust reimplementation's docs), https://wiki.pmdo.pmdcollab.org/GridFloorGen (a different fan game, so only supports the general idea). Licence of those docs not checked; treated as reference only. Not read from decompiled primary material (see section 4).
- Steps (from the docs): a grid of up to 15x15 cells; 2 to 36 of the cells become rooms (rectangle inside the cell with a margin); the remaining cells get a single hallway anchor; connections are made by a momentum random walk; each connection is a hallway, or about 9.75% of the time two connected rooms merge into one large room; unreachable cells are joined or dropped; 40% of eligible rooms get ragged inward wall bumps; flagged plain rooms roll a die for a secondary structure (maze, checkerboard, central pool, island with moat, dividing line); then special rooms by per-floor percentage: kecleon shop (valid room at least 5x4), monster house (not merged, not a maze), maze room.
- Fixed floors exist (boss floors, story floors) by a separate "fixed room" path; not read.
- What makes it varied: the room count per floor ranges widely and merged rooms and secondary structures change a room's shape; the monster house is a per-floor percentage ambush that players cannot predict, which is the strongest "this visit is different" signal in the game. Familiar: the room-grid skeleton.

### 1.6 Developer write-ups
- **Dead Cells** (https://deepnight.net/tutorial/the-level-design-of-dead-cells-a-hybrid-approach/, republished from the lead designer's post; primary, no licence). A hand-made world map is fixed. Each biome has a concept graph that sets level length, number of special rooms, how labyrinthine it is and the distance from entrance to the nearest exit; the generator then picks, for each node, a random hand-built room of the right biome and entrance/exit shape until one fits. Monster count scales with the total length of combat rooms (their example: 250 combat tiles at one per five tiles gives 50), each monster kind has its own spacing and weight rules. They say pure procedural placement made level design weaker, so they set constraints first.
- **Enter the Gungeon** (https://www.boristhebrave.com/2019/07/28/dungeon-generation-in-enter-the-gungeon, Boris the Brave, analysis from the game's files; secondary). Each stage has a set of hand-authored flow graphs (4 in the first stage, 8 in the next), each built around one signature feature such as a big loop or a mandatory shop. A flow is transformed: some rooms become random-length chains on later stages, two spots choose between alternate paths. Special nodes (vendor, jail, fireplace) are injected with a spawn probability and prerequisites; secret rooms usually sit at dead ends but have a 1-in-5 chance to attach to any room, 90% of appearing. Each node picks a concrete room from a pool (nearly 300 for the first stage) and avoids repeats. Loops are laid out as separate units; the closing link is a small room or a corridor 4-30 units long.
- **Unexplored** (https://www.boristhebrave.com/2021/04/10/dungeon-generation-in-unexplored/, Boris the Brave, on Joris Dormans' design; secondary). Each level starts as a 5x5 node grid with a start, goal and a big loop between them. One of 24 hand-written "major cycle" types decides pacing (alternate routes, one-way doors, hubs, a free-roaming lake). Minor cycles, dead ends and longer cycles are added after. Locks and keys are placeholders that keep a special edge, so the pair stays valid while the graph is rewritten (a key can be an item, a switch or a piece of knowledge; soft locks give a shortcut rather than a block). Rooms are then typed (library, forge, prison), each type with its own rules for decorations, items and set-pieces; themes (fire, wood) steer many independent systems at once. Numbers: 20 levels per game, about 5,000 rewrite rules, about 50 rule modules in the floorplan generator. His own takeaway, quoting Dormans: small levels are more interesting to generate than large ones.
- **DunGen** (https://dungen-docs.aegongames.com/2.18/, official documentation of a commercial Unity asset used by Lethal Company; the docs were read through summaries; proprietary, mechanics only). A "dungeon flow" lists the main-path stages; each tile has separate main-path and branch weights with a depth curve; branch counts are set per tile or for the whole dungeon; branches may only end in tagged tiles (end cap, shop, secret room); tile-connection rules ban bad pairs (two corridors). Props: a *local prop set* keeps a random min-max number of a tile's pre-placed child props and disables the rest, count either random or mapped to path depth; a *random prefab* spawns one weighted prefab at a marker; *global props* give a category a dungeon-wide min/max (extra ones are culled by priority; it never adds missing ones). Unused doorways get blockers.
- **Spelunky/others on "samey"**: Yu's own remark (via a secondary video) that the algorithm yields recognisable repeating patterns; no other anti-sameness GDC text could be found (section 4).

## 2. Synthesis: the layers of variety

Ranked by perceived variety per unit of authoring cost (best first), with where each source does it.

1. **Rare vignettes and set-pieces with a rarity budget** (Brogue machines and flavour rows, Diablo quest maps and theme rooms, PMD monster house, Gungeon special rooms). One authored scene, appearing 1 floor in 4-10, is remembered much more than any layout change. Cost: one room each. Best return, as long as each is visibly different at first glance.
2. **Optional dressing with a fill probability** (Brogue autogenerators with count formulas, Spelunky probabilistic tiles, DunGen local prop sets). Costs nothing per item. Gives "a desk in a new spot" if placement also jitters.
3. **Encounter and event variety** (PMD monster house, Dead Cells monster budget by room length, Hades reward choice, Brogue trap rows). The player experiences fights and surprises more than walls; cheap because it reuses existing enemies.
4. **Per-visit lighting and atmosphere variants** (Brogue sunlight and darkness patches, Unexplored themes). One shared knob (fog colour, lantern count, flicker, dust) changes the mood of every room at once; cheap in code, large in feel.
5. **In-room chunks and obstacle blocks** (Spelunky 5x3 blocks, Dead Cells fixed rooms with variants). Several swappable regions inside one room multiply that room's looks (3 regions x 3 options = 27 rooms from one authoring pass). Medium cost.
6. **Macro graph variants and signature features** (Gungeon flows with one distinctive feature, Unexplored cycle types, Brogue lakes). A floor with a big loop plays differently from one with a long spine. Moderate cost, big effect on route memory.
7. **Room-template pool size** (Gungeon about 300 for one stage, Spelunky 8-16 per type, our 21). Linear in cost; players stop noticing past a point because rooms are met one at a time. Useful for avoiding repeats on a single floor.
8. **Fixed anchors** (entry, exit, checkpoint, boss floor, Dead Cells world map, Diablo's town). Not variety themselves; they give the "familiar". Keep them few and make everything around them differ.

Why a player feels "same dungeon path": usual causes seen across sources are (a) the same start-to-exit distance and direction, (b) the same room-kind sequence (always a corridor, then a combat room), (c) rooms that look the same once you ignore walls, and (d) no unpredictable event. Brogue, Spelunky and PMD all attack (d) and (c) before (a).

## 3. Recommendations mapped onto our system

Our system today: `CellarRoom` data grids with doors, dressing sockets (2-3 variants each), spawns, chests; `CellarRoomPool` 21 rooms (12 combat, 3 sub-themes); `CellarFloorGenerator` 14x14 grid of 4 m cells, 4-6 rooms, link to a random earlier room, 1-3 loops, 1-3 dead ends, corridor 4-bit masks, 50% locked leaf room with a key chest. Rooms are already rotated in four ways.

Labels: **Phase 1 cheap**, **later**, **big**.

1. **Optional sockets with a fill chance (Phase 1 cheap).** Add `Chance` (0-100) to `SocketSpec`/`DressingSocket`, rolled with the floor RNG; 100 stays the default so nothing existing changes. Author about 1/3 of each room's sockets at 50-70%. Also allow an "empty" variant counted as one of the 2-3 choices. Validator in `CellarRoom` keeps the 2-3 variant rule for the non-empty list.
2. **Socket placement jitter (Phase 1 cheap).** Each socket may list 2-3 alternative cells along the same wall (a "slot group"), picks one per floor. This is the "desk in a new spot" feature. Keep the existing blocked-cell checks so doors and chests stay reachable.
3. **Per-visit atmosphere variant (Phase 1 cheap).** One roll per floor from a small table: lantern density (fewer or more `Lantern` sockets lit), fog tint and density, dust particles, one off-colour light patch (Brogue's sunlight and darkness rows). Pure presentation, derived from the floor seed so clients agree. Add a "stir meter"-style lantern-out variant later if wanted.
4. **Vignette/set-piece system with a rarity budget and pity timer (Phase 1 cheap for 4-6 vignettes, later for more).** New `RoomFeature`-style table in Core: each entry has depth window (floor), weight, a room-kind it can live in, max per floor 1, and an authored prop group. Budget: expected 1.2 vignettes per floor, each floor rolls 0-2; a hidden `owed` counter (Brogue style) rises by 1 each floor with none and forces one when it reaches 3, resets when one spawns. Keep the pity counter in the world save so it does not reset between dives (needs a save-section note under `sc-netcode`). Start with: a lone desk with a lit candle and note, a smashed wine rack with a hidden chest, a rat nest (event), a sealed pantry needing a lever, a pile of old coats with a small bone figure. Stamp them into chosen rooms by replacing an in-room chunk region (see 5).
5. **Swappable chunk regions inside `CellarRoom` grids (later, but cheap when done once).** Let a room declare 1-2 rectangles (for example 2x2 cells) with 2-3 pre-authored alternative sub-grids (fill of crates vs open vs a low wall with a gap). Rotation already exists so the regions rotate with the room. This is the Spelunky obstacle-block idea and the vehicle for set-pieces in item 4. Needs `Parse` support, validation (doors stay connected) and builder changes.
6. **Pool size targets per sub-theme (later).** Reach 8-10 combat rooms per sub-theme (24-30 total, not 12) plus 3 vignette-capable rooms each, so two floors of the same theme overlap in at most 1 room. The existing 7.2 rule (no repeat on one floor, at most 2 shared between consecutive floors) holds with room to spare. Prefer more rooms with chunk regions over more fully distinct rooms.
7. **Signature macro features (later).** After the layout step, tag the floor with one of 3-4 "signature" rules, like Gungeon flows: a long spine (start and exit forced to opposite corners), a hub (one room with 3+ doors in the middle), a ring (the loop count forced to 2 and the first loop long), a deep dead-end wing. Today the generator picks links from random earlier rooms, so many floors look like blobs of similar diameter; forcing the exit far from the entry (a minimum walking distance) is the cheapest first step (**Phase 1 cheap**: reject layouts where entry to exit is shorter than a bound, and vary which corner by seed).
8. **Cyclic lock-and-key option (later).** Instead of "locked leaf room with its key chest elsewhere", add a second form: a one-way shortcut door opened from the far side (a soft lock), or a lever that opens a gate on a loop. Reuses the existing loop pass; store the lock-to-key relation on the layout so the validator can prove solvability (the Unexplored lesson). Keep the current 50% locked leaf room.
9. **Encounter variety (Phase 1 cheap to later).** One roll per floor for a "twist": an ambush room (monster house style: a quiet room whose chest triggers a second wave, 1 in 4 floors), a thin floor (fewer fights, more props), a crowded floor (spawn budget scaled by combat-room length, Dead Cells style). Hooks into the stir meter (PHASE1 7.2) so it composes with scaling.
10. **Doorway variants as vestibules (later).** Brogue's locked-door vestibules show that the same locked room can be hidden behind different obstacles (a fallen shelf to push, a cobweb to burn, a lever). Cheap content, high recognition, but needs interaction props.
11. **Fixed floor-4 set-piece (keep).** Stay fixed; vary only entry-side dressing and atmosphere on each dive (items 1-3) so the fixed room still feels familiar but not frozen. The Diablo method of stamping a hand-authored block into a generated map is the model if we ever want a fixed chamber inside floors 1-3.
12. **Big: macro grammar generator (big).** A Dormans-style graph rewriter (cycle types, lock and key placeholders) replacing the grid-attach step. Large payoff for route variety, but a full rewrite of `CellarFloorGenerator`; not for Phase 1.

### 3.1 Measuring sameness beyond the 7.2 metric
The current EditMode metric (no repeat on a floor, at most 2 shared rooms between consecutive floors, at least 3 distinct silhouettes over 1,000 seeds) only counts rooms and outlines. Add, per seed batch:
- **Route-shape fingerprint:** the shortest-path length entry to exit, the compass direction of the exit from the entry (8 bins), the number of doors on the path, and the room-kind sequence along that path. Report the spread (standard deviation and the share of the most common bin). Goal: no single exit-direction bin above about 30%, and the median path length varying by at least 2x across seeds.
- **First-30-seconds fingerprint:** what the player sees in the first two rooms (room ids, rotation, socket variants). Count unique pairs over 1,000 seeds; low numbers mean "the first thing I see is always the same".
- **Socket entropy:** per room, the number of distinct dressing outcomes across seeds; flag any room with fewer than 8.
- **Event rate:** over 1,000 seeds x 3 floors, the share of floors with at least one vignette and the longest run of floors without one (should obey the pity cap).
- **Recognition test (human):** the M7 playthrough with a one-line question after each floor: "have you seen this room's arrangement before?" Track the count; it is the only metric that tests the actual complaint.

Sorted by value for effort: items 1, 2, 3, 7a (distance bound) and the route-shape fingerprint are all Phase 1 cheap and attack the stated worry directly. Items 4 and 5 are the next step.
