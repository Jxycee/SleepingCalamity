(SCENE: Hollowmere, the market square. The Tithe Feast, late afternoon, PY 50.)

NARRATOR: The best thing about the Tithe Feast, as far as Tobin Marsh could tell, was that nobody watched the children. The second best thing was the smell.

NARRATOR: It came off the long tables in waves: pork done in cider, baked apples, the dark loaves Hollis Rye only made once a year. Banners hung from every upper window round the square, wheat-gold and berry-red, and the lanterns were strung on lines between them, not lit yet. Nobody touched a crumb until Amos Gill rang the bell and the lanterns went up and the elders said the words.

NARRATOR: Tobin was ten, and he had been hungry for about a year, which his father said was what being ten was.

NELL: You're standing on my foot,

NARRATOR: said Nell.

NARRATOR: He moved his foot. She was five, and she had held his sleeve all day, through the whole pageant, while the Sedge boy in a wool beard played Elder Vane carrying the first tithe down into the dark to see it settled. Everyone clapped when the paper Vane didn't come back. Nell clapped hardest. She liked it when people were brave.

TOBIN: Let go,

NARRATOR: Tobin said.

NELL: No.

TOBIN: I've got to see Da.

NELL: I'll come.

NARRATOR: Hob Marsh was by the tithe ramp, where the carts stood loaded for the weighing and the four great tithe oxen dozed in their traces. Abner Stroud, the tithe-reeve, was checking the ropes while his twin boys kicked their heels on the lead ox's back. Behind them the stone ramp went down under the bell tower into the old mine-mouth. Warm air came out of it. It always had.

NARRATOR: His father was holding the axe.

NARRATOR: Not the little splitting axe Tobin was allowed to use on kindling. The big one, the timber-yard axe with the ash haft worn pale where Hob's hands went and the head ground so bright it took the sky. Tobin's mother used to say it was the only thing Hob Marsh kept clean.

HOB: There he is,

NARRATOR: Hob said, looking at him as if measuring him for a joist.

HOB: Heard you're going under the town tonight. With Odile's girl and the rest.

NARRATOR: Tobin's face went hot.

TOBIN: Who told?

HOB: Nobody told. I've got ears.

NARRATOR: Hob turned the axe round and held it out haft first.

HOB: Here. For the roots down there.

NARRATOR: Tobin stared at it.

HOB: Take it, then. It won't bite you. Well. It will if you're stupid. Don't be stupid.

NARRATOR: He took it. The head dropped and clanged on the cobbles. He got it up onto his shoulder the way his father carried it, and his knees told him what they thought of that.

NARRATOR: Hob crouched. It put his face level with Tobin's, which almost never happened. His breath smelled of the first cider, but his eyes didn't.

HOB: And take your sister,

NARRATOR: he said.

TOBIN: She's slow.

HOB: Take your sister.

TOBIN: She'll cry in the dark. She cries at the cellar.

NARRATOR: Hob put his hand on the back of Tobin's neck. It was a big hand, dry and split at the knuckles. He held on a moment longer than he usually did.

HOB: Tobin,

NARRATOR: he said.

HOB: Take her.

NARRATOR: Then he stood up and went back toward the trestles without saying anything else, and Tobin was left with an axe and a sister and the feeling that something had been said that he hadn't heard.

NARRATOR: Pip Ostrander found them a minute later, which meant she had been watching. She was seven, half his size, with a bag on her back that clanked.

PIP: Is that your da's? Gods' teeth. Ash is gone ahead. We go now, while they're doing the speeches.

NARRATOR: She looked at Nell, then at Tobin, and said what Tobin was thinking.

PIP: She can't keep up. We're going to be running.

TOBIN: Da says I've got to bring her.

NARRATOR: Nell held his sleeve tighter.

NELL: I can run,

NARRATOR: she said.

NELL: I'm good at running.

NARRATOR: She wasn't. She stopped to look at things, and in the dark she would grab him and he'd carry her and the axe both, and Pip would laugh, and Wren would make a plan about it. He could see himself, as he always was, holding the thing nobody else wanted to hold.

TOBIN: Go and sit with Da,

NARRATOR: he said.

NELL: No.

TOBIN: Nell. Go and sit with Da. I'll bring you something back. A treasure. From the tunnels.

NARRATOR: Her lip went. He knew that lip. He had seen it every day of the three years since their mother died, and he had learned that if you waited, it went away, and if you were kind to it, it got worse.

NELL: I want to come.

NARRATOR: he said, and turned, and went after Pip.

Somewhere behind the trestles Marta Fenn was ladling something into a tureen, and he thought she might be looking at him, but when he glanced back she was only looking at the soup. Nell was standing by the end of the long table in her good blue dress, crying with her fists at her sides, and then a crowd of Sedges went between them and he couldn't see her any more.
