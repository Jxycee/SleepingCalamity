# Chapter 1: Before the Meal

*Status: draft, for user annotation.*

### 1.1

*Hollowmere, the market square. The Tithe Feast, late afternoon, PY 50.*

The best thing about the Tithe Feast, as far as Tobin Marsh could tell, was that nobody watched the children. The second best thing was the smell.

It came off the long tables in waves: pork done in cider, baked apples, the dark loaves Hollis Rye only made once a year. Banners hung from every upper window round the square, wheat-gold and berry-red, and the lanterns were strung on lines between them, not lit yet. Nobody touched a crumb until Amos Gill rang the bell and the lanterns went up and the elders said the words.

Tobin was ten, and he had been hungry for about a year, which his father said was what being ten was.

"You're standing on my foot," said Nell.

He moved his foot. She was five, and she had held his sleeve all day, through the whole pageant, while the Sedge boy in a wool beard played Elder Vane carrying the first tithe down into the dark "to see it settled". Everyone clapped when the paper Vane didn't come back. Nell clapped hardest. She liked it when people were brave.

"Let go," Tobin said.

"No."

"I've got to see Da."

"I'll come."

Hob Marsh was by the tithe ramp, where the carts stood loaded for the weighing and the four great tithe oxen dozed in their traces. Abner Stroud, the tithe-reeve, was checking the ropes while his twin boys kicked their heels on the lead ox's back. Behind them the stone ramp went down under the bell tower into the old mine-mouth. Warm air came out of it. It always had.

His father was holding the axe.

Not the little splitting axe Tobin was allowed to use on kindling. The big one, the timber-yard axe with the ash haft worn pale where Hob's hands went and the head ground so bright it took the sky. Tobin's mother used to say it was the only thing Hob Marsh kept clean.

"There he is," Hob said, looking at him as if measuring him for a joist. "Heard you're going under the town tonight. With Odile's girl and the rest."

Tobin's face went hot. "Who told?"

"Nobody told. I've got ears." Hob turned the axe round and held it out haft first. "Here. For the roots down there."

Tobin stared at it.

"Take it, then. It won't bite you. Well. It will if you're stupid. Don't be stupid."

He took it. The head dropped and clanged on the cobbles. He got it up onto his shoulder the way his father carried it, and his knees told him what they thought of that.

Hob crouched. It put his face level with Tobin's, which almost never happened. His breath smelled of the first cider, but his eyes didn't. "And take your sister," he said.

"She's slow."

"Take your sister."

"She'll cry in the dark. She cries at the cellar."

Hob put his hand on the back of Tobin's neck. It was a big hand, dry and split at the knuckles. He held on a moment longer than he usually did. "Tobin," he said. "Take her."

Then he stood up and went back toward the trestles without saying anything else, and Tobin was left with an axe and a sister and the feeling that something had been said that he hadn't heard.

Pip Ostrander found them a minute later, which meant she had been watching. She was seven, half his size, with a bag on her back that clanked. "Is that your da's? Gods' teeth. Ash is gone ahead. We go *now*, while they're doing the speeches." She looked at Nell, then at Tobin, and said what Tobin was thinking. "She can't keep up. We're going to be running."

"Da says I've got to bring her."

Nell held his sleeve tighter. "I can run," she said. "I'm good at running."

She wasn't. She stopped to look at things, and in the dark she would grab him and he'd carry her and the axe both, and Pip would laugh, and Wren would make a plan about it. He could see himself, as he always was, holding the thing nobody else wanted to hold.

"Go and sit with Da," he said.

"No."

"Nell. Go and sit with Da. I'll bring you something back. A treasure. From the tunnels."

Her lip went. He knew that lip. He had seen it every day of the three years since their mother died, and he had learned that if you waited, it went away, and if you were kind to it, it got worse.

"I want to come."

He pulled his sleeve out of her hand. It was easier than he'd thought. She was only five, and he was ten, and he was holding an axe.

"Go on," he said, and turned, and went after Pip.

He heard her start crying behind him, the real kind, with no breath in it. He didn't look round. Somewhere behind the trestles Marta Fenn was ladling something into a tureen, and he thought she might be looking at him, but when he glanced back she was only looking at the soup. Nell was standing by the end of the long table in her good blue dress, crying with her fists at her sides, and then a crowd of Sedges went between them and he couldn't see her any more.

Good, he thought. That's done.

He would think about that word for the rest of his life.

### 1.2

*The feast gate, then the south orchard. Dusk.*

The trick with Gran was not to look at her.

Odile Ostrander had been a sergeant of the town watch for twenty-six years, and if you looked at her, she looked back, and then you told her. So Pip walked toward the feast gate with her eyes on the cobbles and Tobin clanking behind her like a tinker's cart, and she did not look.

Gran was on the gate with Constable Pell, counting heads in. Adults only: every grown-up was touched on the shoulder and counted, tap, tap, tap. Children ran in and out all day like hens.

Children going *out*, though. At dusk. With an axe.

Pip was ten steps from the gate when Gran turned round, quite suddenly, and bellowed at the miller's boy for climbing the fountain. Her whole broad back was to the gate. Constable Pell turned to look too, because everybody turned when Gran bellowed.

"Go," Pip hissed, and they went.

Luck, she thought, running. Pure luck. Or maybe the gods liked her. She'd suspected for a while that they did.

Her bag bounced on her back. Inside it was Gran's old watch dagger, the real one, and strapped flat to the outside was Gran's buckler, with the dent from the time she stopped a drover's cudgel in the market. Gran had packed it herself that morning while Pip watched. Pip had nearly asked why. You didn't ask questions when grown-ups were giving you swords.

"For the spooky things," Gran had said, buckling the strap. Then she'd stopped and looked at Pip for a long time, the way she looked at a stranger at the gate. Then she'd said, "Mind you bring that back clean," and gone out.

A week ago, shelling beans on the step, Gran had told her about the tunnels, flat as a watch report: how the founders dug for silver under the hill, went mad in the dark and walled one of their own up alive, and how the door was still there in the south orchard behind the pickers' hut, and on still nights you could hear the walled-up man knocking. Pip had not slept for two nights. On the third she decided that the cure for being frightened of a thing was to go and look at it, with an audience.

Behind the fallen-in pickers' hut there was a low doorway in the bank, three stones and a lintel, with an iron gate across it that someone had left open.

Ash Calder was sitting on the lintel with a bow across his knees.

"You're late," he said.

"Where did you get *that*?"

It was Jory's yew bow with the green binding, and there was a hunting sword on Ash's hip, which was so far past allowed that Pip felt dizzy. "Jory gave it me," Ash said. "Walked me up here and all. He's been in. He says it's half a mile and then a wall, and the walled-up man is a lie to stop people going in." He swung the gate. It went without a sound. "That's not a ghost. That's lard."

Wren Hale came up the orchard path then, walking carefully, carrying a staff taller than she was. It was her mother's surveying staff, the long one marked in black and white bands, with a brass foot and an iron hook at the top, and on the hook hung a lantern already lit, though it wasn't dark yet. She was eight and she walked like a magistrate.

"My mother gave me this," she said, before anyone could ask. "I'm to hold it for her. She filled the lantern." She looked at Tobin's axe, at Pip's shield, at Ash's sword. "Is everybody's family mad today?"

Nobody had an answer. It was the feast, Pip thought. Grown-ups went strange at the feast. Gran said it was the cider, though Gran didn't drink.

"Where's Nell?" Wren said.

"With Da," Tobin said, and walked into the tunnel first.

It was, in the end, a very boring tunnel.

It was low and dry and smelled of earth. There were pick marks, and initials cut into the rock, and some drawings that made Tobin go red and Wren hold the lantern somewhere else. There was no silver and no walled-up man. Pip knocked on the walls every twenty paces anyway, and nothing knocked back.

"It's a hole," Ash said, after the first hour. "It's a long hole."

"It's the founders' hole."

"The founders dug a long hole and then they got bored."

"Maybe the scary bit is further," Pip said, and kept going, because if she stopped she would have to admit he was right, and then they would go back to the square, and Gran would know.

### 1.3

*The founders' tunnels, the far end. Night.*

Ash felt the bell before he heard it.

It came up through his boots and into his teeth, a long low hum, as if the rock had been struck somewhere a long way off. Then the sound arrived behind it, thin and far, the great bell of Hollowmere tolling over their heads through half a mile of hill.

"That's the end," Wren said. "That's Amos ringing them out. The feast's over."

"We missed the pie," said Pip.

"We missed the speeches," Tobin said. He had been very quiet for a long time. The axe was on his shoulder and he had both hands on the haft, and Ash could see his knuckles. "We'll be in for it."

"We'll be in for it with *pie*," said Pip, "if we go back now."

But nobody moved. They were at the end of the tunnel, which was exactly as Jory had said: a flat wall of dark rock with tool marks on it, and nothing else. Ash put his hand on it. It was warm. Not warm like a stone in the sun. Warm like a dog's side.

He thought of Jory at the pickers' hut that evening, strange all day, laughing at nothing, holding out the bow and the sword. "Here. Since you're always stealing it." He'd ruffled Ash's hair, which he hadn't done since Ash was six, and gone back down the hill without looking round. Ash had stood there grinning like an idiot. He would remember the grin.

The bell tolled again.

It was right in his teeth now, and his teeth hurt with it. He looked at the others. Pip was rubbing her jaw. Wren had her eyes shut. Tobin was looking back the way they'd come, back toward the town, with a face Ash didn't understand.

The bell tolled a third time, and in the middle of the stroke it stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a voice stops when a hand goes over the mouth.

And the lanterns went out.

All at once, or near enough that none of them could ever agree. Tobin's, hanging off the axe head, died to a red worm of wick. Ash's little tin lamp went black in his hand as if somebody had pinched it. There was no wind. There was no sound at all.

Wren's lantern stayed lit.

It hung on its iron hook at the top of her mother's staff and burned with a small steady yellow flame, and it lit four faces in the dark and nothing else. Wren was holding the staff in both hands like a person holding a rope over a drop.

Then the air moved.

It came out of the wall at the end of the tunnel and went past them toward the town: a long warm breath that smelled of bread and hot iron and, under that, the butcher's yard on killing day. It lifted Ash's hair. It went on far longer than anything should be able to breathe.

When it stopped, the rock was cold under his hand.

Nobody said anything. Ash found that he had drawn the hunting sword, and had no idea when. His hand was shaking so hard the blade ticked against the wall.

"I want to go home," Pip said, in a very small voice that wasn't hers at all.

"We can't," Tobin said. "Not in the dark. We'll fall. We wait for morning."

"I want to go *home*."

"*We wait.*" He said it the way his father said things. Then, more quietly, "It's only till morning."

Ash sat with his back to the cold wall and the sword across his knees and watched Wren's lantern, and thought: that was something breathing out. And then, with a feeling half terror and half something worse, which was interest: I wonder what it ate.

### 1.4

*The founders' tunnels, then Hollowmere. Dawn.*

The lantern burned all night.

Wren knew how long a lantern that size burned, because her mother kept a book of what everything cost and Wren had copied pages of it for practice: four hours, five with the wick low. Her mother had filled this one to the brim, which she never did, because oil was dear and she said a brim-full lantern slopped. She had filled it and hung it on the hook and given Wren the staff and said, "Hold this for me," and then she'd kissed Wren on the forehead, which she also never did, because she said kisses were for babies and sailors.

Wren didn't trim the wick. She didn't touch it. She was afraid that if she touched it, it would remember it was supposed to go out.

Pip slept in the end, with her head in Tobin's lap and her shield still strapped to her bag. Ash didn't sleep. Tobin didn't either. Somewhere in the middle of the night he had cut his hand on the axe head, feeling for it in the dark, and he sat with his palm closed and the blood dried black between his fingers and wouldn't let Wren look at it. Once he said, to nobody, "She'll be with Da," and Wren said, "Yes," because it was what you said.

When the tunnel mouth turned from black to grey at the far end, a long way back, they walked toward it without talking.

The orchard was there. That was the first thing. The trees were there, heavy with apples, and the grass was wet, and a wood pigeon was going *who-who* somewhere as if nothing had happened. Wren nearly cried at the wood pigeon.

The pickers' hut was gone.

Where it had stood there was flattened earth and a doorstep. Ash stood on the step and looked down at his feet as if the hut might be under them.

They went down through the orchard to the town, and the town wasn't there.

That was what Wren would remember afterwards, the plainness of it. Nothing was burned or smashed. There was no blood. Where the houses had been there were foundations a hand high, and doorsteps, every doorstep in Hollowmere, set in rows along lanes with nothing on either side. You could see the lake from the orchard gate. The mill and the inn and the bakery were gone, and the fences, and the hens, and the washing.

The church stood.

It stood by itself at the top of the square, grey stone and its square tower, looking as surprised as anyone. The bell was gone out of the tower. You could see daylight through the louvres where it should have hung.

The banners were still there, the long feast banners on their poles round the square. Every one of them had gone white. Not faded. White as a bone left in a field, from top to bottom, as if somebody had drunk the colour out of them.

Tobin started to run.

He ran into the square with the axe banging against his legs, shouting, "Da! Nell! *Nell!*" He ran to where the long tables had been. There were no tables and no plates. The cobbles were bare and dry, as clean as if they'd been licked. He ran to the tithe ramp and the iron gate across the mine-mouth had cracked down the middle, and warm air was coming out of the crack, slow, in and out, and he stopped there with his hands on the bars.

Pip was calling for her gran. Ash wasn't calling anybody. He was walking along the doorsteps with his hand trailing an inch above them, reading them, the way Wren had seen him read animal tracks.

Wren walked to the top of Mill Lane, to the fourth doorstep on the left, which was hers.

It was a good step. Her mother had paid a mason for it the year Wren was born. Under where the mat used to lie, cut small into the stone, there was a little mark like a bent nail and a hook, which her mother had said was against rats. Wren knelt and put her finger on it. It was the only thing left of her house, and it was still there, and she thought, very clearly, the way you think things in dreams: I will have to remember her face now. There is nobody else to remember it for me.

She tried. She found her mother's hands, and the smell of her ink, and the way she said *Wren* with two syllables when she was cross. The face wouldn't come. It was there, just behind the lantern light, and it wouldn't turn round.

The lantern on the staff was still burning, in full daylight, for no reason at all. After a while she reached up and blew it out, and it went out at once, like any lantern would.

### 1.5

*The church, the first winter. PY 50 into PY 51.*

The church kept them alive, and Tobin never forgave it for being the only thing that did.

Everything inside it had survived: pews, hymn-books, the sexton's tools, a barrel of lamp oil, a chest of candles, the vestments and altar cloths, Amos Gill's cracked tar pot. Nobody knew why. Wren said it must be holy. Ash said if it was holy then why was the bell gone. Pip said maybe the bell was naughty, and laughed at her own joke, and then cried for an hour, which was how most of Pip's jokes went that first year.

They had the orchard, and the fields: barley and oats stood in the strips round the town, nobody's now, heavy and gold until the frost. Tobin didn't know why the fields were still good when everything else was gone, and didn't care. He took the sexton's sickle out every morning, and when his hands blistered he wrapped them in altar cloth and cut more.

He was ten. He learned that a sheaf has to dry before it'll thresh, that you winnow on a windy day or eat husks, that grinding between two churchyard stones takes most of a day for one loaf and comes out gritty however long you grind. He built an oven from river clay against the south wall, and it cracked, and he built another.

His bread was terrible. It was grey and heavy, burned black on the bottom and raw in the middle, with grit in it that you could hear between your teeth. It was the best bread in Hollowmere because it was the only bread in Hollowmere, and Pip said so every time she ate it, and every time it made him want to hit her.

He didn't hit her. He went outside and split wood instead.

They slept in the church cellar, because it was the only warm place, and that was the thing Tobin thought about least, on purpose. In the middle of the cellar floor was a trapdoor: old black oak banded with iron, with a ring as big as a dinner plate. The four of them couldn't lift it. Ash bent a crowbar on it.

But it was warm. On the coldest nights, with the lake frozen, you could put your hand flat on that trapdoor and it was warm as bread. So they laid the vestments out on it, and the altar cloths on top, and they slept there in a heap, the four of them, on the warm lid, like puppies on a hearthstone.

"What do you think's under it?" Pip said one night, in the dark.

"The crypt," Wren said.

"What's in the crypt?"

"Dead people."

"Warm dead people?"

"Go to sleep," Tobin said.

"I can feel it breathing," Pip said. "Can you feel it? It goes up and down."

"It doesn't."

"It *does*."

It did. Very slowly, so slowly you'd miss it if you weren't lying with your cheek against the wood: a long warm rise, and a long warm fall. Tobin lay awake and felt it under him and didn't say so. He had decided, round about the second week, that his job was to be the one who didn't say things. Somebody had to.

In the middle of that winter Pip got a fever and Wren sat up with her for three nights, wiping her face with snow, because Tobin had to cut wood and Ash had gone off hunting and not come back for two days. When he came back with a brace of hares and a split lip and no explanation, Tobin was waiting for him at the church door.

"Where were you?"

"Out."

"She nearly died."

"She didn't, though." Ash looked past him into the church, at Pip asleep by the fire. "I brought meat."

Tobin had his hand round Ash's collar before he knew he'd moved. Ash didn't fight. He just stood there and let Tobin hold him, and looked at him with that face, interested, as if Tobin were a thing he'd found under a log.

Tobin let go.

"Next time you tell me," he said.

"Next time I'll tell you," Ash said, and didn't, ever.

That night Pip woke, cool for the first time in three days, and said, "When are they coming back?" Tobin said, "Soon," and lay on the warm trapdoor listening to it breathe, and thought about a blue dress, and the word *good*.

### 1.6

*The pond below the south orchard. Spring, PY 51.*

Wren found Tuppence in the tall grass by the pond, on the first warm day.

She had been looking for him all winter without saying so. You weren't allowed to cry about a cat when everybody in the world was gone. But he was *her* cat, a scruffy grey tom with one ear torn by the Sedges' terrier, and he'd been out hunting by the pond the night of the feast because he always was.

He was crouched in the grass at the water's edge with one paw lifted and his eyes on something in the reeds. He was grey all through. Not his own grey. The grey of a stone in a wall, every hair of him, every whisker, his lifted paw, his torn ear. When she touched his back it was cold and hard and rough, and when she pushed, very gently, he didn't move, as if he'd grown down into the ground.

The collar wasn't stone.

It was the collar her mother had made him two summers ago: a plain band of soft leather with a little brass disc on it, and on the disc, scratched with a needle, a tiny row of marks. Her mother had made it at the kitchen table one night while Wren watched, and when Wren asked what the marks were for, her mother had smiled her small sideways smile and said, "To really give the cat nine lives." Later, when Wren pestered, she'd said it was a charm against foxes and wild dogs in the fields. Wren had thought it was a joke. Her mother didn't make jokes.

The leather was soft and warm under her fingers. The brass disc was warm. The cat inside it was stone.

Wren sat in the wet grass beside him until the sun went off the water. She didn't cry. She had decided in the tunnel that she wouldn't, and her body mostly went along with it.

She thought: everything was taken, and he wasn't. Something stopped it. And the thing that stopped it is round his neck, and my mother made it at our kitchen table.

She ran her thumbnail very lightly along the tiny scratched marks on the disc. There were nine of them. She didn't know what any of them meant.

But a person had written them. A person she knew. Which meant they could be learned.

When she got back to the church in the dark, Tobin was angry because she'd missed supper and he'd had to keep it warm, and he'd burned it. "Where were you?" he said. "Gods' teeth, Wren, I thought you'd gone in the lake."

"I found something," she said.

"What?"

She looked at the three of them by the fire: Tobin with his bandaged hands, Pip with the shield she slept with now, Ash with his split lip healing. She thought of how they'd look at her.

"Nothing," she said. "A frog."

It was a week before she told them. She was eight. It was the first secret she ever kept from them, and she found she was good at it.

### 1.7

*Wren's house, rebuilt. Autumn, PY 52.*

It took them two summers to put Wren's house back.

Tobin did most of it, though Pip would never have said so to his face. He knew timber from the yard. He felled trees at the orchard's far end, dragged them with a rope and his own back because there were no oxen any more, and squared them with Hob's axe, which never seemed to dull. Wren drew the plans: her old house exactly, measured from the foundation stones, and when Tobin wanted to put the door somewhere easier she wouldn't let him.

"That's where it was," she said.

"It'd be warmer facing the other way."

"That's where it *was*."

So the door went where it was.

The house was crooked, and the plaster cracked in the first frost, and the roof was thatch because none of them could make tiles. But it had a hearth and four walls and a door, and the first night they slept in it Pip lay on her pallet and laughed and laughed until Tobin told her to shut up, and then she cried, which Pip said later was a joke too.

The week after, Wren started on the wall.

She did it on the long wall opposite the hearth, the one with no window. She'd made paint from soot and egg and lime, and she had a brush made from her own hair tied to a stick, and she sat in front of the bare plaster with a hymn-book open on her knee for the spellings and said, "Everyone. I want everyone."

"Everyone what?" said Pip.

"Every name. Everyone who was at the feast. Everyone who lived here." Wren looked round at them. "We have to write them down. Before we forget. If we forget them there won't be anyone who remembers they were here."

It took most of a month of evenings. Wren painted and the others remembered, lane by lane, doorstep by doorstep. They fought about whether Granfer Sedge had died before the feast or after, and laughed about the Pell twins nobody could tell apart, and then stopped laughing.

Pip couldn't write yet. Wren was teaching her letters from the hymn-books, and Pip hated being taught by someone barely bigger than her. But she remembered everybody: who'd shouted at her for climbing what, and who gave out plums.

"Dun Harrow," she said. "The smith. He's got a scar on his chin. He gave me a horseshoe nail once and didn't say anything, just gave it me."

Wren painted *Dun Harrow*.

"Odile Ostrander," Pip said. She'd been waiting to say it all evening. "Sergeant of the watch."

Wren painted it, and Pip was surprised by how much she wanted to touch the letters.

"And my mam," Pip said.

Wren's brush stopped.

"She wasn't here," Wren said. "She went downriver. When you were two."

"Put her anyway."

"She wasn't at the feast, Pip. She's not taken. She's just gone."

"Put her *anyway*."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then she painted, small, at the bottom of the Ostrander line, a first name only, because Pip had never known any other. It was the worst kind of lie, the kind everybody knows and agrees not to say. Pip looked at it there under Gran's and felt better.

They came to the Marsh house on the third night.

"Hob Marsh," Tobin said. He was sitting on the bench by the door, mending a boot, not looking up. "Timber yard."

Wren painted it. "Jessamy," she said, softly, and Tobin said, "She died before," and Wren nodded and didn't paint it.

"Nell Marsh," Wren said.

Tobin's hands stopped on the boot.

"She was five," Wren said, painting. "She had a blue dress for the feast. I remember." She finished the last letter and sat back. "Was she with your father? When we went?"

"Da had her," Tobin said.

He went on mending the boot. After a minute he put it down, and got up, and went outside, and they heard the axe start up in the dark, *chock*, *chock*, *chock*, splitting wood they didn't need.

Pip watched the door. She was nine, but she knew the sound of Tobin lying, because it was the same as the sound of Tobin sad, and she'd heard a lot of both. She didn't say anything. She found she was good at not saying things too, when she wanted. She just didn't want to very often.

When Wren had finished, the wall held a hundred and twenty names, and one that shouldn't have been there, in lines of black letters that went from the ceiling almost to the floor. Wren sat in front of it with the lamp and read them all aloud, every one, top to bottom, and it took most of an hour. Then she said, "We'll get them back," in the voice she used for plans, and nobody argued, because what else was there to say.

Pip lay awake that night looking at the two names at the bottom of her line, the real one and the lie, and thought: one day Wren will tick every name on that wall, and when she comes to Mam's she'll stop with the brush and look at me. She found she didn't mind.

### 1.8

*The high scree above Hollowmere, then Wren's house. Winter, PY 59.*

Ash came down off the scree at dusk with his hands warm, which was the problem.

It was the coldest week of the year, the lake frozen white from shore to shore. Ash had spent the afternoon on his stomach on a shelf of rock above the treeline with his ear pressed to a crack no wider than a knife blade, and the crack was warm, and something at the bottom of it had been talking to him.

Nearly three years ago he'd found it tracking a wounded ibex: a stripe of bare rock where the snow wouldn't settle, and a crack in it that breathed. He'd put his ear to it out of the same curiosity that made him turn over every log in the forest, and heard, a very long way down, a tone. A host saying *come in, come in*.

He went back. Of course he went back. The words came after a while. They were never cruel. They were warm and polite, and they told him true things: where the ice on the lake was thin, which old shafts had foul air. Once it told him his brother was asleep and not in pain, and he lay on the rock and wept for an hour, then went home and was very kind to everyone, and nobody knew why.

Pip knew. She'd followed him once, two summers back, and seen him with his ear to the rock, and said with her whole face gone white, "I'm not telling Tobin," and never had. He had never thanked her. It was the kind of thing he was bad at.

He pulled his gloves on before he reached the town so nobody would feel his hands.

Hollowmere at night was doorsteps under snow, four rebuilt houses and three lit windows: Wren's, the Marsh house on the mill road, and Pip's on the watch-house step. His own, the Calder house he'd rebuilt badly last year, stood dark; he hardly slept in it. They'd all moved out of Wren's house in the end, because they were grown and it was small. But every night, whatever the weather, they still came back to it. Nobody had ever said it was a rule.

Tonight Tobin and Pip weren't there yet. Wren was alone at the table, with her mother's staff laid across it and the cat's collar beside it on a square of cloth, and a candle, and she was writing in the air.

She was drawing slow, careful marks with the staff's brass foot in the empty air above the table, and where the brass went it left a faint thread of dull gold, like a hair caught in sunlight, hanging there.

She drew six marks. The last one closed. And a small light came on above the table, without a flame, without anything, a little round glow the colour of a lantern seen through fog, and stayed.

Wren sat back and looked at it. Ash had seen her face do many things in ten years, but he had never seen it do this. It wasn't joy. She looked at the light the way you look at a dog you've just realised isn't yours.

"How long have you been able to do that?" he said from the door.

She jumped. The light didn't go out. It just hung there. "A while," she said. "Not long. A year." Then, because it was Ash and she couldn't help it: "It won't go out. I've tried. I have to rub the marks off the air, and you can't rub air."

He sat down opposite her. She was looking at his gloves.

"It's from the collar," she said. "And a line on the staff's brass foot, under the verdigris. And the step at my house. My mother wrote them all. It's an alphabet. I've had ten years to stare at twenty-two letters. Some of them I can make do things."

"What's it say on the collar?"

"Something like *spare*," she said. "Something like *not this one*." She didn't look at him. "It's close now. I can feel it. I can nearly read the whole thing."

"And then?"

"And then I wake him."

Ash put his hand into the light, and it was the warm from the fissure exactly, the warm with a voice under it, and he knew as plainly as he'd ever known anything that she knew where these letters came from and had decided not to say. And that he wasn't going to say either.

He took his hand back. He took off his gloves.

Wren looked at his hands, which were warm in the coldest week of the year, and then at his face, and didn't ask. He saw her decide not to.

He didn't know which of them leaned first. Afterwards he decided it was her, because that let him off; in the honest part of himself he knew it was him. It was a short kiss, across a table, with a light that wouldn't go out hanging over it and an enchanted cat collar at his elbow. It tasted of candle smoke. When it was done she sat back and looked at the table and he looked at the door.

Tobin's boots on the step outside, stamping the snow off. Pip's voice behind him, complaining.

Wren put her hand over the little light. It didn't go out, but under her palm it hardly showed.

They never talked about it, all that winter. Ash thought about the warm under her hand and the warm in the rock, and how they were the same warm, and what that meant about both of them. He found it very interesting.

He was ashamed of how interesting he found it. He went back up the scree the next day anyway.

### 1.9

*The pond below the south orchard. Autumn, PY 60, a week before the old feast day.*

They all came to watch, which Wren had not wanted and had not been able to stop.

Tobin stood with his arms folded, the way he stood at the edge of anything he couldn't carry. Pip sat cross-legged in the grass eating an apple, loudly. Ash leaned on a willow with his bow unstrung and his eyes on the water, as if the cat were the least interesting thing here, which meant he was watching harder than anyone.

Tuppence crouched where he had crouched for ten years, stone-grey, one paw up, his eyes on a frog that had died of old age a decade ago. Moss had grown on his back. Wren had scraped it off every spring.

She had the whole line now. She had found the last of it in August, on her mother's doorstep in the rain, the way you find a word that's been on the tip of your tongue for a week. She had waited a month after that. She told the others she was making sure. The truth was that she was afraid, and didn't know of what.

She lifted the staff and began to write.

Not on the air this time. On the cat. She drew each letter with the brass foot an inch above his stone fur, beginning at his torn ear, going down his spine, and the gold threads settled onto him like spider silk and sank in. The ninth letter closed under his chin, beside the brass disc of the collar, and she said it aloud, which she had never done before, because some letters wanted saying.

It was not a word anyone should have been able to say with a human mouth. It came out of her anyway. It tasted of iron.

The grey went out of Tuppence the way frost goes off a window when the sun comes round. It went from the ear first. Then the head, the back, the tail. His lifted paw turned from stone to grey fur, and the fur moved in the wind, and the paw came down.

He finished the pounce. Ten years late, he landed in the reeds with a splash, missed the frog that wasn't there, sat up in the water with an expression of deep personal outrage, and looked at Wren.

Then he walked out of the pond, shook himself all over her, and bit her hand hard enough to draw blood.

Pip choked on her apple laughing. Tobin made a sort of bark and turned away. Wren knelt in the wet grass with the cat butting his head under her bleeding hand, purring like a cartwheel on gravel, and she was crying at last, ten years late, like the cat.

"Fifth uncounted," Pip said, wiping her eyes. "Look at him. He's the fifth. Uncounted cat."

Ash wasn't laughing. Ash was looking at the mountain.

The wind had turned. It turned all at once, in the middle of a sentence, from the soft west wind off the lake to a hard cold one off the peak, and the reeds all bent the other way together like a crowd turning to look. The pond shivered. A dark line of cloud was coming over the shoulder of the mountain, fast, too fast, swallowing the blue.

And under Wren's knees, very deep, so deep she wasn't sure she felt it at all, the ground turned over.

Like a sleeper in a bed, she thought. Like a sleeper turning over when someone in the next room says their name.

### 1.10

*Wren's house. The same night.*

The storm got worse until there was nowhere left for it to go.

Rain hammered the shutters. Thunder came off the mountain in cracks you felt in the floor, and the lightning showed the lane white as noon, all those doorsteps standing in the flood. Tuppence had gone under Wren's bed in the first hour and refused all offers.

None of them went home. Nobody suggested it.

"It's weather," Wren said, for the third time. She was sitting at the table with her bitten hand bound up and her staff across her knees. "Autumn storms come off the peak. We get one every year."

"Not like this," Ash said.

"You don't know that."

"I've been up the mountain in every storm for ten years, Wren. Not like this."

"It's weather."

"Ten years," Pip said. She was on the hearth with her knees up, her shield against her back, and she'd stopped joking an hour ago. "Ten years, and nothing. Not a tremor. Not a breath from that gate that wasn't the same as the one before. And then you go and pick the lock on it for a *cat*."

"Pip," Tobin said.

"No, she should hear it." Pip's voice was thin and getting higher. "You know what that writing is. I've seen your face when you do it. And you did it anyway, because you wanted your cat back, and now there's something under the town *rolling over*, and you say *weather*—"

"*Pip.*" Tobin didn't shout. He didn't need to. Pip shut her mouth and put her face on her knees.

Wren hadn't moved. She was looking at the shutters. Her face had gone very still, the way it went when someone had found the place in her that she'd built the walls around.

Tobin went to the window, and after a while she came and stood beside him, watching the lightning walk across the lake through the cracks in the shutter. Her shoulder was nearly touching his arm. He had been feeling the warmth of that gap for six years and had never once closed it, because closing it would have been taking something for himself, and he didn't do that.

"Was it me?" she said, very quietly, so the others wouldn't hear.

He thought about lying. He was good at it. He'd had practice.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe." And then, because he couldn't help it: "I'd have done it. For Nell. If there'd been a spell."

She looked at him then, sideways, surprised. He looked at the lightning.

The next bolt hit the church.

A white rope from the cloud straight into the bell tower. And under the thunder came another sound, from below: a deep, flat *whump* up through the soles of their feet that rattled every cup on the shelf, like a door the size of a hill kicked open from the inside.

Then nothing.

The wind dropped. Just dropped, all at once, as if somebody had shut a window. The rain thinned to a patter and stopped. In the silence they could hear water running off the thatch, and Tuppence growling, low and steady, under the bed.

Pip got up and went to the other window and opened the shutter.

Across the dark town, at the top of the empty square, the church was lit. Every window. Every one of the tall plain windows down the nave and the small ones in the tower, glowing warm and yellow in the wet night, the way it hadn't glowed in ten years, the way none of them had ever seen it glow except on feast nights when they were small.

"Well," Pip said, in a voice that tried hard to be hers. "Somebody's home."

### 1.11

*The church. Past midnight.*

They took their weapons, because they always did.

None of them had to say it. Tobin took the axe from beside the door, Pip buckled on the dagger and the dented buckler, Wren took the staff, Ash strung Jory's bow on the step, and they went out into the wet like people going to work. Ten years had made the heirlooms into tools. Tonight, walking up the black square toward the lit church, they felt like what they'd been given for.

Tuppence came too, at Wren's heel, his fur in a ridge from his ears to his tail.

Rainwater ran off Elder Vane's memorial stone by the churchyard gate: CORWIN VANE, WHO CARRIED THE FIRST TITHE INTO THE DEEP TO SEE IT SETTLED. Ash had always wondered why a man would do that. He touched the stone in passing, out of habit. It was cold, and he was oddly glad.

The church door stood open.

Inside, every lantern was burning: the pillar hooks down the nave, the rood beam, the two big ones on the altar, lanterns that had hung cold and empty for ten years. Every one burned with a steady yellow flame, and there was no oil smell, and no smoke.

"In the Vale," Wren said, "a feast begins when the lanterns are lit."

Nobody answered her.

The vestry door was open, and the cellar steps beyond it, and there was a light coming up the steps that wasn't yellow. It was red. Low and warm and red, like the inside of a hand held over a candle.

They went down.

The cellar was the cellar, where they'd slept that first winter. In the middle of the floor the trapdoor stood open. It had been thrown back so hard that the oak had split along its length and one iron band had snapped and curled up like a ribbon of wood shaving. Below it was a square of darkness with a stone stair going down, and the red light came up out of it, and the warm. The warm came up slow, in and out.

"We slept on that," Pip said.

"For two years," said Ash.

"I *said* it was breathing. I said. Every story Gran ever told me, this is the bit where somebody goes down the stairs. Lanterns that light themselves. Door burst open from underneath. Something breathing in the dark. That's not a crypt, that's a door to hell, that is, that's a *portal to hell*—"

"Every story your gran told you," Ash said, "was a lie to keep you out of holes."

"It's a cellar," Tobin said. "Under a cellar."

From a long way down came a slow wet dripping, and a scraping, as of something heavy dragged across stone. Under both, so low that Ash felt it more than heard it, a beat. Far too slow for a heart. A heart the size of a barn.

He knew that beat. He'd been lying on a warm rock listening to it for three years.

He said nothing. He found that his mouth had simply decided not to open, and he let it.

Wren had knelt by the thrown-back trapdoor. "Look," she said. "Look at the underside."

They looked. The underside of the oak was covered in writing. Not carved. Painted, in some dark ink gone brown, close lines of small hooked letters packed edge to edge from one corner to the other, round the ring, over the iron bands, like ivy grown over a wall. The ones near the split were blistered and blackened, as if they'd been burned from below.

"It's the same," Wren said. "It's the same as the collar."

"Can you read it?" said Tobin.

"Some." She put out a finger and didn't quite touch. "That one's *below*. That's *stay*. That's *shut*, I think, or *closed*. And this one, all down the edges, over and over—" She stopped. "That's the one on the collar. It's the one I couldn't read for years. It's *keep*."

"Keep what?"

"Keep *in*." Wren sat back on her heels. Her face was the colour of the plaster. "It's a ward. It's all a ward, the whole door. Somebody wrote it to keep something down there. And the storm broke it."

"Somebody," Ash said.

Wren didn't look at him.

At the top of the stone stair, Tuppence had stopped. He sat down on the very edge of the first step with his tail wrapped round his feet and his fur standing up like a brush, and he looked down into the red dark, and he did not move. Wren crouched and held out her hand to him, and he didn't come.

"He won't go," Pip said. "Even the cat won't go. That's how you know."

"They're down there," Wren said.

"You don't know that."

"Tuppence was stone and he woke. They were taken the same night. There weren't any bodies, Tobin. They're *asleep*." She stood up with the staff in both hands. "They're down there asleep. Everybody on the wall. Your father. Nell. My mother."

Tobin looked at her for a long time. Then he looked down the stair. Ash watched him do the sum he always did, the one about who would carry what and what it would cost and who would pay, and arrive at the answer he always arrived at.

"Who goes down first?" Pip said.

Nobody moved.

Then Tobin shifted the axe on his shoulder, the way his father used to, and stepped past Wren onto the first stair.

"Me," he said. "Obviously."

### 1.12

*The Undercroft, floor 1: the cellar under the church.*

It was the town's root cellar, Pip decided, if the town's root cellar had been swallowed and chewed and spat out wet.

The stair let them out into a long vault of brick and timber that went on further than any cellar should. Its beams sagged like wet bread and turned corners they had no business turning. Water stood ankle-deep on the floor, warm as a bath, and it smelled of soft apples and, under that, iron and butcher's yard, which Pip remembered from a tunnel ten years ago.

The lanterns were the worst. Feast lanterns, the paper and tin ones from the lines across the square, hung on the cellar hooks all down the vault, every one lit and dripping wax onto the water, *tik*, *tik*, as if it had been burning since the night of the feast.

On the beams over their heads somebody had chalked tallies: little gates of four strokes and a slash, rows and rows of them, the way the carters counted barrels.

"That's Abner Stroud's hand," said Tobin. "He did the tithe count."

"Lovely," said Pip. "Can we go home now?"

The first thing that tried to kill them was a jar of jam.

It came off a shelf with a wet pop and rolled toward them across the water, a fat crock of quince jam with a cloth tied over the top. Then the crock split down one side and the split opened like a mouth. The glaze was cracked all over with glowing red lines, like a coal you've blown on, and two little black horns had pushed up through the cloth. Inside, the jam moved.

"Oh, that's horrible," said Pip, with real feeling, and hit it with her shield.

It burst on the buckler like a rotten egg, and the jam was hot. It went over her arm and hissed and she yelled and then the rats came.

They weren't rats. They were a rat-king, a knot of rats grown together at the tails, eight or nine of them, black and scorched and smoking, with ember eyes and too many teeth, and they moved as one thing, rolling, all heads. One of the heads got her forearm just above the buckler strap and held on.

She stabbed it with Gran's dagger again and again, and the head let go only when Tobin's axe took the knot in half. The halves kept moving for a while. And she looked up and saw Ash on the stair with an arrow on the string, not drawing, just watching the rat-king roll, with that face like he was reading a book.

"You could have *shot* it," she said, when she could talk. Her arm was running red into the warm water. "You were standing right there."

"I wanted to see how it moved," Ash said. Then he heard himself, and his face changed. "Pip. I'm sorry. I'd have shot."

"When? After it ate me? Gods rot you, Ash."

Wren sewed the bite on a crate while Pip swore at her. Wren had taught her to sew wounds at twelve; it was a different thing having it done.

"It'll scar," Wren said.

"Under the bracer," said Pip. "Nobody'll see." She looked at Ash. "I'll know."

Later a broom swept out of a side vault on bristles of black bone, pushing a little wave of embers ahead of it, and Tobin broke its handle over his knee after it took the skin off his shin. A second rat-king had a long iron key with a T-bar handle tangled in its tails, and they chased it through three vaults before Ash put an arrow through the knot.

"Sluice key," said Tobin, working it loose. "There's a valve back there."

The valve turned. Somewhere a sluice gate groaned, and the water drained away through a grate in the floor, uncovering a second stair going down, slick with silt.

Pip knelt at the grate and looked through the bars. A long way down it was red: a deep living red like light through skin, brightening and dimming, slow, in time with the beat she'd been feeling in her teeth since the church.

"Well," Pip said. "It's a portal to hell. Called it."

Nobody laughed. But Tobin put his hand on her head for a second as he went past, the way he used to when she was small, and she let him.

At the far end of the vault, by the stair, there was an alcove sealed with a slab of black stone. Something was carved in its middle: a sigil like a coiled shell, with a line through it. Wren stood in front of it with her staff for a long time.

"Can you read it?" said Tobin.

"No," said Wren. She frowned. "I can't read it. It isn't one of my letters." And then, more quietly, as if she hadn't meant to say it: "The brass is cold. Everywhere else down here it's warm."

### 1.13

*The Undercroft, floor 2: larders and cold rooms.*

The stair behind them was gone.

Tobin turned at the bottom to tell Pip to watch her footing, and there was a wall. Old wet brick, no seam, no stair. It was warm.

"Don't," Wren said. "Don't look at it. Just go on."

He went on.

The larders went on and on, frost on the walls under the warm drip from the ceiling, winter at your feet and summer on your neck. Hams hung on hooks in rows like hanged men. Cheese wheels as big as cartwheels were stacked to the ceiling, and every one had a clean curved bite out of it as wide as Tobin's chest.

"Tell me it's rats, Ash," said Pip.

"It's rats," said Ash, and nobody believed him.

There were flour sacks, too, slumped in every corner, all of them stamped with the same mark in blue: a wheat-ear and a long-tailed *R*. Each sack had a crust of white on it, as if it had been dusted with chalk.

"That's Hollis Rye's," Wren said. "That's his bakery mark."

"Why's it in here?"

Nobody knew.

The jars came again, and the rat-kings, and the brooms, out of rooms Tobin was sure hadn't been there a minute before, and here they found out what they were. Pip was fast and brave and half Tobin's weight, and anything that got past her buckler got her. Wren could write a mark in the air that went off like a thrown pot of fire, but it took three breaths, and in three breaths a rat-king could cross a room. Ash shot well and fought badly up close. And Tobin stood in front of them and took it: a broom handle across the ribs, jam that blistered his arm, teeth through his boot. He blocked with the haft, because you held the line, and every block went up his arms like a hammer on an anvil.

At the end of the third room he sat down on a salt barrel and found he couldn't get up.

"Here," said Pip, and gave him a loaf.

His own bread, grey and gritty. He ate half in four bites and felt it do almost nothing. The cut on his arm closed over, a little.

"Still terrible," said Pip, not unkindly. She ate the other half. "Still the best bread in Hollowmere."

"You've got to stop standing in front of things," Wren said. She was white and sweating from the spell-writing. "You can't take it all. You'll fall down, and then we'll have to carry you, and you're very big."

He wanted to say: somebody has to stand in front of things. He didn't. He got up.

In the next room a broom came at him low and fast, sweeping embers, and he did something he hadn't done before. He didn't block. He stepped aside, the way you step aside from a falling tree, and let it go past him, and as it went past he let the axe swing.

He didn't have the strength to push it. He just let the head fall, and the broom came apart in a spray of bone and cinders, and the axe went on round in a long easy curve and came back to his shoulder as if it knew the way.

The haft went warm in his hands.

And he was in the timber yard, at dawn, aged six, with frost on the sawdust. His father was behind him, his big split hands over Tobin's small ones on a hatchet's handle. *Stop pushing it. Gods' sake, boy. You're not meant to swing the thing. Let the head do the work. You're only there to point it.* The smell of cut pine. His father's chin on the top of his head. His mother calling them in from the house for porridge, alive, cross, alive.

Then he was in the larder, with jam on his boots, and Pip was staring at him.

"Tobin? You went funny. Your eyes went."

"I'm fine," he said. His face was wet. He wiped it on his sleeve and said it was the drip from the ceiling, and they let him say it.

The axe felt lighter. He didn't know how an axe could feel lighter. It did.

### 1.14

*The Undercroft, floor 3: the drowned bakery.*

The bakery was under the water, and the water was cold.

Wren saw the sign first. It hung in the black water of a great flooded vault, upside down, swinging very slowly on its chains: a carved wooden loaf, gilded once, and under it in faded red letters, RYE. Below it, through water clear and dark as night glass, was the whole of Hollis Rye's bakery, folded down into a cistern the size of the church: counter, flour bins, the long kneading table, and three great domed ovens with their iron doors open, drowned. Loaves floated above them, a hundred or more, grey and swollen and turning slowly, like drowned birds.

On the kneading table, under the water, there was a shape.

"That's him," Pip said. "That's a man. Wren, that's a *man*."

The whole floor had been sluices, as if the dream had learned what the key in Tobin's belt was for and built a house around it. The bakery vault had two gates, one high, one low. Wren stood looking at them while the others argued.

"Open both," said Pip.

"Then the low one floods the passage we're stood in," said Ash.

"Shut up," said Wren. "Both of you. I'm thinking."

It took most of an hour: the high gate, then the low gate shut and the cross-channel opened so the water had somewhere to go, then the high gate again, Tobin hauling on the key with his whole weight. The water went down. The loaves went down with it, and the swinging sign, and the bakery came up out of the dark into the red light as if it were surfacing: counter, shelves, oven doors streaming, the kneading table, and the shape on it.

As the water fell below the oven mouths, the middle oven moved.

Something unfolded out of that black iron mouth like a man getting up out of a chair too small for him, and kept unfolding: taller than Tobin by half again, made of soot and a baker's apron gone hard and black. In place of one arm it had a baker's peel, the long flat shovel for pulling loaves, edged in glowing iron. Its face was an oven door. Behind the bars, something burned.

"Is that the baker?" said Pip.

"That's what's guarding the baker," said Ash. He had an arrow on the string, and this time he drew.

The peel swept the table and the counter and anyone near either. It took Tobin's legs out from under him once; the second time he stepped aside the way he'd learned and the peel broke on the counter. Pip went in under its other arm with Gran's dagger and came out with her sleeve on fire. Ash put five arrows into the oven door before one went through the bars.

Wren's first fire-mark didn't take. Her hand shook, the letter came out wrong, the gold thread curdled black and stank, and the backlash went up the staff into her arm like grabbing a hot pan. The thing turned its oven face toward her, and she thought, very clearly: it wants to put me *in*.

She stood her ground and wrote it again. It took.

It fell apart, ash and apron and peel, and the fire behind the bars went out with a sigh. Afterwards Wren found the iron hook at the top of her mother's staff bent nearly straight, and the lantern cracked in the silt at her feet. She didn't remember either happening. She picked the lantern up and held it.

The baker lay on the kneading table on his front, arms out, elbow-deep in dough.

It had risen and gone on rising, over his arms and back and the back of his head, a swollen grey crust set hard as stone. Only his face showed, turned sideways, eyes shut. He looked as though he had fallen asleep mid-knead and been baked where he lay.

"Can you do it?" Tobin said. "Like the cat?"

Wren put her hand on the crust. It felt like nothing. There was no collar here, no letters to read.

"I don't know how," she said. "It's not the same. I don't know where to start."

Tobin looked at the crust. Then he lifted the axe, and before Wren could say *careful*, he set the edge of it into the crust along the line of the man's spine, light, the way you'd score a loaf before it goes in.

"Bread's bread," he said.

### 1.15

*The drowned bakery.*

The crust cracked like any loaf left in too long. Tobin put his fingers into the split and pulled, and a great slab came away whole, and underneath was a man's back in a flour-white shirt, warm, and breathing. When they had the rest off him, Hollis Rye lay in a ring of broken crust with his arms still out in front of him, his fingers kneading nothing.

Then he opened his eyes and sat up.

He was fifty-odd, heavy in the shoulders and soft in the middle, with a baker's thick forearms and a face that had been handsome once and still thought it was. He looked at Pip, and Ash, and Wren, without much interest. Then he looked at Tobin, and his eyes went narrow and very sharp.

"You've got big," Hollis Rye said. "Who's been feeding you? It's not been done right."

Tobin had to turn away and look at the drowned ovens. His throat had shut. Wren put her hand on his back and kept it there.

Ten years of grit between his teeth, of three other children to feed and not one person in the world to tell him whether he was doing it right. And the first grown man to open his eyes looked at him for one breath and saw it all, and told him: no. You did it wrong.

It was the kindest thing anyone had said to him since he was ten years old.

"Where am I?" Hollis was looking round now, at the dripping oven mouths and the upside-down sign. "This is my shop. Why's my shop wet?" He put a hand to his head. "The feast. There was the feast. Amos was ringing us out, and they'd just started on the scales, and—"

He stopped.

"And what?" said Ash.

"Nothing," Hollis said. "I don't remember." He said it too quickly and looked at the floor. "Where's Marta? Marta Fenn. She was by the soup. Where is she?"

"We don't know yet," Wren said.

"What do you mean, *yet*?" He looked at Tobin again, at his height and his beard. "How old are you, Marsh boy?"

"Twenty."

Hollis Rye sat on his kneading table and said nothing for a long time.

Pip, who couldn't bear a silence, held out a loaf. "Here," she said. "You must be starving. It's not good, but it's food."

He took it and bit into it and chewed, and his face did a slow, terrible thing, and he spat it out onto the floor of his own bakery.

"Who taught you to bake?"

"Nobody," said Tobin.

"It *shows*." Hollis wiped his mouth. "Grit. No salt. Never proved. Ten years of that? It's a wonder you've got teeth."

Then he saw the sack.

It was half sunk in the silt by the flour bins, one of the sacks from the larders above: the blue wheat-ear, the long-tailed *R*, the crust of chalk-white. Hollis looked at it, and the colour left his face at once, like the feast banners.

"That's not mine," he said.

"It's got your mark on it," said Pip.

"It's not mine. Somebody's put my mark on it. I never—" He shut his mouth and looked at the red glow coming up through the drains, and his big floury hands began to shake.

Nobody said anything, and Tobin, who knew a lie told by leaving out when he heard one, thought: so it's not just me.

The warm came then.

It came out of the middle oven, where the guard had been: a long breath rushing not out at them but up, into the flue. Tobin looked into the oven's mouth and saw that the flue went on, a round black shaft of sooted brick far longer than any chimney, and at the very top, impossibly far, a small disc of grey morning light.

Hollis rose off the kneading table. His feet left the floor and his apron lifted round him, as though something under him had breathed in and taken him with it. He grabbed for the nearest thing, which was Tobin's arm, and held on with a baker's grip.

"What's this? Marsh boy, what's—"

"Let him go," Wren said. "Tobin. It's letting him go. Look. It's taking him *up*."

Tobin held on. He didn't mean to. His hand just held, the way it held everything, and his feet were sliding toward the oven mouth, and Ash grabbed his belt.

"Tobin," said Wren. "You have to let go of him."

He let go.

Hollis Rye went up the flue like a man pulled up a well. He went up fast, turning slowly, his arms out, his apron flapping, shouting something that came down to them in pieces off the sooty brick. "My oven! On the square! You hear me? *Facing east!* Near the well! Facing *east*, you hopeless—"

Ash was already in the oven mouth with one hand on the flue's rim, as if he meant to climb after him. The brick was hot. He took his hand back fast, and the flue narrowed above him with a sound like a swallow, the round shaft drawing in on itself, and he dropped back onto the bakery floor.

But for a moment, before it closed, they all saw.

At the top of the flue, in that disc of daylight, there was the edge of a hearthstone, grey sky, and a man standing on a bare foundation, turning slowly to look at the square: the dry fountain, the white banners, the doorsteps with nothing behind them, the church standing on its own.

Then the baker sat down on the floor of his ruined bakery, all at once, as if his legs had been taken away, and put his face in his floury hands.

The flue shut. The light went. The oven was an oven, cold, and the warm air settled back to its slow in and out.

"He's home," Wren said, after a while. Her voice didn't sound like anything. "He's home. He's safe. Whatever happens to us now, he's out." And then, as if she couldn't help it: "That's one."

"One," said Pip, "out of a hundred and twenty."

"One," Wren said again, and Tobin could see her already, back at the wall with the soot paint, making the first tick the wall had ever held, beside *Hollis Rye*.

If they got back to the house.

At the far side of the vault the floor fell away in a stone stair. A warm wind came up it, and on the wind there was steam. He could hear water down there, not dripping now but rolling, low and steady, like a pot coming up to the boil.

Behind him Pip was retying her buckler over the sewn bite, Ash was counting arrows, and Wren had hung her cracked lantern back on its bent hook and was looking at no one. Tobin let the axe settle on his shoulder. It was lighter than it had been at the top of the stair. He didn't trust that.

*It's not been done right.*

No, he thought. It hasn't. Nothing has.

He went down first. Obviously.
