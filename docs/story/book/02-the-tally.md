# Chapter 2: The Tally

### 2.1

*The Undercroft, floor 4: the wine vaults and upper cisterns.*

The water had started to talk.

Ash heard it before he saw it: a small, busy sound under everything, like a pot left on the hob by somebody who has gone out. At the foot of the stair the vault opened into long aisles of wine racks, black oak and iron, running away into the red dark further than any cellar under any inn. Between the racks ran channels of water, stone-lipped, the width of a cart, and the water in them was moving. Not flowing. Moving the way soup moves just before it comes up: a slow turning, a skin of steam, and every so often, at the edges, a bubble the size of a fist that rose and broke with a sound like a tongue clicked against teeth.

He crouched at the lip of the nearest channel and held his palm over it, not close. The heat came up into his hand like the heat off a horse's flank. Further down the aisle a spout of steam burst from a crack in the floor with a long hiss, hung, and fell back.

"It's getting hotter the further down we go," Ash said. "The bakery was cold."

"Very good," said Pip. "Shall we give you a prize?" The sewn bite on her arm had stiffened, and she held the arm a little away from her body, the way you hold a burned thing.

There were thousands of casks, racked to the vault, each with a vintner's mark burned into the head. Ash knocked on one as he passed, the way Pip had knocked on the tunnel walls ten years ago. A deep full note. The next the same. The third answered with nothing, a dry flat sound, like knocking on a door with nobody home.

The cask head had a seam round it that no cooper would have left. He got his knife into it and levered, and the whole round front swung out on two little brass hinges, like a cupboard door. Inside, where there should have been wine, there was a sack. Grain. Barley, by the smell, packed tight to the staves, with another sack behind it, and another.

He started to laugh. He couldn't help it.

"Look at that," he said. "Even the cellar was lying."

Tobin came and looked over his shoulder. He didn't laugh. He put his hand on the sack and felt the barley through the cloth, the way he felt a loaf to see if it had proved, and his face did something closed.

"That's good barley," he said.

"It's somebody's good barley."

"The inn had racks like these," Wren said, quietly. "Under the inn. My mother said they were for the feast wine." She looked down the aisle, at the thousands of casks, and Ash watched her do sums. "How many of them are hollow?"

They knocked on twenty more. Six were.

Nobody said what it meant. Ash thought of the tithe carts going down the ramp every autumn, and the grown-ups round the scales nodding, and all the while, under the inn, barley sitting in false casks waiting for winter. He thought: every grown-up I knew. And then, because he was Ash: I wonder what else.

The drip-spirits found them in the next aisle. They came off the vault ribs like any drop of water, a fat bead swelling on the stone, and on the way down they opened: arms, a round mouth, a little body of hot clear water no bigger than a cat with something red turning in it like a coal in a bottle. The first hit Tobin's shoulder and burst, and his shirt steamed. Ash shot two in the air and they popped like bubbles and stung the backs of his hands. Wren wrote a mark, three breaths, gold, and a sheet of heat went across the ceiling and boiled every swelling drop off the stone at once, and the vault filled with steam so thick that Pip said, "*Brilliant*, Wren," and walked into a rack.

The eels were in the deep channel at the end: dark backs as thick as his leg, a line of ember-red along each flank like a split in a stove. One rose with a mouth like a bucket as Tobin reached for the valve wheel, and Tobin took his hand back so fast he fell over.

"Let me," said Ash. He sat and watched them a while. They came up when the steam vents went, every time, as if the noise called them, and went down when it stopped. He counted. Then he turned the valve in the quiet between two vents, three full turns, and was back on the step before the next hiss. The eels came up and found nothing.

"You could have *said* you'd done that before," said Pip.

"I hadn't. I watched them first." He was pleased, and tried not to look it.

The valve emptied a cistern beyond the racks, and they found the smith there.

Dun Harrow stood upright against the cistern's far wall in a pool of steaming water that came to his knees, and he was wrapped in chain. Cart chain, the heavy kind for traces, round and round him from his shins to his chest, so many turns that he looked like a barrel bound in iron. His head hung forward. His beard was full of steam beads. On every link, small and neat, there was a stamped mark: a hammer over a wavy line.

"That's Dun's mark," said Pip. "That's the stream. He showed me once." She went into the hot water without thinking, and swore, and kept going. "He's wrapped up in his own *chain*."

The axe skated off the links and rang. Ash went round the back, careful in the water, and found the last link at the man's shoulder closed with a cart linchpin. "Pip. You've got the smallest hands."

She worked it out with her sleeve round her fingers, swearing steadily, and the chain slid off the smith in long loops like a snake deciding to go, and he fell forward onto his knees in the pool.

Dun Harrow was a big man gone thick, forearms burned shiny from fifty years of sparks, a white scar on his chin. He breathed a while. Then he lifted his head and looked at them one at a time, and his eyes stopped on Pip.

"Odile's girl," he said.

"You gave me a horseshoe nail," said Pip. "When I was little. You didn't say anything. You just gave it me."

The smith looked at her for a long time. Ash knew that look. He'd seen it on Hollis Rye, looking at Tobin: the look of a grown man doing ten years of arithmetic in one breath. But it wasn't the same look. Hollis had looked at Tobin like a loaf done wrong. Dun Harrow looked at Pip the way a man looks at a bill he'd hoped had got lost.

"Did I," he said.

"Where's Gran? Was she with you? Did you see her?"

"Don't know." He looked away from her, at the chain in the water. "Don't know, girl."

The warm came then, as in the bakery: a long breath, rising. There was a well in the corner of the cistern that Ash hadn't seen till now, and the air went up it like a chimney drawing and took the smith with it. He went up streaming, slowly turning, and he didn't shout a wish. He looked down at Pip the whole way, until the brick closed over him with a soft wet sound, like a mouth.

"Well," Pip said, into the quiet. "Friendly."

"He's out," said Wren. "That's two."

Ash looked back the way they'd come. At the stair down, on a hook by the top step, there had been a feast lantern burning, one of the tin and paper ones from the lines across the square. He'd seen it when they came in. Now it hung dark, a little curl of smoke going up from the wick.

He opened his mouth to say so and shut it again. He wanted to see what it did next.

Before the stair there was an alcove sealed with black stone, like the one on the first floor, with a different sign: a miner's pick crossed with a flame. Wren held the brass foot out to it and shivered, though the vault was hot as a bathhouse.

"Cold again," she said. "It isn't writing. It's a mark, like Hollis's *R* on the sacks, or Dun's hammer. A sign for a place." She lowered the staff. "It isn't ours."

"Whose, then?"

Wren didn't answer. The stair beyond was full of steam, and from the bottom of it came the sound of a great deal of water boiling all at once, and under that a slow brazen bellow, like a beast in a byre a long way off, calling for its supper.

### 2.2

*The Undercroft, floor 5: the cistern dome.*

Pip had seen the tithe oxen every autumn of her life, and she knew them at once, and that was the worst of it.

The dome was bigger than the square. Its ribs were tithe-cart wheels, hundreds of them, iron-shod, set rim to rim up the vault to a hub the size of a house. Round the floor the water stood in a ring as wide as the mill-pond, boiling white with a sound like a crowd, and the steam came back down as hot rain. Lantern-moths flew through it, little paper lanterns on soft grey wings, leaving black marks where they touched the brick.

On an island of brick in the middle stood the Ox, and it made the Peelman look like a coat on a peg.

It had been four oxen. You could still see where. One great body of hollow brass, polished in places and green in others; one head with four pairs of horns set round it like a crown; too many legs, not moving together. The tithe yoke was still on its neck with chains trailing, and behind its brass ribs Pip could see the fire, a furnace in its belly, and every time it breathed out the ring boiled harder.

She remembered the Stroud twins on the lead ox's back, kicking their heels. She remembered being five and patting its nose and it breathing on her hand, warm and grassy and enormous.

"Oh, you poor thing," Pip said, before she could stop herself, and the Ox turned all four pairs of horns toward her and bellowed.

It came across the island at them like a cart down a hill.

They scattered, and the Ox went between them hammering the brick, and turned at the water's edge slow as the real oxen had turned at the bottom of the ramp, and came back. Its breath went over Pip like an oven door opened in her face. She stabbed it on the way past, because she always stabbed things, and Gran's dagger skidded off the brass like a knife on a plate. Ash's arrows rang off.

High on the dome wall, between the wheel-ribs, there were sluice gates: four rusted iron doors, a channel under each running down to the floor, cold water dripping from them. Water from the floors above.

"Look at its back," Wren said from the stair. There was a grate between the Ox's shoulders where a yoke-pin should have gone, and the fire showing through it. "We get it under a gate and open it, and the water goes straight into its belly. Tobin, the key. Ash, go with him. Somebody has to—" She stopped.

"Somebody has to bring it," said Pip.

"Not you."

"Why not me?"

"Because you're—"

"Because I'm what? Small? It's a big cow, Wren, it can't turn. I can turn." She was already out on the brick, bouncing on her toes, because if she stood still she would think. "Tobin's slow and you're useless in a run. Go and open the gate." And she turned and shouted, "*Oi!* Cow!"

It came.

The trick was the same as with Gran: don't look at it. She looked at the cracks in the brick in front of her feet and ran, and when she felt the heat on her back she cut left, the way you dodge a goose, and the Ox went past in a rush of brass and steam. She ran under the first gate, waving her arms. Above her the sluice key went into its socket and the iron door shrieked. She waited until she could smell her own hair, and threw herself sideways.

A column of black water came off the end of the channel straight into the grate on the Ox's back. A shriek, a hiss as big as the dome, a bang, and steam that knocked the lantern-moths out of the air. When it thinned, there was a long jagged crack down the Ox's flank, and through it the fire, sulking.

"Again," said Wren.

The second time, it nearly had her.

Ash was crouched by the second channel with an arrow on the string, watching the Ox move with that interested face, the face he'd worn on the stair while the rat-king had her arm. She didn't decide to do it. She just found she was running his way, and when she cut aside at the last moment the Ox went straight on at Ash. He went backwards over the channel lip with a yell and the horns passed over him by a hand's breadth.

"Wanted to see how it moved," Pip shouted over her shoulder. "Didn't you?"

She was grinning. Then she wasn't, because the Ox had turned faster than anything that size should, and she wasn't quite out of the way.

It took her on the side of the neck, below the ear: a jet of steam from the cracked flank, thin as a finger. It didn't feel hot. It felt like being hit with a stick. Then it felt hot.

She was under the second gate and didn't know how. She heard herself screaming, and then the water came down and the Ox shrieked louder than she did, and that was something.

The third gate was easy. By then its cracks ran all over it like ice on a puddle, and when the water went into its back it simply stood and let it, the way the real oxen had stood in the rain. Tobin walked up to it with the axe, set his feet, and let the head fall into the longest crack.

The brass split from shoulder to haunch with a noise like a church bell dropped down a well. The Ox came apart into four brass oxen falling away from each other, and then they weren't oxen, they were cart wheels and yokes and loops of chain, a heap of old tithe harness steaming in a puddle.

The ring stopped boiling all at once. The lantern-moths came down and were paper.

Pip sat on the brick with her hand over her neck. Under her hand it had stopped hurting, which she knew from the larders was worse. Gran's dagger was in her other hand, and it was warm from inside, like a cat.

And she was at the feast gate in the rain, aged six, on Gran's knee under the gate arch with a sack over both their heads. Market day, wet and dark. People coming in with baskets and Gran tapping each one on the shoulder as they went past, tap, tap, the way she did every market day of her life, and saying the number under her breath. Pip had asked why. *Because you count everybody, chick. Every one. Somebody always wants not to be counted, and that's the one you want to know about.* Gran's breath smelled of peppermint. Gran's big hand was on Pip's knee, holding her still, in case she fell off into the mud.

"—Pip. Pip. You went funny. Your eyes went."

It was Tobin, kneeling in front of her. His face had black streaks down it from the steam. He was trying to get her hand away from her neck to look, and she wouldn't let him.

"Shut up," she said. She was crying, which was stupid, because it didn't even hurt any more. "Shut up, shut up. I'm fine."

He got her hand off in the end. He looked at her neck for a long time, and then he looked away.

"It'll scar," said Wren, from somewhere.

"Lovely," said Pip. "That's two. I'll be the best-looking girl in Hollowmere." Ash was standing at the edge of the channel, wet to the waist, looking at her. She made herself look back. "Sorry," she said. "I'd have shouted."

"When?" said Ash. "After it ate me?"

And then, to everybody's surprise, he laughed, and so did she, and it hurt her neck.

The chains in the heap under the dome all had the same small mark on every link. A hammer over a wavy line. Pip picked one up, and looked at it for a while, and put it in her pocket.

### 2.3

*The cistern dome, the side vault.*

The innkeeper was laid out at her own table, and the table was laid for a hundred and twenty.

The door in the dome wall had opened by itself when the Ox fell. Behind it a long warm vault held a trestle table like the ones in the square every autumn, a white cloth and a clean empty bowl at every place, and at the head of it a woman in a chair with a ladle in her hand.

He'd have known her anywhere. He had looked back at her across the trestles on the last night and she had been looking at the soup.

Marta Fenn sat with the ladle raised over a tin tureen, as if somebody had said her name just as she went to serve. Something had set over all of her, hair and apron and red hands and ladle: a thin yellow-white skin like the fat that sets on a pot of stock left overnight.

"Don't touch the soup," Wren said, quickly, behind him.

"I wasn't going to touch the soup."

"Nobody touch the soup."

He put his hand on Marta's shoulder. The skin was cool and waxy, and under it, faintly, there was warm. He found the place at her wrist where it was thinnest, where the ladle came out of her fist, and worked his thumbnail in, and it cracked and peeled away from her skin in one long curl, the way the fat comes off cold stock. The rest went more easily. It came off her face last. Her eyelids moved.

Marta Fenn sat up and put the ladle into the tureen, all one movement, as if she'd never stopped. Then she looked down into the tureen and took the ladle back out and laid it on the cloth, very carefully, and looked up.

She was forty or so, broad and quick, with her hair bundled up in a cloth and a face made for remembering who owed her. She looked at the four of them standing round her table in the warm vault. She looked at Ash, and at Wren with her bent staff, and at Pip with her hand still over her neck. She looked at Tobin longest.

He watched her count. She did it with her eyes, the way you count the places at a table, one, two, three, four. Then she did it again.

"Four," she said. "Where's the little one?"

Nobody answered.

"Hob's girl," Marta said. "Nell. She was with you. Where's the little one?"

"I don't know," said Tobin.

It came out easily. That was the thing he'd think about afterwards. He'd been saying *Da had her* for ten years, to himself and once to Wren's wall, and this came out the same way, flat and smooth, like a plank planed so often it had gone shiny.

Marta looked at him. It was the same look. Across the trestles, over the soup, the last night of the world: a woman watching a boy walk away from his crying sister, and not saying anything, and not looking away either.

"Is that so," she said.

"We don't know where anyone is," said Wren, too quickly. "We're finding them. We found Hollis. He's home. We found Dun Harrow. He's home too. We're—"

"Hollis." Marta's mouth went thin. "Course you found Hollis. First out of anything, Hollis." Then the rest of it reached her: the four of them, grown; the beard on Tobin; the way they stood with their weapons like people who'd been holding them a long time. "Home," she said. "What do you mean, home? How long?"

"Ten years," said Pip.

Marta didn't sit down, because she was already sitting. Tobin watched her put both hands flat on the table cloth. Red hands, a cook's hands, burn scars across the knuckles from fifty years of oven doors.

"Abner," she said. "My husband. Abner Stroud. And my boys, my two boys, they were—have you got them? Have you found them?"

"Not yet," said Wren.

"Yet." Marta said it the way Hollis had said it, as if she were biting the word to see if it was real coin.

She looked down the long table at all the empty bowls.

"I made enough for everyone," she said. "That year. I always make enough. You'd be amazed what people put away at a feast." She smoothed the cloth with her palm. "Nobody went short at my table. Not one."

The warm came then, the long breath. There was a vent in the vault ceiling over the head of the table, a round black hole like a chimney, and the air went up it, and the cloth on the table lifted and fluttered, and Marta Fenn began to rise out of her chair.

She grabbed. She didn't grab the table, or the chair, or Wren, who was nearest. She reached past Wren and got Tobin by the wrist, and held on, and pulled him half across the corner of the table toward her, and put her mouth by his ear.

"She had her supper," Marta said. "Your Nell. I saw to that. She was crying, and I sat her down and gave her her plate myself. I didn't let her go hungry."

Then she let go of him and went up.

She went up fast and turning, her apron flapping, her cloth coming off her hair, and halfway up the shaft she seemed to remember something and started shouting it down at them, furious. "The inn! Across from the fountain! You hear? Bakery close, but *not next door*! Not next door to that man, I'll not have him in my kitchen window—"

The shaft closed on the rest. The cloth on the table settled back down. A dozen bowls had blown off the end and lay broken on the floor.

Pip let out a long breath. "Well," she said. "She's not changed."

"What did she say to you?" said Wren.

"Nothing," said Tobin. "Where the inn goes."

He stood looking at the closed shaft. He was thinking about the word *supper*, and a blue dress at the end of a long table, and a woman with a ladle who had seen a crying child and done the kind thing. The kind thing. He didn't know yet why the word felt like a hand round his throat. He only knew that it did, and that his wrist still hurt where she'd held it.

### 2.4

*The cistern dome, the bank room.*

There was a room behind the side vault where the red didn't reach, and Wren knew before they were through the door what it was for.

It was a counting room. A tithe-reeve's room, she thought, from the look of it: whitewashed walls and an iron-bound door, a high desk with a stool and a slate on it, and chalk tallies all over the walls in the same hand as the beams on the first floor, little gates of four strokes and a slash, rows and rows. Abner Stroud's hand. There were shelves along one wall with iron strongboxes on them, dozens of them, each with a hasp and no lock. In the corner there was a hearth, and in the hearth a fire was burning, and the fire was yellow.

Plain yellow. Wood fire. It was the first light in the whole Undercroft that wasn't the red, and all four of them stopped in the doorway and looked at it the way you look at a face you know in a crowd.

Under the hearth's arch, set into the floor, there was a stone basin full of water. Ash knelt and put his fingers in it, and took them out, and put his whole face in, and came up gasping.

"It's cold," he said. "It's cold. It's clean."

They drank. It tasted of stone and well, and it went down into her like going home. When she sat back her hands had stopped shaking, and the ache in her arm from the bakery backlash was gone. Pip's burn was still there below her ear, but it had a shiny skin on it now, as if it were a week old instead of an hour.

There was writing on the inside of the lintel, in the script. Four letters; Wren knew three. "*What is put by,*" she said, "*is kept.*"

Ash worked out the rest, because Ash would put anything into anything to see what happened. He put a spare bowstring into a strongbox and shut the lid, and the hasp turned over by itself, and after that the box wouldn't open for him. Not stuck. It simply wasn't going to. "It's keeping it," he said. "For us. Up there. Whatever happens."

The deep was full of things, as if the town's pockets had been turned out into it. Pip had a pair of old boots off a rack on the fourth floor, worn soft and stitched with red, which she said made her faster and nobody believed until she ran round the room in them twice. Tobin had a purse of old Vale silver from a hollow cask. Each of them stood in front of the shelves and chose, and it was a stupid small thing to have to choose. Pip held the boots against her chest a moment, then put them in. "I've never had new boots. Not since." The hasp went over. "There. Now I've got something to go home to."

In the far corner, in a square shaft through the ceiling, hung a cage of iron bars on a chain, an old mine hoist, big enough for four. The chain went up into the dark, and at the top of the dark, a long way off, there was a little grey square that might have been sky.

"That's home," said Pip.

Across from it the other door stood open on a stair going down, and up the stair came a dry oven heat and a smell of coal and, under that, roasting meat.

And strung across the ceiling between the two doors, back and forth like washing, there were lanterns.

Wren had seen them coming in and not looked, the way you don't look at a thing you're afraid of until you've drunk your water. Feast lanterns, tin and paper, every one lit, and at the end of the line by the hoist, three hanging dark.

She thought of the lantern at the top of the stair on the fourth floor, the one Ash had looked at and not mentioned. She'd seen him not mention it.

She counted the way her mother had taught her to count stock, along the line and back, in fives. The others were arguing behind her by then and she didn't listen.

Eighty-nine lit. Three dark.

Hollis. Dun. Marta. Three.

Ninety-two.

She did the sum again, though she had done it right the first time; she always did it right the first time. A hundred and twenty names on her wall, and one that shouldn't be there. Take the three. A hundred and seventeen.

Eighty-nine.

Twenty-eight lanterns short.

She stood under the line with her face turned up, and the feeling that went through her was not fear. She'd been afraid since the church. This was the feeling she'd had at eight, at the fourth doorstep on Mill Lane, when she found she couldn't bring her mother's face round to look at her. Something left out. Something that should have been there.

Twenty-eight people who weren't in the Undercroft at all.

"—so I'm saying it. Somebody has to say it." Pip was on her feet by the fire. "Three out. Three. Hollis and Dun and Marta, and they're up there now, safe, and we can go up that chain and *be* there. Tonight. We go home, we get some of Hollis's bread in us that isn't Tobin's—sorry, Tobin—and we come back down with our heads on. Gran used to say you leave the alehouse while you can still walk."

"Your gran never set foot in an alehouse," said Ash.

"That's how I *know* she's right."

"My father's down there," said Tobin. He was standing by the iron door with his back to the heat, and he hadn't looked at the hoist once. "If we go up, we come back down cold. Floor one. We do it all again."

"Then we do it all again!"

"With what? The Ox doesn't get up twice. Wren—" He turned. "Wren. Tell her."

They were all looking at her now. Ash too, leaning on the desk, with his face doing nothing, which meant he'd already decided and wanted to see what she'd say.

She could tell them. She could count it out loud in fives and watch them understand that a quarter of Hollowmere wasn't under Hollowmere at all, and then they would look at her and wait for a plan, and she didn't have one. And they'd go home, because Pip was frightened and the hoist was right there, and she'd stand in front of her wall tonight painting three ticks and knowing about the twenty-eight, alone, with nothing to do about it.

"We go on," Wren said.

"Course we do," said Pip. "Course we do. You've decided."

"Pip—"

"No, you've decided, that's fine. That's how it goes. You decide and Tobin carries it and Ash does whatever he was going to do anyway, and I go along. That's fine." Pip picked up her buckler. Her hands were shaking again, and she hated it, Wren could see her hating it. "Don't ask me next time. That's all. Don't stand there asking me like it's a vote."

She walked through the iron door and onto the stair first, which none of them had expected, and the heat came up round her.

Wren went last. As she went through the door she heard it behind her: the iron door swinging shut on its own, slow and heavy, and the latch going down. And then, through it, faintly, a long rattling run of chain from the corner where the hoist had been, faster and faster, and a crash a very long way down.

Nobody looked back. She thought: that's the first thing I've hidden from them down here. Then, being honest, because she tried to be honest in her own head at least: no. That's the second.

### 2.5

*The Undercroft, floor 6: the coal cellars.*

The thing about heat, Ash found, was that it got into your thinking.

The sixth floor was coal cellars, long and low and furred with soot, coal heaped to the roof and chutes pouring more in, a black whispering trickle that never stopped. Ovens were set in the walls between, bread ovens you could have walked into without ducking. Flues ran everywhere with dampers in them, and when you pushed one in, the heat in that cellar dropped and the heat in the next one roared.

He'd stopped sweating after the first hour. He thought that was probably bad.

Tobin lifted a smith's bellows off a hook and pumped it once, and the coals in the nearest oven went from red to white, and something inside screamed. A kettle came out on three short legs, squat and furious, steam shrieking from its spout. Ash shot it through the lid and it went off like a cork.

Those were the kettle-imps. The spitjacks were worse: turnspits that ran on their skewers, six sharp legs each, *tik-tik-tik* like a crab, going for the backs of your knees. Scuttlers lay in the coal heaps until you were close and then opened ember mouths. And in the flues overhead something long and black crawled across a flue mouth like a hand across a window, and when Ash looked again there was only soot coming down.

The spitjacks made him miss. One got under his guard and put a skewer through his boot into his calf, and he fell over it and lay in the coal dust stabbing at it with the hunting sword until it stopped.

When it stopped, the bow in his other hand was warm.

And he was in the bracken on the high slope in early autumn, five years old, with dew soaking his knees. Jory was fourteen and kneeling a little way off with the yew bow on the ground beside him, and in front of Jory, in the bracken, lay a hare. Its back legs were still going. Jory had his hands on his thighs and his head down, and as Ash watched he leaned over and was sick into the fern, twice, and wiped his mouth on his sleeve. *Don't tell Da,* Jory said, without looking round. *First time. You'll be sick too, your first.* Ash walked over and squatted beside the hare. Its eye was brown and wet and very bright, and he watched the brightness go out of it, and it was the most interesting thing he had ever seen.

He was lying in the coal dust with a dead spitjack on his leg.

He had never been sick. Not the first time. Not any time. He'd forgotten that Jory was, and he'd forgotten that he'd wondered, aged five, why his brother was sick and he wasn't, and decided that it must be because he was the one who'd turned out wrong.

"Ash? Your eyes went."

"I'm fine," he said. He got up. His calf was bleeding into his boot. The bow felt lighter, the way Tobin had said his axe felt lighter in the larders, and he didn't like it any more than Tobin had.

In the next cellar feast banners hung on the soot-black walls, scorched, and under the burns their colours were still bright, the colour gone out of the ones in the square. Beside them stood rows of roasting spits, the long kind for the feast pigs, empty, dripping grease.

"Someone's cooking," said Pip.

"Someone's always cooking," said Ash. "It's a kitchen. The whole thing's a kitchen."

"Then where's the food?"

He didn't answer. At the far end of the cellar, in the mouth of the biggest oven, something was standing.

He saw it a breath before the others, because he was the one who looked. It stood just inside the oven door, in the glow, taller than Tobin, and it wore a face of plaited straw: a wheat-sheaf mask like the ones the children made for scarecrows at harvest, the ears fanned round the head like a mane. Two black horns had grown through the straw at the temples. Below the mask it was scorched hide split with ember cracks, like the jars and the rat-kings, but it stood like a person. Like a tired farmhand at the end of a day. It had a sickle on its belt.

In its hands it held a plate with a slice of feast pie on it, pork and apple, the steam going up in a thin straight line. Ash could smell it from where he stood. It smelled of every autumn before he was nine, of Jory's hand on his hair and his mother laughing at the next table.

It held the plate out. Not threatening. Offering. Here. You must be starving.

He found he had taken a step. Then another.

He wasn't hungry. That was what he would remember. He was curious. He wanted to know what was under the straw, what it would do if he took the plate, what the pie would taste like after ten years warm. He wanted it so badly it felt like hunger, and he couldn't tell the difference, and that was interesting too.

"Ash." Pip had his belt. She'd got hold of the back of it with both hands, the way he'd held Tobin's in the bakery. "Ash. Don't you dare. Don't you *dare*."

The masked thing watched them. Then, from behind the straw, it whistled.

Three notes, falling, and then a little run going up. A tune. It went round the cellar and up the flues, cheerful and thin, a carter's sort of tune, the kind of thing you whistle walking beside a beast to keep it going. Ash didn't know it. Pip, behind him, went very still, as if she nearly did.

The figure bent and set the plate down on the floor of the oven mouth, carefully, like setting out a bowl for a cat. Then it stepped backwards into the glow, and the glow folded round it, and it was gone.

They stood there a long time. The pie steamed.

"What," said Tobin, "was that."

"Somebody," said Ash. "That was somebody."

"It had *horns*."

"It stood like somebody." He couldn't explain it. "It stood like a man who's tired."

He went and crouched over the pie in the end, with the others shouting at him to leave it. Every flake of crust was whole. There wasn't a speck of soot on it, in a cellar where soot lay on everything like snow.

He knelt there and wanted it, and then he stood up and walked away, and that was harder than anything he'd done on the stair with the rat-king, and nobody would ever know.

Behind him, as they went on, Pip was humming. Three notes falling, and a little run going up. She didn't seem to know she was doing it.

### 2.6

*The Undercroft, floor 7: the flues.*

The chairs were the worst thing Pip had seen down here, and nothing was even happening to them.

They stood in a row before an oven at the end of a long brick room. Children's chairs: the stools and little ladder-backs every family in Hollowmere had carried down to the square so the children could sit at the end of the long tables. A low table in front of them with a cloth, a small plate at every place, and a cup. The oven behind was the biggest yet, its iron door as high as a house, and the door was shut. Heat came off it in waves you could see.

Pip counted, because Gran counted. Twenty-four chairs.

Tobin walked down the row. At the end one chair had been knocked over, and he set it upright and pushed it in to the table, the way you'd tidy after a meal, and stood with his hand on the back of it.

"There's no children," Pip said. "It's all right. Tobin. There's nobody."

"I know."

At the stair head there had been a feast lantern on a hook, lit. Ash had pointed at it this time. Wren had looked at it a long while and said, "Somebody's on this floor." And then, slowly, as if she'd been carrying it since the counting room and had to set it down: "One lantern for each. In the bank too. One for every one of them still down here."

So they searched. Coal cellars, oven rooms, flue stairs, calling, and the heat got worse, and the beat under the floor got louder. Not faster. Louder. Pip felt it through the soles of her new boots.

The masks came in the room with the spits.

There were three, and these didn't offer anything. They came out of the flues headfirst, the way the soot-shapes came, and dropped into a crouch, straw faces turning, and one was behind Pip before she'd got her buckler round.

It was very strong. That was what she'd remember. An arm like a cart-trace across her chest, pinning her arms, lifting her off her feet, and the smell of it: scorched straw and hot hide and, under that, apples. Its other hand came round in front of her face with a wooden spoon in it, heaped with something dark and sticky. Feast honey cake. It came at her mouth.

It wasn't stabbing her. It was *feeding* her. It was patient about it, the way you'd feed a child who won't open: nudging the spoon at her lips, and when she turned her face away, following.

She shut her mouth so hard her teeth hurt. Sticky warmth smeared across her lips and up her cheek. She couldn't get her arms free. She couldn't breathe for the honey smell, and she thought, quite clearly, *if I open my mouth to scream, it'll go in*, and that was the most frightened she had ever been in her life.

So she didn't scream. She bit.

She got the thumb that held the spoon and bit down with everything she had, the way the rat-king had bitten her, and it wasn't flesh. It was straw and something like charcoal, dry, crunching, tasting of ash. The thing made no sound. Its arm loosened, and Tobin's axe came down through its shoulder from behind, and it came apart like a scarecrow in a bonfire.

Pip spat. She spat and wiped her mouth on her sleeve until her lips bled, and Wren was there with the flask. "Spit. Again. *Again*. Did any go in? Pip. Did you swallow?"

"I don't know!" Honey and ash and blood. "I don't *know*—"

Ash had shot one through the mask. The third had gone back up its flue. And at the far end of the room, by the door, a fourth was standing that hadn't moved at all.

He was bigger than the others. She thought *he* without deciding to. He stood with his feet apart and a long carter's whip coiled on his shoulder, looking at them one at a time, slowly, as if counting. Then he whistled. Three notes falling, a little run going up. The third mask came back down its flue and stood beside him, and in the walls others answered, not whistling. Scraping. A lot of them.

"Oh, *no*," said Pip.

The room was getting smaller.

The far wall came in the way dough comes in when you knock it back: soft, all over, the bricks sagging toward them. The ceiling came down a hand's breadth. Coal poured from every chute at once. The beat under the floor was so loud she could hear the bricks tick with it.

"It's closing," said Wren. "The floor's closing on us. The stair. *Now*."

"There's someone here," said Tobin.

"I know."

"The lantern's lit, Wren, we haven't—"

"*I know.*" Wren had him by the arm, half his size and hauling on him like a rope. "If we stay we're in the wall with them. All of us. Do you want to be in the wall?"

He looked back, past the masked man and the closing wall, down the long corridor toward the room with the small chairs. Pip had never seen a face want something that much and not take it.

The masked man didn't come after them. He watched them go with his whip on his shoulder, while the walls came in round him as if he were standing in the middle of somebody's breath, and whistled softly.

The stair lantern was still lit when they passed it, one small steady flame, and Tobin looked at it until the stair turned and took it away.

Pip didn't look at him. She had honey on her face that wouldn't come off, and somewhere on the way down she noticed she was humming that tune, and made herself stop, and a few steps later found she'd started again.

### 2.7

*The Undercroft, floor 8: the feast hall.*

The colour was down here.

That was what Wren saw first. The banners hung from the roof beams of a hall as long as the square, wheat-gold and berry-red, bright as the morning they were dyed: every colour drunk out of the ones in the square, ten years ago, in a single night. The square was here too, folded into a hall. Long trestle tables, benches, lantern lines overhead with every lantern lit, and between the tables, in iron baskets on poles, braziers. They burned red. They had been burning for ten years and the coals hadn't burned down.

And the food. Every place set, every plate full. Pork in cider with the skin crackled gold. Baked apples split and oozing. Hollis's dark loaves cut thick with the butter melting in. Pears in wine. Honey cake. It steamed. The gravy was still moving on the plates, very slightly, as if someone had only just poured it.

"Oh," said Pip, small. "I'd forgotten. I'd *forgotten*."

Fire barred the way to the high table: rows of braziers across the aisles and the flames between them going up in sheets. There were dampers on the flues over each row. Push one in and that row fell and the row two on roared to the beams. It was the sluices again, Wren thought. The dream had learned how they thought and built a house round it. Tobin worked the rods, Ash pumped the bellows into the rows they needed hot to draw the flame off the ones they needed cold, Wren called the order. She got it wrong once, and a sheet of fire came down the aisle and took the hair off Ash's forearm, and nobody said anything.

Halfway down, Pip reached for a pear.

Her hand just went out to the nearest plate the way it had at every feast of her childhood, the way a hand goes to a fire on a cold night. Wren slapped it away so hard her own palm stung.

"I wasn't," Pip said, staring at her hand. "I wasn't, Wren, I didn't even—"

"I know." Wren's heart was going like a bird. "Don't."

Behind the seven carved chairs at the high table, hung from the beams like the biggest banner of all, there was a page.

It was as tall as a house and as wide as the table, yellow and stiff, and covered in names. Line after line in plain black ink, in the hooked letters of the script, in a small neat hand that Wren had known all her life before she let herself know it. Every name in Hollowmere, and only she could read them. And over every name, set on the ink like a wax seal on a letter, an ember. A little round coal, white at the heart, sitting on each name and burning, a hundred and more of them breathing brighter and dimmer together in time with the beat. Bright, dim.

At the head of the page were three lines in the script.

She read them aloud. She didn't decide to. They wanted saying, the way the last letter on the cat had, and her mouth went ahead of her. The words tasted of iron, and when she'd said them her lip was bleeding.

"*Hollowmere. The fiftieth table.*" Then: "*Here are written all who sit.*" Then, slowly, finding it: "*Who eats is bound, until the next table is laid.*"

A fourth line under it was half burned away. *Weighed.* *After.* A letter from the trapdoor that meant something like *owed*, or *kept back*. The rest was char.

"Bound," said Ash. "Bound to what?"

Nobody answered. "Read them," Ash said. "Wren. Read them out."

She read. Each name came easier than the last, a word of the contract learned and kept; she felt them go in and stay. She found the Calders for him: *Hester Calder*, an ember; *Jory Calder*, an ember. Then she went down to the bottom of the page, where the names changed.

The last block was the children. *And the children of the town*, in her mother's hand, house by house, and she read them aloud while the others watched the coals. The Sedge boy who'd played Elder Vane in a wool beard. The Pell twins. The two Stroud boys who'd kicked their heels on the lead ox. Every one with a coal on the name, breathing.

Except four. The others saw those before she reached them: four names with no coal at all, plain ink faded brown, like any old ink on any old page. She read them anyway. *Ash Calder.* *Wren Hale.* *Pip Ostrander.* *Tobin Marsh.*

And then, because it was the next line and her mouth was already going, the one beneath, with a coal on it as bright as any: *Nell Marsh.*

"We didn't eat," said Pip.

She said it the way you say a thing to see if it's true. Then louder. "We didn't eat. That's it. That's what it is. We weren't brave, it didn't *miss* us. We missed supper. We skived off before the meal and everybody else sat down and ate and they're *bound*, and we're—" She started to laugh. It was the worst sound Wren had ever heard her make. "We're alive because we were *naughty*."

Ash asked which was his. Wren pointed. He stepped up onto the dais and touched it and drew his finger along it and held it there. His face did nothing. Then he put his hand in his pocket and said nothing at all.

Tobin was standing at the foot of the page looking at two lines. His own, cold and brown. His sister's under it, burning.

Wren saw his face, and then she couldn't look at it.

He turned and walked away down the hall, between the steaming plates and the braziers, toward the far stair. He didn't run. He walked the way he walked to the woodpile at night. Nobody called after him.

Wren stayed. She'd seen them while Pip was laughing. In the margin of the children's block, beside the four plain names, in the same plain ink: ticks. Small neat ticks like the ones her mother made down the side of her cost book when a thing was paid. Five, in a short row. The fifth had a line through it, one firm stroke, as if the hand had pressed hard.

Five ticks. Four names.

She knew the way the tick went down short and up long. She'd copied pages of it when she was seven.

She looked at them while the coals went bright, and dim, and bright. Then she made herself stop, the way she'd made herself stop crying in the tunnel. She didn't think about what five meant, or whose hand. She said to herself, in the plan voice, *Later*, and put it away and shut the lid and heard the hasp go over.

Then she went after Tobin, because somebody had to, and because he was the one person in the hall she was sure hadn't seen.

### 2.8

*The Undercroft, floor 9: the root wall.*

*She had her supper. I saw to that.*

Tobin carried it down the stair the way he'd carried the axe on the first night, too heavy, banging against his legs.

Marta had sat Nell down and given her her plate. Nell had eaten, because she was five and crying and somebody kind had given her a plate, and she was bound. And if he'd held on to her sleeve, if he'd carried her and the axe both the way he'd known he'd have to, her name would be cold brown ink on that page, next to his.

*Take your sister.* Da had said it three times. Da had crouched, which he never did.

He didn't let himself think about why. He thought about it anyway.

The ninth floor was under the town. Crypts first, stone shelves and stone boxes, and then foundations: the undersides of things. He looked up and saw the bottom of a doorstep in the ceiling, square and worn, with a mason's mark. Then another. They were walking under the lanes, every doorstep in Hollowmere seen from below.

And through all of it the roots, out of floor and ceiling and wall, as thick as a man's leg and then as thick as a man, black bark split with red, pulsing slow in time with the beat. Things lived in them. Root-crawlers, all knuckles. Lean hounds with ribs like toast racks and ember eyes, that came in threes and went for the throat. The first one Tobin killed fell against a root, and the root drank it.

The wall was at the end of a long crypt: roots grown so thick and close there was no way through, all twisted round one place in the middle the way wood twists round a knot.

His father was the knot.

Hob Marsh stood upright in the wall with the roots grown round him and into him, through his sleeves and his collar, one up through the back of his coat and out at the shoulder. His eyes were shut. His big split hands hung open with roots wound round the fingers like twine.

His mouth was moving. No sound. The same few shapes, over and over. Tobin stood in front of him and couldn't read them, and would have given his right hand to.

"Da," he said.

"Tobin," Wren said behind him. "Don't."

He shifted his grip until the old scar across his palm sat on the haft where it always sat, and set the edge against the root through his father's collar, light, the way you score a loaf. He knew wood. He pressed.

The root bled. Red light came out of it, thick, like the inside of a hand held over a candle, and ran down the bark like sap. His father's face twisted, eyes still shut, the way a sleeping man's face twists when you pull a splinter.

"Not yet," said Wren. "We can't. *Not yet.* It's all through him, you'll—"

"I know."

"Then *stop*—"

He swung. A real swing, from the hip, the head doing the work.

The axe went in a hand deep. Red light sprayed over him, hot as blood and weighing nothing. His father's mouth opened. The whole wall shuddered, and under the floor the beat stumbled, missed, and came back faster. Every root in the crypt shrieked like a door opened in winter, and from every gap in every wall came the scraping.

"Oh, well *done*," said Pip.

They came out of the roots like rats out of a rick, straw masks and horns and sickles, dozens, fast and low, and they weren't trying to cut anybody. They were trying to hold them. Tobin saw a slice of roast pork, a buttered heel of bread, a baked apple held out in scorched hands as they came. One got Ash by the hair and knelt on his chest and pushed crackling at his mouth while he heaved, and Pip went over the top of it like a terrier and put Gran's dagger through the straw where an ear would be.

The big one with the whip stood on a stone coffin at the back of the crypt and whistled, and the masks moved to it like dogs to a shepherd. He didn't come down. He watched Tobin. Tobin felt it through the straw the whole way across the crypt.

They ran. Tobin went last, and every time he swung the axe went round in the long curve and came back to his shoulder, and every blow he took went up the haft into his teeth. A sickle opened his forearm. A hound got his boot. At the stair he turned, and his father was still in the wall behind all of it with his mouth moving and the cut root bleeding light down the bark.

He'd done that, and he'd known what it would do.

At the bottom of the stair Wren sat down on the last step, white to the lips.

"You did that," she said.

"I know."

"You *knew*."

"I know," he said, and sat beside her. Above them the scraping died away. Below them, very close, something tolled.

### 2.9

*The Undercroft, floor 10: the bell chamber.*

Ash felt it in his teeth before he heard it, the way he had at the end of the tunnel ten years ago.

The chamber was the bottom of a shaft so tall its top was lost, and he knew where it went because he'd stood under it from the other end every Sunday of his childhood. They were under the church tower. The floor was rings: great rings of old oak, one inside the next, each a stride wide, with gaps between going down into red light, and they turned, slowly, some one way and some the other, grinding like millstones.

They were the bell-frame. The headstock and the cross-timbers that had held the bell in the tower, pulled out long and bent into circles. There were still pegs in them. There were joints.

"That's Da's," Tobin said. "He cut those."

The bell hung in the middle, from nothing, over the innermost ring. The great bell of Hollowmere, that every child in town had been held up to see on blessing day, green-black now and furred with soot, its bronze cracked with red like the Ox's brass. Its mouth was turned toward them. Inside, where the clapper should have been, there was dark, and something in the dark moved.

It swung back and up, as if a ringer far above had pulled the rope, and at the top of the swing it tolled.

The sound went out across the chamber in a ring, like a ripple across a pond, and where it passed the world slowed.

Ash was in the middle of a step, and his foot came down like a foot coming down through honey. Dust falling from the vault stopped and hung. Pip turned her head toward him the way the hand of a clock turns. The note went on and on in the bones of his jaw, and while it lasted nothing hurried. Then it faded and everything snapped back, and Pip finished turning and said, "*What* was—"

It swung again.

They learned, because they had to. You moved between the tolls: saw it go back, ran, got where you were going before it reached the top. When it tolled you were caught wherever you stood, and things came at you through the slow, root-crawlers out of the gaps, lantern-moths, a spitjack. They were slow too, but they'd been moving when the toll came. Ash got caught mid-jump over a gap and hung above the red light the whole length of a toll, falling so slowly he could count the pegs in the oak below, and landed a hand's width from the edge.

His arrows went into the soot on the bell and stuck like pins in a cushion.

"The headstock!" Tobin shouted. "Look *up*—"

High in the shaft Ash could just see it: a great beam across the well, and the bell hanging from it on iron straps. You'd have to climb the rings where they rose against the wall, between tolls. Nobody could do it in one breath.

Then the bell stopped swinging, and hung still, and began to toll the names.

He didn't know how he knew. It tolled where it hung, slow and even, the way Amos Gill used to toll for a funeral, and every stroke had someone in it. He couldn't hear the names. He felt them. And each stroke slowed the world a little more and didn't let go before the next came, so they sank deeper with every name, like stones into mud.

He counted. He couldn't help it. Twenty. Forty. It took him a breath to move his hand an inch. Sixty. Across the chamber Wren was writing a mark one letter between one toll and the next, her cracked lantern hanging stopped on its bent hook, the gold thread crawling out of the brass foot like a snail. Ninety. Pip's mouth was opening as slowly as a flower. A hundred.

A hundred and twenty. He knew, because the next stroke didn't come.

Everything snapped back so fast that Pip fell over.

And the bell swung again, all the way up, as if to toll, and made no sound.

Back down. Silent. Up again. Silent. Four great swings, the bronze mouth turned toward them at the top of each like a face turned to listen. Then it hung at the bottom of its swing, and waited.

Ash understood, and it went through him colder than anything in the Undercroft. It had rung the whole table, and come to the four places left, and it was asking. Like Bram Tolley at the schoolhouse. Like Odile at the gate. *Ash Calder?*

It was so quiet he could hear the rings turn.

"*Not here!*"

Pip was on her feet in the second ring with her buckler up and her face streaked with honey and soot, and she shouted it the way she used to shout at school when Bram called her name and she was standing right in front of him. "Pip Ostrander, *not here!* Never was! Mark me absent, you great tin *pot*—"

The bell trembled, and in the silence nothing slowed.

Tobin saw it too. He was already running up the rings where they rose against the wall, jumping the gaps, climbing the bent frame his father had cut, and the bell hung trembling and didn't ring, because nobody had answered it.

"Wren," said Ash. "Now."

Wren finished her mark. The gold went off the staff and across the chamber into the dark inside the bell's mouth, and the dark caught like tinder.

Tobin reached the headstock. Ash saw him up there, standing on the beam, looking down for half a breath at the joint where the strap was pinned into the oak. His father's joint. Then he let the head fall.

The beam split. The bell dropped the height of the shaft and broke the centre ring and struck the stone beneath with its rim, and it rang.

Not a toll. The real voice, the one Ash had heard every Sunday and every feast: a big round warm note so loud it was a place you stood in, so loud it shook the soot off the bronze in sheets. The red cracks went dark. Under them the bell was green and gold, with its blessing cast round its shoulder in plain letters anyone could read.

The rings stopped turning. Far under everything, Ash felt the slow beat miss, once, as if something very large had heard a sound it didn't like in its sleep.

He didn't say so.

Tobin came down off the wall with his hand over his left ear and blood between his fingers, and didn't seem to notice.

Under the bell's lip, curled on the broken stone, lay an old man with a bell rope wound round both wrists. Amos Gill opened his eyes and said, in the loud flat voice of a man who hasn't heard himself in years, "Is it evening? I'm late. I've to ring them out."

### 2.10

*The tithe ramp, then the square. Dawn.*

The deep breathed the bell home. Pip watched it go: the warm came up from under the broken rings and the bell rose off the stone, slow as a cloud, with Amos Gill clinging to the rope beneath it and shouting at them to mind his frame. It went up the shaft into the dark and was gone, and a long time later, from very high, they heard it settle into the tower with a sound like a cart backing into a barn.

The far wall of the chamber had opened. Behind it a ramp went up, broad and stone, with two worn ruts down it a cart's width apart.

"That's the tithe road," said Tobin. "That's the ramp. That goes to the square."

It went up through the roots. Halfway, the ramp ran through the top of the root wall, and they could see it from behind: the great knot, and a pair of boots hanging in it, and the cut root still bleeding light. Tobin stopped under it.

Then the bell rang over Hollowmere.

Pip felt it before she heard it, through the stone, through her boots, up into her teeth. Amos Gill, who couldn't hear a thing, was ringing it the way he'd rung the feast in for forty years, with his whole weight on the rope, and it rang and rang. The ramp shook. Dust came down in sheets. And then the shaking didn't stop when the ringing did. It went on, deeper, bigger, the way a cart keeps going down a hill after the horse has stopped. The whole hill moved. Pip sat down hard on the ramp.

Above them the roots let go.

They slackened all at once, like a hand opening, and Hob Marsh fell out of the wall. Tobin caught him. He went down on one knee under the weight and didn't drop him. Then he got up with his father across his arms the way you'd carry a child out of a fire, and walked up the ramp.

The iron gate at the top had cracked down the middle ten years ago. It swung open as they came to it, both halves, slowly, on its own.

The square was grey with dawn, and shaking. Pip saw the lake shiver from one shore to the other. Birds went up off every tree in the valley at once, every rook and pigeon in the Vale, a black cloud turning. A slate slid off the church roof and broke on the cobbles. High on the mountain a long white tongue of snow came loose from a shoulder of rock and poured down, soundless at that distance. And in the tower, the bell was still swinging, though Amos had stopped.

Then it was still.

There was smoke coming from Tobin's old clay oven against the church wall, and bread on a board beside it, and Hollis Rye standing there in his apron with his mouth open. Marta Fenn was by the dry fountain. Dun Harrow sat on a doorstep with a jar between his feet.

Tobin laid his father down on the cobbles in the middle of the square, where the long tables had been.

Hob Marsh opened his eyes. He looked up at the sky, and at the tower, and at the big bearded man kneeling over him, and he knew him. Pip saw him know him. Then his eyes went past Tobin, round the square, looking for someone smaller.

"Where's Nell?" he said.

Pip watched Tobin. She'd been watching Tobin lie for ten years, and she knew every way he did it, and she waited for one of them.

"She's still down there," said Tobin.

It wasn't a lie. She almost wished it had been.

Hob looked at his son for a long time. Then he got up off the cobbles, slowly, an old man's getting-up in a forty-one-year-old body, and walked out of the square toward the mill road where the timber yard had been. He didn't look back. Nobody went after him.

By the fountain Marta Fenn was looking at Tobin. Pip looked at Marta looking, and put it away.

### 2.11

*Somewhere far up the mountain. The same dawn.*

The sound came a long way through rock, and when it arrived it was very small. A bell.

A man stood in the dark where he had stood for ten years. He had not sat down. He had not slept; he could not. His eyes had been shut the whole time, and he had been awake behind them, counting nothing, the way a clock stopped at an hour goes on being that hour.

The bell came through the stone. Then the stone moved, under his feet and over his head, a long slow shudder like something enormous settling in its bed.

He opened his eyes.

It was cold. He noticed the cold first, and then the dark, and then, by habit, he put his hand to his chest and waited, and felt nothing there, as he had felt nothing for sixty years.

Beside him, someone slept on.

He listened until the bell was gone. Then he said, in the dark, to no one, in the courteous voice of a man greeting late guests, "Four places."

### 2.12

*Wren's house. That night.*

Wren painted five ticks.

*Hollis Rye. Dun Harrow. Marta Fenn. Amos Gill. Hob Marsh.* She did them small and neat with the soot paint and her hair brush, the way her mother ticked the cost book, and when she stood back the wall had five ticks on it, and she looked at them and didn't think about anything at all.

Tuppence sat on the table with his fur up and wouldn't be stroked.

"Well, I'll say it," said Pip. She was on the hearth with her knees up and a slab of Hollis's bread, and her burn shone in the firelight. "Somebody has to. We're alive because we were naughty. That's the whole of it. All these years I thought the gods liked me. Turns out they just like children who skip supper."

"That's not funny," said Wren.

"It's a bit funny."

"It said *bound*," said Ash. He was at the window with his back to them. "Who eats is bound. It's a contract. That's what the feast was, every year. Everyone in that square sat down and signed for themselves with the first bite and called it supper." He turned round. "Every one of them. Fifty years. They watered the grain and hid barley in the wine casks and sat down and signed."

"Don't," said Wren.

"Your mother wrote the page, Wren. That's her hand. You knew it as soon as—"

"*Don't.*"

"My father," said Tobin, very quietly, "is in that wall."

"Your father," said Ash, "sat down and ate."

Tobin had him by the collar before anyone saw him get up. It was the first winter again, the church door, the brace of hares. Ash didn't fight. He looked at Tobin with that face, interested, and waited.

Tobin let go. His hand was shaking.

"It means it can't have us," Wren said. She heard the plan voice come up in her like water in a well, and let it. "That's what it means. We're not on its list. We're the only people in the Vale it can't take. We go down, we bring them up. That's all it means."

"It means it wants us more than anything," said Ash. "We're four empty seats. You heard the bell."

"And *you*." Pip pointed her bread at Wren. "Reading it out. Like a hymn. Like you'd been practising."

"I have been practising."

"I know. I know you have. That's what I'm *saying*. You woke it with that writing, and now you can read its menu." Pip's voice went high and thin, the way it had in the storm. "What else does it say, Wren? What's the bit at the bottom that got burned off? Did you read that too?"

Wren looked at the wall. Five ticks, one row. She thought of a firm straight stroke through the fifth.

"No," she said.

Tobin picked up his axe from beside the door.

"Where are you going?" said Pip.

"To see Da," said Tobin, and went out.

### 2.13

*The timber yard on the mill road. Later that night.*

His father was sitting on the bare foundation of the yard with a bottle of the church's altar wine, which Dun had found and Hob had not given back.

Tobin sat down on the step. The yard was a hand-high wall of stones round a square of nettles. In the moonlight you could see where the saw-pit had been, and the loft posts.

"Ten years," Hob said.

"Ten."

"You've got my axe."

"You gave it me."

"Keep it." Hob drank. "You hold it wrong."

They sat. An owl went over. Tobin's left ear was still ringing from the bell, a high thin note that wouldn't go.

"I told you to take her," Hob said.

"I know."

"I told you three times. I got down and told you."

Tobin looked at the nettles. He had been carrying this stair-step by stair-step since the feast hall, and he had thought, coming up the mill road, that he might set it down here. That this was the place.

"She wouldn't keep up," he said. "At the orchard gate. I turned round and she'd gone. Back to the square, I thought. Back to you. I looked." He heard how smooth it was. "I looked, Da."

Hob was quiet for a long time.

"She'd wander," he said at last. "Her mother wandered. Couldn't keep either of them on a path." He drank again, and wiped his mouth, and his big split hand was shaking on the bottle, and Tobin understood that his father believed him, and that it had cost nothing at all.

"Why did you want us out?" he said. "Out of the square. You and Odile and Jory and Wren's mother. Everybody's mad family with their swords. Why?"

His father looked at him. It was the look from the tithe ramp: measuring him for a joist.

"Not mine to tell," said Hob Marsh.

"Then whose?"

Hob didn't answer. After a while he said, "I'll build you a house. A proper one. Yours is a disgrace, I saw it coming up. Yours and Odile's girl's and that Calder boy's. Hale's daughter's is the only one with a straight wall." He held out the bottle.

Tobin took it. He'd never drunk with his father. He'd been ten.

### 2.14

*Hollowmere, then the Undercroft, floor 7. Six weeks on.*

The square had an inn now, or the frame of one, across from the fountain. The bakery stood by the well, facing east, close to the inn but not next door. Hob had raised both. He was up every dawn in the timber yard with the saw going, and by the second week he was drinking again in the evenings with Dun, and by the fourth nobody mentioned it.

Pip was carrying plates across to the inn frame for Marta when she caught herself humming. Three notes falling, a little run going up.

Behind her a tray hit the cobbles.

Marta Fenn was standing in the inn's half-built doorway with broken crockery round her feet and her face gone the colour of the banners. "Where did you get that?" she said. "That tune. Where did you hear it?"

"I don't know," said Pip. "Somewhere. It's just a tune."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Then she bent and picked up the pieces, and didn't ask again, and didn't say anything else to Pip all that day.

They went down the next morning by the tithe gate. It opened onto the counting room now, straight down the ramp to the bank, the fire yellow in its hearth, the lantern line over their heads with half its lanterns dark. Four dives in six weeks had brought up Merrin Brook, who wanted to live next to her sister and wouldn't speak to her, and the schoolmaster, and the miller, and the lamp-keeper, and a dozen more. Every dive the floors were different. Every dive the seventh floor's stair lantern was still lit.

This time the coal bin was in the room with the chairs.

A different room, different chairs, the same twenty-four. And at the end of the row, where Tobin had set the fallen chair upright six weeks ago on some other floor, there was a coal bin, iron, the kind with a sloped lid. The lid was open. In the coal, asleep, black with dust from her hair to her shoes, lay a little girl in a dress that had been blue.

Tobin's hands shook. Pip saw them. He knelt by the bin and lifted his sister out, and the coal ran off her in a black stream, and she was warm and limp and five years old.

Nell opened her eyes.

She looked at him for a long time, this big bearded man with an axe on his back. Pip saw her know him, the way Hob had.

"You left me," Nell said.

Not angry. As if she were telling him the weather.

"I came back," Tobin said. "I came back for you, after. You'd gone. I looked all night."

Pip had been with him all that night, at the end of the founders' tunnel, with her head in his lap. He'd never come back. None of them had.

Nell considered this. "You said you'd bring me a treasure."

Tobin felt in his coat. He took out a curl of bronze as long as a finger, shaved off the bell's rim by his axe, green on one side and gold on the other, and put it in her hand.

The warm came. The coal chute in the corner drew like a chimney, and Nell rose out of his arms, holding the bronze curl tight, and halfway up she remembered the important thing and shouted it down. "I want to live at *Tobin's*! Near the *cat*!"

The chute closed.

Tobin knelt by the empty coal bin. At the stair head, the lantern had gone out.

"You're a good liar," Pip said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud.

He didn't answer. She didn't say anything else, then or ever, and she put it away where Gran used to put the things she might need one day at the gate.

### 2.15

*Wren's house. Late autumn, PY 60.*

The last lantern on the line went dark a fortnight after Nell came home, when they carried up a grandmother from the sixth floor who had been asleep in a flue with her knitting. Wren stood in the counting room and looked up at the line, every lantern black, and the stair lanterns all black on every floor, and the Undercroft had nobody left in it.

That night she stood in front of her wall with the lamp.

Ninety-two ticks. She'd counted them twice.

Twenty-eight names without.

*Odile Ostrander. Jory Calder. Isolde Hale.* She saw those first, because she was looking for them, and they were the names she had been afraid of since the counting room. Then *Hester Calder*. Then *Abner Stroud*, Marta's husband, who wasn't anywhere.

And the children. Twenty-three of them. The Sedge boy who'd played Elder Vane. The Pell twins. The two Stroud boys. Every child in Hollowmere who'd sat at the end of the long tables on the little chairs, except one, and that one was asleep upstairs in Tobin's house with a cat on her feet and a piece of the bell in her fist. Not one of the others had been in the Undercroft at all.

And at the bottom of the Ostrander line, small, a first name only, that would never get a tick and never needed one.

She heard the door. The others had gone home an hour ago. Tobin came in and stood beside her, and looked at the wall, and she saw him find the twenty-three and stop.

"Where are they?" he said.

"I don't know."

He stood there a long time. Then he said, without looking at her, in the voice he used for things he'd carried too long to put down gently: "I left her."

Wren didn't move.

"At the trestles. She had my sleeve, and I pulled it out of her hand, and I told her to go and sit with Da. She was crying. I didn't look round. Da told me to take her, three times, and I didn't, because she was slow." He breathed. "I told him she wandered off. I told her I came back. I never came back."

The gap between her shoulder and his arm was about the width of a hand. It had been that width for six years.

She thought of five ticks in a short row in a margin, and a firm straight line through the fifth. She thought of her mother's hand, going down short and up long. She could tell him. She could say: there were five, Tobin. Somebody meant there to be five. She didn't know what it meant. She knew exactly what it meant.

She closed the gap. She put her shoulder against his arm and kept it there.

"I know," she said, though she hadn't, and it was the kindest lie she had, and she kept the other thing in its box, and heard the hasp go over.

Outside, for the first time in ten years, the mill wheel was turning on the stream, and in the timber yard Hob's saw had stopped for the night, and down at the end of the mill road, past the last doorstep, the road out of the valley lay white under the moon, open all the way to the sea.
