# Chapter 3: Short Weight

### 3.1

*Hollowmere, then the river road. The first frost, PY 60.*

The whole town came out to see them off, which in Hollowmere now meant ninety-one grown-ups, one small girl and a cat, and Pip could have screamed.

For ten years nobody but Tobin had minded where they went. Now there were people on every doorstep with their arms folded, watching them down the square the way you watch a cart you've loaded yourself, to see if anything falls off.

There was smoke from twenty-odd chimneys and the square smelled of Hollis's crust. Marta's inn had a roof now. Tansy and Merrin Brook were shouting across their gardens about a gooseberry bush, which Wren said was progress. Every dusk Amos Gill rang the bell, late and much too loud, and the whole town stood still until it was done, and nobody had agreed to that either.

What Pip couldn't get used to was the quiet underneath. A square full of grown-ups, and nobody running. Bram Tolley opened the shutters of his new schoolhouse every morning at eight and stood in the doorway with a slate, and every morning nobody came, and he closed them again at noon.

Dun Harrow was waiting at the bottom of the square with her buckler.

She hadn't known he had it. It had been on its peg by her door last night. Now it had a new strap, black, oiled, with a brass buckle where the old one had been knotted string, and he held it out to her without a word, the way he'd held out the horseshoe nail when she was little.

"I didn't ask you to do that," she said.

"Strap was rotten." He didn't look at her. He looked at the dent in the buckler's face, the one from the drover's cudgel, and touched it with his thumb. "Your gran'd have had my hide, seeing that strap."

"How much?"

"Get off." He went back toward the forge with his shoulders up round his ears, and Pip stood with the buckler in her hands and his chain link from the Ox in her pocket, and didn't know why she was angry.

Marta gave the food bundle to Tobin, and looked at Pip for exactly as long as it took to see her. She hadn't said a word to Pip since the dropped tray. Pip had stopped humming in front of her. She hadn't managed to stop humming.

On Tobin's step at the end of the mill road, Nell sat in a shawl that went round her three times with Tuppence on her knees like a loaf, and watched them come.

"You're going away," she said to Tobin.

"Not far. To the sea."

"Bring me a treasure."

"I will."

"You said that before," said Nell, in her weather voice, and went back to the cat.

Pip watched Tobin's face not move, and looked at the road.

It went down the valley beside the river, white with frost, past farms that were only doorsteps. Ash went in front, as always, but further in front than he used to. He and Tobin hadn't said a whole sentence to each other since Tobin had him by the collar in Wren's kitchen. And Wren and Tobin walked together in the middle, and the gap between Wren's shoulder and Tobin's arm, which had been there all Pip's life, the width of a hand, wasn't there any more.

Pip noticed it at the first stile. Ash noticed it at the second, because she saw him look back, once, and then not look again.

Nobody said anything. That was the Vale all over, Gran would have said. Everybody knowing everything and nobody saying.

"You're doing it again," said Ash, an hour later, without turning round.

"Doing what?"

"The tune."

She shut her mouth. They walked on. The second evening the wind came round off the estuary and smelled of salt and rot and tar, and something in Pip that she'd forgotten about sat up like a dog hearing its name.

### 3.2

*Saltwake. Two days on, at low water.*

Wren had been to Saltwake once, when she was six, on her mother's hip.

She remembered gulls, and a copper roof gone green that hurt to look at in the sun, and her mother going into a long building full of the slap of fish and coming out with her mouth thin. She remembered a big man in a blue coat on the quay, who had given her a silver penny and said she had her mother's eyes. *The reeve*, her mother had said, after, in a voice Wren had filed away and never opened.

There was no copper roof now. There was the quay, a long arm of stone curving into the grey-green water, its mooring rings rusted the colour of dried blood. There were doorsteps, a hundred and sixty of them up the slope in rows, one with a horseshoe set into it for luck. Out on the spit the lighthouse stood up white against the cloud, the only whole thing in the town, and even that wasn't whole. Its top was gone, broken off ragged like the heel of a loaf, and where the lamp room should have been there was sky.

"Well," said Pip. "It's cheerful."

Nobody laughed. Pip hadn't expected them to. She'd been quiet since the second stile, which Wren noticed and put away.

The tithe slip ran off the end of the quay into the estuary, a ramp of square stones with two ruts down it a cart's width apart. As the water drew back, the ramp kept going: across the mud, weed-slick, further than any slipway had a reason to go, to the foot of the headland, where the rock had a mouth in it.

The mouth was dark. Then, as the last of the water ran out of it with a long sucking sigh, it wasn't. Red light came up from inside, slow and warm, like a coal blown on.

"There," said Ash.

"It's only open when the sea's out," said Tobin.

"Twice a day." Wren had her mother's book open, the cost book with her own figures in the back now, and was writing down the hour by the light. "Low water at dusk. Six hours and a bit to the next. We'll want to know how long it stays open. We'll want to know a lot of things."

Tobin had his left hand cupped by his ear, which he did now without knowing, since the bell. Ash was crouched at the edge of the slip, looking at the red the way he'd looked at Pip's arm. Pip was looking at the lighthouse.

Twenty-eight names on Wren's wall had no tick. Three belonged to people who had handed the four of them a dagger, an axe and a bow on the last night of the world, and only Wren knew about a short neat row of five.

"Gran's down there," Pip said. Not asking.

"Somebody is."

"Don't do that." Pip didn't turn round. "Don't do the careful voice. If you think it, say it."

"I think it," said Wren. "I don't know it."

Pip nodded, as if that were fair, and started down the slip in the red light before anyone could say who went first.

### 3.3

*The Drowned Arcade, floor 1: the fish market.*

The sea was in the market, and it was breathing.

Tobin felt it before he understood it. Under the stone arches the water came to his shins, cold and clear and full of drifting scales, and as he stood there it drew back off his boots, all of it, all at once, down the length of the hall. A long wet rattle of shingle. Then it stopped, and lay still for a while, and came back.

It was a covered market, arches down both sides, going on further than any market should. On the marble slabs lay cod and ling and gurnard in rows, eyes open, fresh as the morning. Their ice had gone to brine years ago and nobody had told the fish.

"I'm not eating anything," said Pip. "In case anyone was wondering. I'm a reformed character."

Tobin tried a door in the arches and found stone behind it. Then the water went out again, and as it went the arch changed: a door stood open on a room full of hanging nets. The water came back. The door was stone again.

"Some rooms are only there when it's out," said Ash. "Like the slip."

"Then we're quick."

"Or we change when it goes out."

The bell was on a post in the middle of the hall, where a market crier would have stood: a brass hand-bell on a hook, green at the lip, with a wooden handle worn pale. Pip got to it first, because she would have climbed over Tobin to get to it first, and lifted it off its hook, and rang it.

He felt it more than heard it. His left ear rang on its own since the Bell-Warden, a thin thread that never stopped, and sounds came to that side as if through a wall. But he felt this: a small clear note, and the water, which had been coming in slowly, came in all at once, rushing up past his knees.

"*Oi*," said Pip, delighted, soaked to the waist. "It brings it in. It brings it in early."

"Give it here," said Tobin.

"Shan't. I found it."

"Pip—"

"*Shan't.*" She hung it on her belt, next to Gran's dagger, and grinned at him, and then rang it again on purpose when the water was going out, to see. The water stopped, as if it had remembered something, and turned round and came back.

Wren wrote that down.

At the end of the hall, wedged against the last arch with weed growing on its staves, there was a barrel. A flour barrel, an ordinary one, the iron bands rusted through and bleeding orange into the brine. On its head, burned small into the wood, there was a mark Tobin had seen on every sack and barrel that went down the tithe ramp his whole childhood: a millwheel with four spokes. Aldous Sedge's mark.

He stood and looked at it.

"That's ours," he said. "That's from home. How's that here?"

"It's all one thing," said Wren. She'd come up beside him. She didn't touch the barrel. "One stomach. Hollowmere went down first. It's still going through."

He thought about that all through the second floor, where the gulls came off the drowned rooftops with hooked beaks and wings like torn nets, crying almost in words, *gut, gut, gut*, and while they found the salter asleep in a smokehouse barrel with salt on her face like frost on a window.

Maud Tiller woke counting. She counted the barrels in the smokehouse twice, moving her lips, and then she counted the four of them twice, and then she said, "The smokehouse goes downwind of everyone. They'll thank me," and the chimney drew her up, still counting.

Tobin stood in the empty smokehouse with salt in his beard and thought of a little girl asleep in a coal bin, and a word that had been going round his head for a fortnight, which was *bait*.

### 3.4

*Floor 3: the slipway at low tide.*

The sea had gone out of the bay and was standing at the mouth of it like a wall, waiting.

Ash sat on the top of the slipway and watched it, because nobody else would. Below him the seabed lay bare as far as the light went, boats on their sides in the mud with their masts pointing every way, crabs going between them in hundreds. And half a mile off, across the mouth of the bay, the water had pulled back into a single grey-green ridge, taller than the church tower, glassy, holding.

It wasn't still. He watched it long enough to see. It leaned. A hair's width at a time, like the minute hand on the clock in the schoolhouse that you could never catch moving.

"There," said Wren, pointing.

Out on the seabed, a little way past the nearest boats, one boat sat upright on its keel as if somebody had set it down carefully. Blue paint gone grey. A man asleep in the bottom of it, sitting up, his head on his chest, and round him, from his boots to his neck, net. Fishing net, wound round and round, and gone to stone. Grey stone with the knots standing out on it like knuckles.

"The water's coming back," said Ash. "Not yet. But it's coming." He'd been counting since they arrived. "We've got as long as it took us to walk down the stairs and get our breath. Not much more."

"Then stop *sitting* there," said Pip.

They ran.

The mud took their boots to the ankle and let go with a noise like a kiss. The sleeper had a red face, white stubble and a gold ring in one ear, and he was snoring faintly through the stone. Tobin swung at the net and the axe rang off it and jarred him to the teeth. Wren tried a mark, and the gold went into the stone and did nothing that anyone could see.

Ash looked at the knots.

"It was knotted before it was stone," he said. "Knots are where it's thinnest. Tobin. Not the edge. Turn it round."

Tobin looked at him. It was the first thing Ash had said to him in a fortnight that wasn't about the road. Then he turned the axe in his hands and hit the nearest knot with the back of the head, the poll, short and sharp, the way you knock a wedge, and the knot cracked clean through. The net round it sagged.

"Again," said Ash.

There were a lot of knots.

He stood up in the stern and watched the wall. It was coming now, the way a crowd comes across a square when the bell goes, unhurried, with white water at its foot and boats lifting off the mud like toys.

"Pip," he said. "Whatever you do. Don't ring that bell."

"I wasn't going to!"

"Your hand's on it."

She took her hand off it as if it had bitten her. Tobin hit a knot. Wren pulled the cracked stone off the man's chest in pieces and threw them over the side. The sleeper's head came up, eyes shut, and he said in a thick slurred voice, quite clearly, "Not with *her* aboard, I said, I told you, it's bad luck—"

"He's waking," said Wren.

"He's *complaining*," said Pip. "Lovely. Lift him."

They got him out of the boat with half the net still on his legs, and ran. The mud was water now, shin deep, and the roar filled the bay. Ash had the man's feet. The slipway came up ahead like a road out of a flood, and Pip was on it, and Wren, and Tobin with the man's shoulders across his back.

The water hit Ash at the foot of the ramp and picked him up. It wasn't like being hit. It was like being taken, the way a hand closes on a coin. Salt in his mouth, cold, turning, and he thought, very clearly: *oh, this is interesting*, and then: *no it isn't.*

A hand had him by the belt.

He came up on the stones on his face, coughing brine, with Tobin lying on the ramp beside him, one arm hooked through a mooring ring and the other through Ash's belt. Tobin let go. He didn't say anything. He rolled onto his back and lay breathing, and the sea went back down the slip and came up again, softer, and went back.

Ash lay beside him on the cold stone. After a while he said, "Thanks."

"Shut up," said Tobin.

That was the most they'd said to each other in two weeks. Ash decided it would do.

Above them on the slipway the sleeper sat up in his ruin of net. He looked round at the four of them, wet through, with his red face working. His eyes went to Pip, and to Wren, and his mouth opened.

"Two of you," he said. "Two women. In my *boat*. No wonder."

"Three," said Pip, "if you count Tobin," and the fisherman, to his own surprise, began to laugh, and was still laughing when the warm lifted him off the stones, net and all. Halfway up he shouted down about his shed on the slipway, and the nets out where they'd dry, and to tell Agathe Voss he'd seen her light go out, he'd *seen* it.

### 3.5

*Floor 4: the sunken warehouses.*

The fourth floor was warehouses, and every barrel in them was lying.

Wren found it out with the first one. *SALT HERRING*, painted on the staves in white, and the weigh-house mark beside it, a beam scale in a ring. Tobin stove the head in to see, because he was tired of being surprised, and grain poured out over his boots. Good oats, salted dry, packed so tight they'd kept their shape for ten years.

"Every one?" said Ash.

They knocked on a dozen. Every one.

"Night barges," said Pip. She'd gone quiet. "Gran used to say it. When something was dodgy at the gate. *That came down on the night barge.* I never knew what she meant."

Wren knew. Hollowmere had hidden its grain behind false wine casks. Saltwake had hidden it inside fish and sold it downriver. One town was a thief and the other a merchant, and she didn't know which was worse, and that worried her, because she'd always known before.

The stair down was in a loading bay that was dry for one quarter of every tide, and they lost a quarter-tide to the scale-mites: brass weights off the weigh-house counter, a pound and an ounce, scuttling on spider's legs, clicking when they died. They found the ferryman asleep at the oars of a barge in a flooded dock, rowing in his sleep, slowly, going nowhere.

Kip Rudd woke with a smile already on. He was thirty or so, handsome in a worn-out way, and he looked at the four of them as if they were exactly who he'd hoped to see.

"Don't need a house," he said, before anyone asked. "Need a jetty. Out on the river road. I'll want to be going places." He looked at Pip, and something sharpened in his face. "Ostrander? You've the look. There was an Ostrander used to ride my barge, oh, years back. Going downriver, wasn't she. Pretty. Asked after a little girl."

Pip went very still.

Wren watched the ferryman's face, which was warm and easy and interested, and watched it watch Pip's, and knew he was lying the way you know a coin is light by the weight of it in your hand. He was a man who said the thing people wanted, because it was cheap and it made them like him.

And she watched Pip want it. She had never in her life seen Pip want anything so much.

She could have said, *He's lying, Pip.* It would have been true. It would have been the kind of thing Pip would say herself.

She didn't. She let Pip have it. She told herself that was kindness, and put it in the same box as the other things, and the warm came and took the ferryman up through the loading bay, waving.

"What did she ask?" Pip said, to the empty air. "He didn't say what she asked."

"We'll ask him," said Wren, "at home," and they went on, and the water came in behind them and filled the bay.

### 3.6

*Floor 5: the weigh-house. Then the bank.*

The Weigh-Crab weighed Tobin first, and threw him away.

The weigh-house was a hall of wet flagstones under a green copper roof, and in the middle stood the great scale that took a whole cart of fish at once. Only the iron beam was its back now, and the two brass pans were its claws, and the post had split into eight barnacled legs, and the pointer was set in its face where a crab keeps its eyes.

It came at them sideways and got Tobin in one pan before anyone moved. The pointer swung, trembled, settled. Then the claw flung him the length of the hall into a heap of fish boxes.

"I'm fine," he said, getting up slowly. "It's weighing things."

It was. It weighed a barrel of salt herring, and the pointer swung right over, and it cracked the barrel and gulped the grain like a gull with a crust. It weighed Ash, and flung him at the wall.

It picked up Pip.

The pan closed round her like a fist, cold brass stinking of old fish. She was lifted and swung, and she saw the pointer in its face twitch. Barely. It hardly moved at all.

Short weight.

The crab shook her. It shook her the way Gran used to shake a sack at the gate that didn't weigh what the carter said, hard, to see what fell out. Her teeth met in her tongue. Her ribs went *crack* on one side, two of them, she felt them go, a sound like green sticks. Then it slammed her down on the flags, as if she'd been caught cheating, and turned away to look for something worth eating.

She lay there. She couldn't breathe in all the way. Somewhere Wren was shouting at Tobin, and Tobin was shouting back, and Pip lay on the wet flagstones and thought, absurdly, *short weight*, and wanted to laugh, and couldn't because of the ribs.

"Pip. The bell." Wren was on her knees beside her. "Look at the floor."

The floor sloped, so it would sluice clean, and at the low end there were grates, and under them, far down, the sea sucking in and out.

"When it's on the low end," said Wren, "ring it."

Pip got the bell off her belt. Her hand didn't want to close. Tobin was hitting the crab's legs like a man chopping a tree he knew he wouldn't finish, and it was backing away from him, sideways, down the slope, over the grates.

She rang it.

The sea came up through every grate at once, a step early, white, roaring, and lifted the crab off its legs like a cork and turned it over. It lay on its back with its legs going, and Tobin walked up onto its belly with the axe and set his feet and split the beam.

It came apart like a dropped scale. Pans and chains and weights skittered across the flags. The pointer rolled to Pip's feet, still trembling.

In the body of it, where the beam met the post, curled up small inside a crust of salt like a man asleep in a snowdrift, there was a man in a blue coat.

Pip cut him out. She didn't know why she wanted to be the one. Her ribs hurt so much she was crying, and she worked the salt off his face with Gran's dagger, and the dagger went warm in her hand like a cat.

And she was at the feast gate, aged seven, on market day, with Gran's big hand on her shoulder. A boy came through with his shirt bulging. Ten, maybe. Thin as a lath. A loaf under his shirt, you could see the shape of it, and his eyes going everywhere. Gran put out her hand and took him by the collar, quite gently, the way you take a cat that's been on the table, and looked at him. He looked back at her. Pip waited for the shout. *Thief!* There was no shout. Gran tapped him on the shoulder, once, the way she tapped everybody, and let go, and he ran. *You let him go!* Pip had said, outraged. *You let him go, he had a whole loaf!* Gran had looked down at her. *I counted him,* Gran said. *I count everybody, chick. That's my job. Telling on them isn't always.*

"—Pip. Your eyes went."

"I know," she said. "I know they did."

The man in the blue coat opened his eyes. He was sixty or so, broad, gone soft in the face the way rich men did, with a seaman's tan ten years asleep hadn't taken off him. He looked up at Pip and knew someone in her.

"Odile's girl," he said. "You've her chin. God help you." His voice was deep and warm and used to being listened to. "Gideon Hask. Reeve of this harbour. Has anybody told you what you've got on you?"

"Fish," said Pip.

He laughed, and sat up, and looked round at the broken scale, the copper roof, the four of them. Then up, as if he could see through the roof. "Is the light lit?" he said. "On the spit. Is Agathe's light lit?"

"No," said Wren.

His face went old for a moment. Then the warm came up through the grates, and he rose, slowly, in his blue coat, with his hand over his heart as if somebody had sung an anthem, and called down to them where his house would go: on the quay, the reeve's house, where he could see every boat come in. Every one.

The bank was through the back door: the yellow fire, the cold basin, the strongboxes, the hoist. The line under the ceiling here was hung with glass net-floats instead of lanterns, each with a small light in it. Wren counted them, and didn't say the number.

Pip drank till her ribs stopped grinding. They weren't mended. They were only quieter.

"Home or on," said Wren. And then, looking straight at her: "Pip. You said not to ask you like it's a vote. So I'm asking you like it is one."

"On," said Pip. "*On.* She's down there. You know she is."

"The tide's coming in," said Ash, from the stair door. "It'll be high when we get down. Half the doors shut, the whole way. We bank on a falling tide. Not a rising one."

"Home," said Wren.

"Home," said Tobin, after a moment, not looking at Pip.

Pip looked at the three of them. Then she laughed, which hurt.

"Well," she said. "That's what voting's for, isn't it." And she got into the hoist first and sat down on the floor of it with her arms round her ribs, and didn't say another word all the way up.

### 3.7

*Saltwake. The weeks before midwinter.*

Gideon Hask got Saltwake back on its feet the way a good foreman gets a barn up: by knowing everyone's name, and shouting it.

Tobin watched him do it and couldn't decide whether he liked him.

The pieces came up from every dive, and Saltwake rose round them: the weigh-house, the smokehouse downwind of everything, tarred sheds along the slipway, and the reeve's house on the quay with a bow window looking straight out to sea. People came up the shaft and stood on their doorsteps blinking, and Gideon was there before they'd got their breath, with his hand on their shoulders, saying their names. He knew whose boat had been whose and who owed the chandler. He told the widows their husbands would be home by spring, and half of them were.

He was very good at it. Every dusk he stood on the quay with his hands behind his back and watched the empty estuary and the broken lighthouse, as if waiting for the boats to come in.

"He loves this place," said Wren.

"Yes," said Tobin.

He didn't say the rest. He didn't know how to say it. It was the way Gideon talked: easy and warm and with no edges on it anywhere, like a plank planed so often it had gone shiny. Tobin knew that kind of voice. He'd been using one on his father for six weeks.

Kip got his jetty on the river road, and his barge came up after him, and after that it was a couple of hours to Hollowmere's lake instead of two days. Before the barge they held the meeting in Teague Pellow's shed, on upturned crates under hanging nets, and Pip said, "First time in ten years it's not at Wren's," and Wren said, "It's not about the house," and nobody believed her, least of all Wren.

Teague taught them to fish. He'd said on the first morning that he wouldn't have a woman in his boat, not now, not ever, look where it had got him. On the second he took Pip out because she sat in the boat and wouldn't get out. On the third he took Tobin, because Tobin carried the nets down without being asked.

Tobin was good at it. It was like the axe: you stopped pushing and let the line do the work, and you watched the water and waited, and he had been waiting for things his whole life. He brought up eleven codling to Teague's three, and Teague looked at him with something like grief.

Pip caught a boot, Teague's hat, Teague, and on the fourth day a mackerel, which slapped her in the face and went back over the side.

"Don't laugh," she said.

"I'm not laughing."

"You're laughing *inside*, Tobin, I can see it, it's in your *beard*."

He was. It was the first time he'd laughed since the bell.

She was all right out there, with her ribs strapped and her neck burn going brown and the bite on her arm a pale seam, swearing at the fish. She was all right with everyone except Wren, and with Gideon most of all.

The reeve liked her the way old men like girls who answer back. He told her about Odile at the Saltwake market forty years ago, a big raw-handed girl down with the Hollowmere carts. "Threw me in the harbour once," he said. "Off this quay. I'd made a remark about the weight of her father's cheeses. Fair remark. True remark. In I went." He laughed. "I married a gentler woman."

"Dorrit," said Pip. "We haven't found her."

"You will." He put his hand on her shoulder. "You're Odile's girl. You find things."

Tobin watched Pip glow, and didn't like it, and couldn't have said why.

In the evenings Wren taught Pip figures, on the old weigh-house tally slates Gideon had given them: how to carry, how to set one column against another, how to find a number made to look like a different number. Pip hated being taught by Wren. She'd hated it at seven and she hated it now.

She was quick, though. Tobin saw it. She'd been counting heads with Gran since she could walk, and somewhere in her the numbers had been waiting.

"You're good at this," Wren said one night, surprised.

"Don't sound so pleased," said Pip. "I'm not doing it for you."

When he went home that night, Nell was asleep on his bed with the cat, and he put a shell on the pillow by her head. A big spiral shell from the estuary, pink inside like an ear. In the morning it was on the table by his boots, put back.

### 3.8

*The Drowned Arcade, floors 6 and 7: the kelp streets. Midwinter's eve.*

They went down from the bank on a falling tide, the way Ash had said, and the second half of the Arcade opened in front of them like a fist unclenching.

The sixth floor was Saltwake's lanes as they'd been, drowned, with kelp grown up out of the cobbles in brown and gold forests taller than the houses. At low tide you walked the lanes in green half-light with the kelp hanging off the eaves like washing. At high tide you went up onto the roofs, and the kelp stood up straight round you toward a surface that wasn't there.

They found Dorrit Hask asleep in a chair on a doorstep, hands folded, kelp grown up through the chair. She was small and neat and grey, and her face, waking, did the most beautiful thing Ash had ever seen a face do: it looked round for someone.

"Gideon?" she said.

"He's home," said Pip. "He's on the quay. He's got a bow window."

"Then put me next to him," said Dorrit Hask, simply, and patted Pip's hand, and went up.

On the seventh floor, the stir came up and the Gleaners came with it.

Ash had been waiting for them since the pie in the oven mouth. He wanted to know what they were the way he'd wanted the pie, and he knew that about himself now, and it didn't help.

These held smoked herring instead of pie. One wore a clerk's sleeve garters on its black arms, and one had a sou'wester jammed down over its sheaf mask, and Ash thought: *they look like the town. They've kept bits.* They came with the barnacle-men, mooring posts that walked, crusted white, slow and impossible to stop, and with eels Ash knew with a lurch from Hollowmere's fourth floor, the red line down their flanks, grown fat on the tide.

"Those are ours," said Tobin, behind him. "Those are *ours*."

"It's all one stomach," said Wren.

A gutting-gull came off a chimney and took her across the back of the right hand, the hook of its beak going in at the knuckle and out at the wrist, as neat as a knife opening a fish. She didn't make a sound. She looked at her hand, the back of it open and pouring, and then she finished the mark she'd been writing, with the blood running down the brass foot of the staff, and the gold went off it and took the gull out of the air in a puff of feathers like a pillow burst.

Ash sewed it at the stair while Pip talked rubbish about Teague's hat to stop Wren looking. Seven stitches in her writing hand. He watched her flex the fingers after, one at a time, the way you'd test a bowstring.

"It'll stiffen," he said. "In the cold."

"Then I'll write in the warm," said Wren.

They looked at each other over the lamp. It was the closest they'd been since the winter, since the light over the table that wouldn't go out. He thought she was going to say something. He thought, for a moment, he was.

"*Tide*," said Pip, and they ran for the stair with the water coming up the lane behind them like a crowd.

### 3.9

*Floor 8: the counting-house.*

There were two desks, and one of them was drowning.

The counting-house was a long panelled room, the kind rich towns built for the people who added up their money. On a dais at one end stood a desk with a ledger open on it, dry in the window light. Down three steps at the other end stood its twin, and the sea was coming in under the door there, already round the desk's feet, and rising.

"Read the wet one," said Wren. "Pip. The wet one. Now."

The water was at Pip's knees by the time she got there, and at the desk's lip. She got the ledger up off the desk and held it against her chest, open, and Wren came down the steps and put her bandaged hand on the page.

Two columns on every page. Pip had learned that much on the slates. *Landed*, in a plain hand, and the weight of fish and grain beside it, every boat, every barge. *Shown*, beside it, and a smaller number. Always smaller. And at the foot of each year, a third line: *Owed to the deep*, and a fraction.

"Don't read it," said Wren. "Run your finger down the last line. Just the last line. Every year."

Pip ran her finger down. Her ribs ached with the weight of the book. The water came up past her thighs, cold enough to make her gasp, and she turned the pages with her teeth chattering.

*Owed: one part in ten.* That was the first year. *One in ten.* *One in nine.* She turned faster. *One in eight.* A long run of eights. *One in six.* *One in five.* *One in four*, in the year thirty-one, and under it, for the first time, a signature, in a big confident looping hand: *G. Hask, reeve.*

"It's getting hungrier," she said.

"Keep going."

*One in four.* *One in three.* The signature, every autumn, a little bigger. *Two in five.* *One in two*, the year fifty, and the *Landed* column fat and the *Shown* column thin, and the difference between them, which Pip worked out in her head without being asked, the way Wren had taught her, was most of a harvest.

"It grew every year," said Pip. "However much they grew. It grew faster."

"Yes," said Wren.

"So they were always going to—"

"Yes."

The water was at her waist, and the ink on the bottom pages coming off in little blue clouds. Pip looked at Wren and saw her face doing a sum it couldn't get out of, and thought: *she's thinking about her own mother's book.* And then: *I bet she is. I bet she'll never say.*

Pip took the ledger in both hands and tore out the last twenty years. It went with a noise like cloth, and Wren made a small sound as if Pip had torn her. Pip folded the pages in half, and in half again, and stuffed them down her shirt against her skin, under the strapping on her ribs, where they were wet and cold and stayed. Wren's slate of notes went down after them.

"*Pip*—"

"It's going under anyway." The water closed over the desk. The ledger in her hands was pulp. She let it go. "You want it read? I'll read it. I'll read it till I know it. But I'm not leaving it here for the sea."

She waded back up the steps to the dry end. At the dry desk the other ledger lay open in the window light, clean and neat and lovely, every *Shown* number in its place, every year a fair tenth, then a fair quarter, then a fair half, all of it paid. She looked at it for a long time. It was the neatest thing she had ever seen.

"Who wrote this one?" she said.

"The same hand," said Wren. "Both of them. The same clerk wrote both."

### 3.10

*Floor 9: the kelp piers. Night.*

The Gleaners were waiting for them on the piers, and one of them was keeping score.

It was night on the ninth floor: rotten piers running out on black stilts over glistening mud, hung with kelp, with red lamps on posts. The tide was out. Ash heard the scraping before he saw anything. A lot of it.

Then he saw, at the end of the longest pier, on top of a mooring post, a Gleaner sitting with a book.

It sat the way a clerk sits on a high stool, a ledger across its knees and a pen in its scorched black hand, its straw mask neatly combed. As they came out onto the pier it looked at each of them in turn and wrote. Four small movements of the pen. It was counting them in.

Beside the post, coiled whip on his shoulder, stood the big one from Hollowmere. The whistler.

He whistled. Three notes falling, a little run going up. And beside Ash, Pip, who had been humming it on and off for two months, went white and said, "*Oh*," as if somebody had told her the end of a story.

They came from under the piers. From between the stilts, up the ladders, out of the kelp, with herring and honey cake and slices of feast pie held out in their hands. The tide was coming. Ash could hear it far off, a long hiss across the mud.

It was the worst fight they'd had. The piers were narrow and the Gleaners never tried to hurt anyone, only to hold, and every time one got a grip the others came in with food. Tobin went down under three of them with a herring pushed at his clenched teeth and came up roaring. Wren burned the pier behind them so nothing could come from that side. Ash put arrows through masks until his fingers were raw, and every time he looked up, the clerk on the post was writing.

"The one with the book," he said. "Pip. The book."

Pip went down the pier like a thrown stone and hit the post, not the clerk, with her buckler and all her weight. The rotten wood cracked at the foot, and the post leaned out over the mud with the clerk clutching its ledger, and fell.

Tobin was there when it landed. He put the axe through the straw, not deep, the way you'd split a sack, and the mask came apart.

There was a man's face under it, thin, forty or so, with a spectacles mark across the nose and ink on his lower lip from licking a pen. The black went out of his hands like a stain going out of cloth, and they were hands, pale, inky at the second finger. He lay in the mud and blinked up at them.

All along the pier the Gleaners stopped.

"Ivo Sallow," the man said. He said it the way you say your name to an official. "Harbour clerk. I assume you'll want that for your records."

Ash crouched beside him. He felt the way he'd felt watching the hare's eye. He was ashamed of it and he did it anyway. "What are you?" he said. "What are they? Tell me."

Ivo Sallow looked at him with pale wet eyes.

"We're the ones who said yes," he said. "It's very simple. You go down at the feast, and you sleep, and some of us wake a little. Inside it. Not much. Enough to know where we are. And then something asks you, very courteously, whether you'd like to help." He coughed. "Serve, and the ones you love sleep sweet. And they wake first. When it's time."

"Sleep sweet," said Wren.

"A good dream, instead of a bad one. That's all sweet means. You've seen the bad ones. You've walked through them." He moved his head toward the piers, the black water. "Everyone who serves chose it for someone."

"Who'd you choose it for?" said Pip.

He was quiet for a moment.

"My sister's boys," he said. Then, because he was a clerk and couldn't leave a figure wrong: "And the books balance. Down here. Every night. Every column. You can't imagine what that's like, after nineteen years of the other." He shut his eyes. "That was the truth. You can put it in."

"And the children?" said Tobin. His voice was strange. "What's a sweet dream for a child?"

Ivo opened his eyes again. He looked, for the first time, as if he didn't want to answer.

"There's only one," he said. "For children. All of them, every town, the whole Vale. The same dream. One long table, at the very bottom, and sweets on it, and nobody sends them to bed." He looked at Tobin. "They're very happy. They're being kept for the end."

Nobody said anything. Ash heard the tide coming across the mud, closer. He heard Wren breathe in and not breathe out.

"Twenty-three," said Wren. "Twenty-three of ours."

"Oh, more than that," said Ivo Sallow. "Every town's. It's a very long table." He made a small tidy gesture with his inky hand, as if straightening papers. "And you're not to think it's chaos down here. It isn't. There's a man who reads the page. He sorted us, after. He's not one of us, he doesn't wear the straw, he—"

The whistler pulled his own mask off.

He did it with one hand, the way you take off a hat coming into a house, and dropped it on the pier. Under the straw was a broad, weathered face, fifty or so, with a grey moustache and a long white scar through one eyebrow where a cart-trace had once caught it. A carter's face. Ash had seen it every autumn of his childhood, at the head of the tithe oxen, walking beside the lead beast and whistling to keep it going down the ramp.

"Mister Stroud," said Pip. Her voice was very small. "You let us ride on the oxen."

Stroud looked at Tobin.

"Don't tell her," he said.

Then he whistled, and the Gleaners went to him like dogs to a shepherd, and he went off down the dark pier with them round him, unhurried, into the kelp and the hiss of the tide. Nobody went after him.

Ivo Sallow lay in the mud with the sea coming. He turned his head and found Wren.

"Did they balance?" he said. "My books. Up there. You'd know. You've the look of somebody who'd know."

"No," said Wren.

"No." He seemed almost relieved. "No. They never did."

The warm was coming up through the mud under him, Ash could feel it, but Ivo held up his inky hand like a man at a counter, and after a moment Tobin understood and bent and picked him up, and carried him to the place where the mud breathed, and set him down there, and stood back, and watched him go.

### 3.11

*Floor 10: the dark.*

On the stair down, the dagger had gone warm again.

Pip had been on the last step with her hand on the hilt and the tide coming up behind them, and then she was on the doorstep at home, aged seven, shelling beans into a bowl, with Gran on the step beside her. Gran was telling her about the tunnels. Flat as a watch report: the founders, the silver, the man walled up alive, the knocking on still nights. And at the end of it Gran had stopped shelling, and looked at Pip for a long time, the way she looked at a stranger at the gate, as if she were measuring how far Pip would go and how many she'd take with her.

Pip had come out of it on the stair knowing something. She had put it down and stepped over it, the way you step over a dead thing on a path.

Now there was nothing on the tenth floor but the dark and the light.

It was the seabed: sand and broken shell under her boots, cold, and the weight of water she couldn't feel but could somehow hear, pressing. She couldn't see her hand. There was no red down here at all. There was only, a long way off and high up, a light.

It was beautiful. A round white light, warm at the heart, like the lit window of your own house at the end of a long road in the rain. It was Agathe Voss's lamp. Pip had seen it from the Hollowmere road on clear nights her whole childhood, a little star at the edge of the world.

It hung on a long iron stalk, the lighthouse stair grown out into a fishing-rod and bent over. And below the light, where the light didn't go, there was a darker dark, and in it, when the lamp swung, something gleamed. Harbour piles, black and wet and close together, like teeth.

And in the light, hanging in it, there was Gran.

She hung under the lamp as if laid out on air. Her hands were folded on her chest, neatly. Her watch-cap was set straight on her grey hair, and her boots were laced, and her jacket was buttoned to the neck, every button, the way Gran never in her life buttoned it. Someone had laid her out. Someone tidy. Her eyes were open, and they looked at nothing.

Pip took a step toward her.

"Pip." Tobin's hand found her arm in the dark. "Pip, don't."

"That's *Gran*—"

"I know." His hand closed. "Look at Ash."

Ash was walking toward the light.

Steadily, face turned up, bow hanging from his hand, the way a man walks toward a fire on a cold night. His face in the white light was calm and quite empty. He was halfway to the teeth.

Tobin let go of her and went after him. She heard him hit Ash low and the two of them go down in the sand, Ash fighting silently to get up and go on.

"It draws you," said Wren, somewhere off to the left, in a voice thin with effort. "Whatever looks at it. Don't look at it. Don't look at the light. Don't look at *her*."

Pip looked at the sand.

She'd done this all her life. *The trick with Gran was not to look at her.* At the feast gate with the dagger in her bag; every time she came home late. Eyes on the cobbles, because if you look at Gran she looks back, and then you tell her.

The sand in front of her was lit faintly from one side, and on it, long and black, lay her own shadow. It pointed away from the light. Every shadow on the seabed did, like the spokes of a wheel, and when the lamp swung on its stalk they all swung together, like the shadow on the church sundial.

"Wren," Pip said. "Can you see your shadow?"

"Yes—"

"Watch it. Don't look up. Watch your shadow. When it gets short, you're under the light. When it goes long, the light's going away." She was already moving. "Tobin. Hold him."

"*Pip*—"

She turned her back on the light and walked toward it backwards.

The lamp spoke to her when she was halfway there.

It had Gran's voice. It was the best thing it could have done, and it knew it, and it did it gently. *Chick*, it said, behind her and above her, close, warm, with peppermint on its breath. *Pip. Look at me. Let me see your face.*

She looked at the sand. Her shadow was getting shorter.

*You went down the tunnel. You took them all. Clever girl. Turn round. Let me look at you.*

"No," said Pip. She was crying. She didn't stop walking. "You never wanted to look at me. You wanted to look at whether I'd go."

Her shadow was a pool round her boots. She was under it. She could feel the warm of the lamp on the back of her neck, on her burn, like the sun. Above her and behind her, Gran was hanging in the light with her eyes open, so close Pip could have reached up and touched her boots.

She reached up behind her with Gran's dagger, without looking, into the warm, and found the place where the iron stalk met the lamp's housing, the joint, by feel, the way Ash had found the knots, and put the dagger into it with everything she had.

Glass cracked. The light flickered.

"*Now*, Wren—"

Wren's gold came across the dark, written with her eyes shut, crooked as a child's letters, and it went into the crack and the lamp went out.

The dark was total. Then a great wet slam right in front of Pip, so close it blew her hair back: the teeth closing on the place where the light had been. Then a small round glow, the colour of a lantern seen through fog, Wren's light that wouldn't go out, and Tobin running past in it with the axe, and the head falling on the iron stalk.

The stalk came down in coils like a dropped chain. The mouth sagged and was only harbour piles in sand, a broken jetty.

And Gran fell. Pip caught her. She went down on her knees under the weight, the way Tobin had with Hob, and didn't drop her, and the dagger in her hand was warm, warmer than it had ever been, hot as a hand.

And she was in Gran's kitchen on the morning of the feast, and her hands were big and red-knuckled and old, packing a bag. Gran's hands. She was looking out through Gran's eyes at a small girl in the doorway, gap-toothed, scab on her knee, watching. Seven. So small. Gran's hands put the dagger in the bag and buckled the shield flat on the outside, and pulled the strap tight, and stopped. And looked at the child for a long, long time. The hands didn't shake. *For the spooky things*, Gran's mouth said. And then, after a while: *Mind you bring that back clean.*

That was all. No reason. Only the looking.

When Pip came out of it the dagger felt different in her hand. Heavier. Easier. And the buckler on her arm, with its old dent from the drover's cudgel, sat on her arm the way Gran's hand used to sit on her knee, holding her still, so she wouldn't fall.

Gran opened her eyes.

She looked up at Pip, at her wet face and the burn on her neck. She looked past her at the other three, one, two, three, the way you count at a gate. Then back.

"Did you bring it back clean?" said Odile Ostrander.

"No," said Pip.

Something happened at the corner of Gran's mouth. It might have been a smile. With Gran you never knew.

The warm came up through the sand, and it took Gran out of her arms, and up, a very long way, toward a town a day's walk off. And out of the wreck of the lamp-housing, where the glass lay broken in the sand, another woman rose behind her, tall and gaunt with cropped grey hair, holding the great lamp of the Saltwake light against her chest in both arms like a child, and shouting down at them as she went, furious, "*Is it lit?* Is it *lit*? Who put it *out*?"

### 3.12

*Hollowmere, the gate lane. That night.*

Gran was sitting on her doorstep in Tansy Brook's second-best blanket, with Marta's broth going cold beside her, and she watched Pip come up the lane the whole way.

The town had come out for her, and drifted off by the time Kip's barge got the four of them across the lake. Dun Harrow hadn't. He was standing in the dark by the forge at the top of the lane, not coming closer, not going in. Gran saw Pip see him.

"Harrow," Gran said, not loudly, and the smith went into his forge and shut the door.

Pip sat down on the step below her. Not beside. Below.

"Turn your head," said Gran, and looked at the burn, and pushed Pip's sleeve up to look at the bite, and felt along her ribs until Pip yelped.

"Who stitched that arm?"

"Wren."

"Badly." Gran let go. "Who strapped those ribs?"

"Wren."

"Better." Gran picked up the broth, looked at it, and put it down. "Ten years," she said.

"Ten."

"You're taller than your mother."

Pip had nothing to say to that. Up the lane the other three were going into Wren's house, and the light came on in the window, and Wren's wall would be in there with Gran's name on it, and Pip's mother's small underneath, and tonight Wren would paint a tick by one and not the other.

"Why did you give me a real dagger?" Pip said. "I was seven."

Gran didn't pretend not to understand. She'd never once pretended, in Pip's whole life. She looked down the lane toward the square, where the feast gate had been, and she told it the way she'd have told a watch report: flat, in order, nothing left out that mattered and nothing put in that didn't.

"The tithe was short. I was told at the weighing what that meant for everyone who sat down to eat. I told you the tunnel story the week before, because you never in your life went anywhere without an audience, and you'd take the others. I packed you my dagger because I didn't know what was down there."

That was four. Pip counted.

"Who told you?"

"Not mine to tell."

"That's what Hob said."

"Then Hob's got more sense than I gave him credit for."

Pip looked at her hands. "You turned your back," she said. "At the gate. You shouted at the miller's boy for climbing the fountain, and you turned right round, and Pell turned too, and I walked straight past." She heard her own voice go up. "That was on purpose."

"Twenty-six years on that gate," said Gran. "That was my last watch, that feast, and I turned my back on it." She said it as if it were a fact about the weather. "So you could get past."

"You chose me."

"I chose you."

"Over Tilly Rowe. Over the Pell twins. Over the Stroud boys, and the Sedge boy who played Elder Vane in the pageant, and—" Her voice cracked. "They're at a *table*, Gran. All of them. Every child in the Vale. Being fed sweets. Being *kept*."

Gran's face didn't change. But she was quiet for a long time, and Pip, who had spent her life not looking at her, made herself look, and saw that Gran had known that too, or something like it, for ten years asleep and a hundred nights before.

"A few children wandering off at a feast is mischief," Gran said at last. "A dozen's a search, and the search comes before the meal." She looked at Pip. "I chose you over every one of them. I'd do it again. I'd do it to you again."

No sorry. Gran had never said sorry for anything, not a slap, not a burned supper. Pip had always thought it was because Gran was never wrong. It wasn't. Gran had already done the sum, every time, before she did the thing, and sorry wasn't in it.

"I thought it was me," Pip said. "The tunnels. I thought that was the one thing. I found them, and I took everybody, and that's why we're alive. I thought that was mine."

"It was yours. You did it."

"You *made* me do it."

"I told you a story," said Gran. "You did the rest. You were always going to be at the front of something, chick. I only picked what."

Pip got up. Her ribs hurt. Gran watched her get up and didn't help, the way she'd never helped her up in her life, because you got up yourself or you stayed down.

"Wren'll want you for the meeting," Gran said. "Go on."

Pip went. Halfway up the lane she stopped, and turned round, and Gran was still on the doorstep in the blanket, watching her the way she watched the gate. Pip looked back at her. It was the first time in her life she'd done it on purpose.

Gran didn't look away either. That was all. Neither of them said anything, and after a while Pip turned round again and went on up to Wren's, humming without knowing it, three notes falling and a little run going up.

### 3.13

*Hollowmere, the inn kitchen. The next morning.*

Marta Fenn was jointing a hen when Tobin came in, and she didn't stop.

"You've a face on you," she said. "Sit down or go out. Don't stand in my door."

He stood in her door. The kitchen was warm and smelled of onions and new plaster. She had her sleeves rolled to the elbow and the cleaver going, *chock*, *chock*, like the axe in the dark outside Wren's house on the nights he couldn't sleep.

*Don't tell her.* It would have been easy. He'd done it to her face in the side vault, and she'd said *Is that so*, and known. *We didn't see him.* It would come out smooth as a planed board.

"We found Abner," he said.

The cleaver stopped.

"He's down there. He's one of them, the masked ones. He's the one who whistles." Tobin made himself keep going. "He leads them. He took the mask off on the ninth floor and we saw his face. He serves so the boys sleep sweet. That's what they do. That's what they get for it. He told me not to tell you."

Marta looked down at the hen. She put the cleaver down beside it, carefully, the way she'd laid the ladle on the cloth.

"I knew the tune," she said. "When the girl hummed it. He whistled it to the oxen thirty years. He whistled it to me." She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, front and back. "You're a better liar than that, Tobin Marsh. So it's true."

"It's true."

"Thank you," said Marta.

It went into him like a nail. She meant it. She picked up the cleaver again and went on jointing the hen, and he stood in her door, and he understood that she had thanked him for the first true thing he'd said to her, and that she'd been waiting for it since the side vault, and that it was worse than anything she could have said about Nell.

### 3.14

*Saltwake, the weigh-house steps. Dusk, the same day.*

They were putting up bunting across the quay for the light, and Pip sat on the weigh-house steps with twenty years of somebody else's sins on her knees, and read.

The pages had dried stiff from two days against her skin, but the numbers were there, and Wren's slate beside her with Wren's neat workings. Pip didn't need the slate any more. She could see the difference between *Landed* and *Shown* without the sum, the way Gran could see a short sack.

Agathe Voss's lamp had gone up into the new lamp room that afternoon, with Agathe on the gallery swearing at everyone who touched it. Tonight she would light it. Half the town was out hanging flags off the sheds, and someone had found a fiddle.

Gideon Hask came down the quay slowly, in his blue coat, and sat on the step two above her.

"You read figures now," he said. "Who taught you?"

"Wren."

"Clever girl, the Hale girl. Her mother was cleverer." He looked at the page on her knee, upside down, and didn't need to turn it round. "That column's mine," he said. "The neat one. Ivo wrote it, but it's mine. I told him what to write, and I signed under it every autumn for nineteen years."

Pip didn't look up. "Why are you telling me?"

"Because you'll have it worked out by Thursday," said Gideon, "and I'd sooner you heard it from someone who was there." He folded his big hands between his knees. "We had a hungry year in thirty-one. Your gran will remember. The deep wanted a quarter. A quarter of nothing is still nothing, girl. So I kept two books, and the barges went downriver, and the coin came back, and nobody in this town buried a child that winter. Not one."

"And the year after?"

"The year after, I kept two books because it had worked." He was quiet. "And the year after that because I'd a new roof. I knew what the oath said. I swore to it at the Hearth. I thought, every town's at it. One more won't tip it."

"It tipped."

"It tipped," said Gideon Hask.

The fiddle started up across the quay, a reel, badly. Pip turned a page. Her hands were steady. She was surprised by that.

"What do you want?" she said.

"Burn it." He nodded at the pages. "Not for me. Look at them." He meant the quay: Teague on a ladder with a string of flags, Maud Tiller counting the lanterns twice, Dorrit on the step of the reeve's house with her hands in her lap, watching him. "Ten years asleep, and they've woken to nothing. Their boats are gone. Their children are gone. They need one thing to be true, and what they want to be true is that it wasn't *them*. That it was the deep, or bad luck, or Hollowmere. Let them have that. Let them have a reeve."

"And you get your house on the quay."

"I get to watch the boats come in." His voice went rough. "That's all I ever wanted, girl. Every one of them home."

Pip folded the pages. She thought of Gran at the gate, tapping the boy with the loaf on the shoulder and letting him go. *I count everybody. Telling on them isn't always.* She thought of Gran on the doorstep last night, saying *I'd do it to you again*, as if it were nothing, as if it were the weather.

"I'll think about it," she said.

"That's all I ask." He put his hand on her shoulder as he got up, the way he put it on everyone's, and went down the quay toward his wife.

Pip sat on the step. She didn't open the pages again. She said it over to herself instead, under her breath, the way Gran had taught her to learn a watch report: what he'd said, every word, in order, nothing left out. *That column's mine. The neat one.* Twice through. Three times. *I knew what the oath said. I swore to it at the Hearth.* By the time the light came on across the water she had every word of it, and the fiddle was playing something slow.

### 3.15

*Saltwake, the quay. The same evening.*

The light came on at full dark, and the whole town cheered, and Wren watched Pip climb onto a fish crate.

It came on low and yellow and grew, and the lens began to turn, and the beam went out across the estuary and came round over the faces on the quay. Teague Pellow wept openly into his hat. Up on the gallery Agathe Voss stood in the glare with her arms folded and said, loud enough for the quay to hear, "There. Now you can all see each other," and went inside.

Pip was standing on the crate when the beam came round again. It lit her up like a saint in a window.

"Gideon Hask," said Pip, in a voice that carried to the end of the quay, "told me this today, on the weigh-house steps. I'm going to tell you what he said. Every word. He said: *That column's mine. The neat one. Ivo wrote it, but it's mine.*"

The fiddle stopped.

Wren could have stopped it. She was three paces away. She could have taken Pip's arm and pulled her down off the crate, and Pip would have fought her, and it would have been ugly, and it would have been over. She stood where she was. She told herself afterwards that she'd been too surprised to move. That wasn't true. The true thing was smaller and worse: she stood there and listened to Pip read out Saltwake's sins to Saltwake, and the feeling in her chest was relief. Not her mother's book. Not Hollowmere's casks. Someone else's.

Pip didn't miss a word. Flat, in order, the way Gran gave a report. *We had a hungry year in thirty-one.* *I'd a new roof.* *I knew what the oath said.* *One more won't tip it.* Her voice rang off the sheds, beautifully, like a bell, and her face in the turning light was alight with something Wren knew. She'd felt it on the dais in the feast hall, with the words tasting of iron in her mouth: the joy of saying the thing nobody wants said, and being the one who says it.

When Pip got to *Let them have a reeve*, somebody laughed. It was a terrible sound.

Ivo Sallow stood up. He'd been sitting on a bollard at the edge of the crowd, alone, where the town had left him since he came up. He took off his spectacles and wiped them and put them back.

"It's true," he said. "Every figure. I can give you the years."

Somebody spat on him. Then several people did. Ivo stood there and let them, with his hands at his sides, and then the crowd went past him, round him, as if he were a post, toward the reeve's house.

Gideon was on his step with Dorrit. He didn't run. He stood with his hand on the door frame and watched them come, with the face of a man watching the boats come in.

It was quick and it was ugly, and Wren didn't see most of it, because the crowd closed. She heard it. A noise like a sack of wet sand hit with a spade. Dorrit screaming his name. And then Teague's voice, roaring, and Kip's, and the crowd being pulled apart a man at a time, and Teague standing over the reeve with his arms out and blood on his knuckles that might have been anybody's, shouting, "*Enough. Enough.* He's a man. He's *our* man. Enough."

Gideon Hask lay on the cobbles of the quay with his leg bent wrong below the knee.

They put him on the river road that night with a stick and a bundle, and the town watched a lame man in a blue coat go away down the white road in the moonlight, and nobody walked with him. Dorrit didn't go. She stood on her own step with her hands folded and watched him out of sight.

By morning the town had taken down the reeve's house, plank by plank, bow window and all, and carried it off for their own sheds. Nothing on the plot but a doorstep.

Pip came out of the crowd at some point in the night with her knuckles skinned raw. Wren didn't see how. She didn't ask. Pip wouldn't have known what to tell her anyway; Wren could see it in her face. She didn't know if she'd been pulling somebody off or getting in.

### 3.16

*Wren's house, Hollowmere. Late that night.*

Pip came in last, and Ash saw her hands before he saw her face.

"You're late," said Tobin.

"I was helping."

"Helping who?" said Ash.

"Saltwake." She shut the door behind her. "With its civic duties."

"They broke his leg, Pip," said Tobin.

"I didn't break anything." She sat down without taking her coat off. "I read. Out loud. Wren taught me. Blame her. Every word of it was true."

"That's never been your problem," said Ash. And then, because it was true and he couldn't help himself: "You did it beautifully, though. Like a bell."

"Don't," said Tobin.

"I'm not encouraging her. I'm describing her."

Tuppence got up from his place by the lamp and walked the length of the table and lay down on Pip's hands, both of them, the skinned knuckles under his grey belly.

"Even the cat's on your side," said Pip.

"The cat's on the side of whoever's warm," said Wren.

There was a new column on the wall, painted the night after the piers with Wren's stitched hand slow on the brush: *At the table.* Twenty-three names had been moved there one by one. The Pell twins. The Stroud boys. Tilly Rowe. Tonight there was a fresh tick in the main list by *Odile Ostrander*, and under it, small, a first name with no tick and no need of one.

"Gran's home," said Pip, after a while, to the table.

"I know," said Wren.

"She told me why she packed the dagger. She said she'd do it again. She said she'd do it *to me* again." Pip laughed. It didn't work. "So that's nice. It's nice to be wanted."

Nobody said anything. Ash slid his cup across to her.

"It's Kip's," he said. "It's terrible."

"Everything Kip makes is terrible." She drank it anyway.

Tobin was looking at the window, at the dark road down the valley. "He'll be on the river road by now," he said. "Gideon. Somebody should've—"

"Should've what?" said Wren.

"I don't know. Walked him to the edge of town. So he didn't go alone."

"He sold the grain that would've—" Pip started.

"We all know what he sold," said Wren.

Ash looked at the new column. He thought about a long table at the very bottom of everything, and the voice in the scree that had answered him last night when he went up there with his hands cold and asked, for the first time, a question he hadn't wanted the answer to. And he said, before he could stop it, "The Pell twins are sat together. At the table. Somebody thought of that."

He heard it as it left him. He hadn't meant to give it away.

Pip looked at his hands round his cup, and he knew she knew they weren't cold. Ivo had never said a name. Ash watched her know it, the way she'd known two summers ago with her face gone white, and waited. She'd said a true thing tonight on a fish crate, and a man had gone down the river road lame for it.

Pip drank Kip's terrible wine and put the cup down.

"That's good," she said. "That they're together."

Wren looked from one to the other and didn't ask. Tobin hadn't heard; it had been on his bad side. Ash sat with his warm hands round his cup and decided what he felt was gratitude, and knew it was partly fear, because now it was hers to keep, and he'd seen tonight what Pip did with things she kept.

Wren got up and took her brush and went to the wall. She didn't paint anything. She stood there with the brush in her stiff hand in front of the new column until Tobin got up and took it out of her fingers, gently, and put it back in the jar.

"Same time tomorrow," said Tobin.

"Where else would we go?" said Pip.

Afterwards, every time they went down to Saltwake, Pip went along the quay the long way round: past Dorrit's door, where Dorrit said *good morning* to her in a voice like a well-kept ledger and never once smiled, and past the bare plot with nothing on it but a doorstep. Nobody built on it. Nobody even sat on the step. Pip walked past it every time, and Ash never once saw her look away.
