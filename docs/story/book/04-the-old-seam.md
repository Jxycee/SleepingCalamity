# Chapter 4: The Old Seam

*Status: draft, for user annotation.*

### 4.1

*Saltwake, then the ore road to Cinderholt. The end of winter.*

The ore road went up out of Saltwake like a rope somebody had dropped down the hill and never come back for, and Ash walked it two hundred yards ahead of the others, because he'd been up the scree every night for a month and he was afraid it showed.

It didn't show. He'd checked in Wren's window. It was only a face, a bit thinner. But Pip knew, and Pip was behind him, and he could feel her knowing between his shoulder blades like a thumb.

Odile had seen them off at the bottom of the gate lane in her watch-cap, looked at the four of them one at a time, and said, "Something's turning over up there. You'll start warm." Pip had walked past her without stopping, and at the corner stopped and looked back, and Odile had looked back too. Ash filed it, the way he filed everything, and was ashamed of the filing. At Saltwake, off Kip's barge, Pip went the long way round the quay, past the bare plot. She went that way every time now.

The road climbed. The estuary went grey and small behind them, and the hills went from brown to rust to a red so deep it looked painted on: cliffs the colour of a fox, layered like slates, with snow in the creases. Two worn grooves ran down the middle of the road where the ore carts had gone for sixty years.

"I can't feel my hand," said Wren, behind him.

"Put it in your coat," said Tobin.

"Then I can't feel my coat."

Ash had sewn the hand. He knew what the cold did to seven stitches across the knuckles. Tobin was walking beside her with his shoulder where the gap used to be, and every so often, without a word, he took her hand and put it under his arm against his ribs, and she let him. Ash watched that happen three times between two milestones, decided it was none of his business, and went on thinking about it for a mile.

He and Tobin were all right now. Since the slipway they'd said about thirty things to each other, all useful, none about Wren's kitchen. *Mind that.* *Pass the rope.* *Shut up.* Ash had found he could live on that. He'd lived on less.

Cinderholt came round a shoulder of the cliff all at once, and Pip said, behind him, "Oh," in a voice he hadn't heard from her since the lighthouse.

It was built up the face of the cliffs in terraces, like seats in a theatre, or it had been. Now it was doorsteps, row above row, climbing the red rock on ledges. Iron chutes ran down the cliff in long rusted gutters. Off to one side white steam hung over a pool where no snow lay. And at the very top of the cliff three round black holes went up into the sky, side by side, each as wide as a cart.

"Chimneys," said Ash. "They bored them. Through the rock."

"Nothing coming out of them," said Tobin.

At the foot of the cliffs, where the biggest terrace had been, there was the foundry. There was no building. The building had gone with everything else. But its furnaces had never been a building: they were cut into the cliff itself, three great arched mouths in the red rock with soot round their lips like a drinker's, and the flues behind them went up through the stone to those three holes at the top. That was why they were still here. You couldn't wipe a hole.

Ash walked into the left-hand mouth, and it was cold, and smelled of old ash and pigeons. The right one, cold. He walked into the middle one, and stopped.

It was warm. Warm the way a bed is warm when someone's only just got out of it. He rubbed the soot off the back wall, and under it there was metal: a bronze pipe, as thick round as a man, going out of the back of the furnace straight into the cliff. The join had been packed with clay, and the clay had cracked, and through the crack, very faintly, it breathed.

He knew that warm. He knew it in his hands.

"Ash?" Wren was in the mouth behind him.

"Furnaces get fed from the front," he said. "You put the fuel in the front." He took his hand off the pipe. "This one got fed from the back."

Wren came and put her stiff hand on the bronze, and he watched her face do the thing it had done over the light that wouldn't go out, a year ago, across a table. Then she took her hand away and put it in her coat.

"Something was feeding it," she said.

"Or it was feeding something," said Pip from the mouth, and nobody answered her.

Below the foundry two iron rails ran across the yard and into the cliff through a square black adit propped with timber gone silver. The tithe level. Rusted carts lay tipped beside it. And from inside, slow, like a coal blown on, came the red.

Ash stood in the yard with his hands warm at the end of winter, and didn't put his gloves on, because there was no point now, and nobody asked.

### 4.2

*The Glow Seams, floors 1 and 2: the pithead and the old workings.*

The first thing in the Glow Seams was a board full of brass, and Pip knew what it was before anyone told her, because it was a gate.

It was the lamp room at the pithead, low and long, with racks for lamps that weren't there and a counter worn to a dip by elbows. On the back wall hung a board as big as a door, studded with nails, and on the nails hung little brass discs, each with a number stamped on it. Hundreds. Some nails were bare.

"Checks," said Ash. "Pit checks. You take one when you go down and hang it back when you come up. If your number's still on the board at the end of the shift—"

"Somebody comes looking," said Pip. "Gran had a slate. Same thing."

As she said it a check near the bottom dropped off its nail, rang once on the stone, and lay there.

They all looked at it.

"It's the lanterns again," said Wren, writing. "The floats. It's how many."

Pip didn't count them. She'd learned at the gate that you didn't count the ones still out. You counted the ones in.

Past the lamp room the rails went down into a cavern full of junctions, iron points and levers sticking out of the floor like the handles of buried spades. A stalled cart sat across the main line, loaded with red ore. On a crate beside it, stencilled in faded white, was a mark Pip knew: a pick crossed with a flame. She'd seen it carved over a sealed alcove on the fourth floor of the Undercroft.

"That's them," she said. "Wren, the alcove. It's this place."

Inside the crate, packed in straw, lay sticks wrapped in red paper with a cord out of one end.

"Don't," said Tobin.

"I haven't done anything."

"You were going to."

She was. They used one on a rockfall across the far tunnel, Ash lighting the cord with the steadiness of somebody who'd been waiting all his life to be allowed, and Pip's ears rang until the second floor. Behind the rock was a set of points, and Ash and Tobin pushed the ore cart through on the right line while Pip hauled the lever, and the cart ran away down the slope and broke a door at the bottom.

"Routing," said Wren, writing.

"Smashing," said Pip. "Same thing, more cart."

The second floor was dry-hot, oven-hot, so the sweat dried on her before it ran: old workings with charred timbers and red glow seeping out of the walls in seams like veins in a wrist. Ash went in front with his head on one side.

"It's coming up faster," he said. "The stir. Every room that's hot, it's faster. The Undercroft woke up when you made noise. This one wakes up when you're warm."

"Then I'll think cold thoughts," said Pip. "Gideon's plot. Dorrit's face."

Nobody laughed. She hadn't meant them to. She'd said it to see if it hurt, and it did.

The things here were made of the place. Slag-hounds came off the heaps, molten ore that had learned to walk like a dog, grey on the back and white in the cracks, and where they bit your boot the leather smoked. Lamp-heads stalked on legs of pick-handles, the flame in the glass turning to look at you. And in the narrowest gallery a swarm came out of the dark with a noise like a purse being shaken: brass tokens, hundreds, flying like moths with edges.

"Tallymen," said Ash, swatting. "Pay tokens. The guild paid the diggers in them."

One stuck in Pip's buckler. It wasn't brass. She worked it out afterwards: a silver penny, worn thin, with a ship on it. Saltwake's.

"Saltwake's coin bought Cinderholt's iron," said Wren. "It's all one stomach."

Pip put the penny in her pocket with Dun Harrow's chain link. She didn't know why.

They found the powder-man asleep in the powder magazine, which was so exactly where you'd expect a powder-man that Pip laughed out loud. He sat on a keg among kegs, arms folded, a dead pipe in his teeth: thirty or so, sandy, sunburned under the soot, with three fingers on his left hand. He woke when Tobin took the pipe out of his mouth, very carefully.

"Don't light that in here," he said at once, and then grinned. "Oh, *hello*. Ludo Fitch. Don't shake hands, you'll come up short." He waggled the three fingers. "That was a Tuesday. Wasn't the guild's fault. They said so in writing."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"Anywhere the guild can't see me." He looked round at the kegs as if saying goodbye to friends. "I opened the best seam in this Vale, you know. Ask the guild, they'll say they never heard of me. Ask them who went through to the warm. Best day's work I ever—"

The warm came up through the floor of the vault, and took him up mid-sentence, still grinning, and he shouted something down about a shed and a lock on it, and then the shaft closed.

Pip stood with her mouth open on a question. Six weeks ago she'd have had it out of him before the floor was warm. She shut her mouth. It was like holding your breath. She'd been holding it since a fish crate.

### 4.3

*Floor 3: the pouring floor.*

The iron was coming, and Tobin had a cart full of slag and the wrong set of points.

The pouring floor was a casting hall the size of the church, roofed in soot, and down the middle of it ran a channel cut in the stone, a yard wide. At the top end a furnace stood open with its tap knocked out, and out of it, slow and thick as honey, came molten iron, white at the heart and orange at the edges. The heat hit his face like an opened oven. It was going toward the far end of the hall, where a woman was asleep in a mould.

The mould stood upright in a pit at the channel's end, an iron husk in two clamped halves, the shape of a gun barrel and wide as a barrel of ale. In its open top, curled on her side like a kitten in a boot, a big woman slept with her arms folded. When the iron reached the pit it would fill the mould, and that would be that.

"Dam it," said Ash. "Slag. Fill the channel before it gets there." He was looking at the rails, a tangle crossing and re-crossing the floor, with carts of cooled slag on sidings and levers at every junction. "There. And there. Pip, the third lever. *No*, the other third—"

"There are *four* thirds—"

Tobin had a cart moving. It was the only thing he was good for here, and he knew it. The cart rolled and gathered and ran, and Pip threw a lever, and it went down the wrong line and tipped its slag neatly into a pit that already had slag in it.

"Next," said Ash, not looking up. "Tobin, the one by the pillar."

Slag-hounds came out of the heaps, and Wren stood on a cart and wrote at them, her stiff hand loosening in the heat, and the nearest set grey all through and fell over like a dropped poker. Tobin pushed another cart. This one Pip got right. It tipped its load into the channel just above the pit, and the iron came down on it and hissed and banked up behind it, white and furious.

"Another," said Ash. "It'll go over."

The third cart stood near the furnace, where the heat came off the iron in a wall, and wouldn't go: the rail under its front wheel was buckled. Tobin, with the hair on his arms crisping, put the axe-head under the wheel and leaned on the haft. The cart went over the buckle with a bang and ran. Pip's lever was right. The iron rose against the dam, glowed, slowed, and stopped, a black crust forming on it, sighing. Not a drop had reached the mould.

Tobin went down into the pit. The halves were clamped with iron pins knocked home with a hammer, and he looked at the pins and thought about knots.

"The pins," said Ash, from the edge.

"I know."

He hit the first pin out with the poll of the axe, short and sharp, and the second, and the halves of the mould came apart with a groan, and the woman inside lay on her side in the half-shell, and smelled of hot iron and lye soap, and opened her eyes.

She was fifty or so, broad as a door, with forearms like hams and burn scars on them in shiny islands. Her hair was cropped close as a man's. She looked up at Tobin, and then, before she'd looked at anything else in the world, at the axe in his hand.

"Who put that edge on?" she said.

"I did."

"Who forged it?"

"Dun Harrow. In Hollowmere. The head. Years back."

"Harrow." She sat up and took the axe out of his hand without asking, and looked down the cheek of it into the light from the cooling iron, the way Wren held a page to a window. "He couldn't sharpen a turnip. This is good steel badly treated. Somebody's been using it for ten years like he meant it." She looked at Tobin. "That you?"

"Yes."

"Hm." She gave it back. "Hilde Saxby. I made the iron in this town, and the guns that broke the rock, and if anybody calls themselves a smith in my hearing I'll want to see their hands." She looked down the hall at the dam. "Who did that? That's not stupid."

"Ash."

"Is he a smith?"

"No."

"Good." She heaved herself out. "The foundry. Under the chimneys, where it was. And Harrow can come and watch." It was the first time Tobin had heard a wish said like a threat.

The warm took her up still talking about a proper quench, and he found that he liked her more than anyone he'd met in a month.

Ash came down into the pit and looked at Tobin's hands on the haft: the old scar across the palm, soot, the hair burned off the wrist.

"You'll lose that hand one day," said Ash.

"Probably." Tobin put the axe on his shoulder. Then, because it was true: "Good dam."

"It was a good dam," said Ash, and it was the longest conversation they'd had since Wren's kitchen.

### 4.4

*Floor 4: the spring workings. Floor 5: the guild's deep store. Then the bank.*

Mother Bette Rook was asleep in a bath, and she had opinions about it.

She lay in a great stone bath gone dry, in her shift, with a towel over her knees, among springs that steamed red, and she woke the moment Wren touched her shoulder and looked up with small bright eyes in a big face.

"Hollowmere," she said. "I can tell by the boots. Shut that door, there's a draught." There was no door. "I wash people. The living and the dead. Forty years. You'd be surprised what you know about a town when you've washed it."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"The bathhouse. On the spring. And a bench outside for the old men; they'll sit there anyway." She looked at Wren's hand. "Who stitched that? Not bad. You've the look of someone who knows things and doesn't say them. I'm the opposite." Bette folded her hands. "Spring of forty-six I washed seven diggers. Ferrule's shift. Young lads, most of them. Red inside the mouth. Red down the throat. Like they'd drunk the furnace. Cooked from the inside like a pudding in a cloth." She said it the way Tansy Brook said things, cheerfully, to see your face. "I told the guild. The guild said heat-sickness, too deep, Ferrule's fault. Didn't tell anyone else." She considered. "Well. Didn't tell *many*."

The warm took her up with her towel held round her knees and her mouth still going.

Wren wrote it down. *PY 46. Seven diggers. Red inside.* Ten years held and spilled in a minute to strangers; and Wren had a box with a hasp on it; and they were the same kind of woman, only facing different ways.

The fifth floor was the guild's deep store, and the Bore was in it.

They heard it first: a deep regular thud through the rock, like somebody beating a carpet the size of a field. Then the gallery wall ahead bulged and burst.

It had been a gun: a great iron barrel, the kind Hilde said had broken the rock. The dream had given it a mouth of grinding teeth, and six short iron legs that dug, and on its back a fuse that burned and never burned down. It came out of the rock in a cloud of red dust, shook itself like a dog, and then its fuse flared and it *fired itself*. The whole beast leapt the length of the gallery like a ball out of a cannon, through the space where Pip had been standing, into the far wall, and through.

"It digs," said Ash. "And when it's in the open, it shoots itself at you."

"It shoots itself *at* you," said Pip. "I'd like that on my grave, please."

There was an ore cart on the gallery rails, points at either end. Ash was already looking. "It comes out where it's soft. Same holes. If there's a cart in front of the hole when it fires—"

"It hits the cart," said Wren.

It took four tries. The fourth time, Tobin was at the far points with the cart already rolling, and the lever wouldn't go. It stood a foot from the Bore's last hole, where the beast had come out red-hot, and the iron was the colour of a dull coal.

He took hold of it with both hands and threw it.

Wren heard it. She would hear it afterwards, sometimes, in the night: a sound like a rasher going into a pan. Tobin didn't make a sound himself. The points went over. The cart ran down the right line, and stopped dead across the mouth of the hole as the Bore came out of it, fuse blazing, and fired.

It hit a ton of ore at full speed, face first. The teeth jammed. The barrel split along its seam with a crack like the end of the world, and the beast lay twitching on its side with its legs digging at air, and Pip went in under the legs and put her dagger into the fuse, and it went out.

Tobin was standing by the points with his hands held out in front of him, looking at them as if they were somebody else's.

"Let me see," said Wren.

"In a minute."

"Now, Tobin."

He let her. The palms were white and the fingers blistering across the backs. She wrote a small cool mark on them, and the heat went out a little, and that was all she could do. Ash wrapped them in a wet shirt.

"You'll lose those hands one day," Ash said.

"You said."

"I'm saying it again."

Behind a strongroom door the Bore had dented on its way through, a man in a good coat was asleep at a desk with a ledger shut under his hand and his chin up, as if sitting for a portrait. He woke the way people wake who expect to be listened to: all at once, mouth already open.

"Who let you in here? This is guild ground." Then he took in the broken Bore, and his face rearranged itself, fast and smooth. "Osric Varn. Guild master. Elder." He straightened his coat. "If you've come about the shift, I want it known I warned Ferrule. In writing. Her crew went too deep. The guild cannot be held to account for men who will not be told."

Nobody had asked him anything. It was the voice of a man who had slept ten years with one speech in his mouth, practising.

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"The guild hall. With the doors polished. My chair at the head."

Wren watched him go up and thought, quite clearly, that she'd rather have left him in his strongroom, and was ashamed, and wrote down his name anyway.

The bank was through the strongroom's back wall: the yellow fire, the cold basin, the strongboxes, the hoist, and a pit-check board by the fire, every disc on it somebody still down there. Tobin held his wrapped hands in the cold basin. Wren watched his jaw.

"Home," said Ash, from the stair door, before anyone could ask. "It's hot below. Hotter every floor. It'll wake faster than we can walk."

"Home," said Pip, and looked at Tobin's hands.

"Home," said Wren, and noticed as she said it that her own hand, the stitched one, had opened all the way for the first time since midwinter, in the heat, without being asked.

### 4.5

*Cinderholt. The weeks after.*

The first thing Hilde Saxby did with the foundry was light it, and the second thing she did was shout at Dun Harrow, and Pip had a seat for both.

The foundry went up in a week round the three furnace mouths. Hilde stood in front of the warm one with her arms folded for a long time, and then had Ludo brick it up, mouth and all, and lit the other two with charcoal carted up from Saltwake, and when smoke came out of the chimneys at the top of the cliff, everyone in Cinderholt who was home by then came out onto their doorsteps and cheered.

Then Dun Harrow turned up one morning in the yard with his arms folded, the way Hilde folded hers, and the two of them stood on either side of her anvil like two dogs who had heard about each other for years.

"You'll be Harrow," said Hilde.

"I'll be Harrow."

"I've seen your edges."

"I've seen your guns," said Dun. "Didn't think much of where you pointed them."

Pip, sitting on a pile of pig iron in the corner of the yard with her chin on her knees, laughed out loud, and Dun looked at her and away, the way he always did, as if she were a light that hurt.

They fought for a fortnight, about quench and temper and whether Hollowmere had ever produced a blade worth the name, and in between Hilde did things to their weapons that made Wren write very fast. She folded a new strip of steel into Ash's hunting sword, and it came out of the quench with a pattern down it like water. She put a rim of pale hard alloy on Pip's buckler, and it rang when you hit it now, instead of thudding. She wouldn't touch the heirlooms themselves.

"That's not iron," she said, when Tobin held out the axe. "That's somebody's dead father."

"He's not dead. He's in the timber yard."

"Then it's somebody's living father, and I'm not putting my hammer on it. Bring me the iron you find down there and I'll feed it *into* them. Feeding, not cutting."

Dun Harrow said that was the first sensible thing she'd said, and they fought about that for a day.

Pip watched him, and didn't know why. His hands were big and freckled, the knuckles broad and red, and she looked at them once and then down at her own on her knees and had a feeling she didn't want, and went to the bathhouse.

Under the steam at Bette Rook's, Cinderholt talked. Pip sat in the hot water to the chin and listened. It was nearly as good as the gate. Ludo came in every evening and told anyone who'd listen about the best seam in the Vale. "Went through to the warm, we did. Like cutting into a loaf fresh out—"

"Ludo." That was Osric Varn, in the doorway in his good coat. "That'll do."

"I'm only saying—"

"Then stop saying."

Ludo stopped. He grinned at Pip across the water, and put a finger to his lips, and on his three-fingered hand that looked like a joke as well.

Pip held her breath under the water till her ears sang. She could have climbed out and asked Osric Varn, in front of the whole bathhouse, what the guild had gone through, and to what. She could feel how good she'd have been at it. She came up for air and didn't.

At night they went home on Kip's barge, and the meeting at Wren's went on as it always had, and after it Ash went up the scree. Pip watched him go from Gran's step, with Gran in the chair behind her not talking, which was how they talked now. Every night, head down, the way you'd walk to work. Every grey dawn, back.

"He's up there again," said Gran, one night. It wasn't a question.

"He likes the view."

"That boy," said Gran, after a long while, "looks at people like a man reading a gauge."

"You don't know him."

"I counted him through my gate for nine years," said Gran. "I know the ones who look at the gate and the ones who look at you. That one looks at the latch."

Pip didn't answer. In the morning Ash came down the scree path past Marta's kitchen window, and took his gloves off at the bottom, and flexed his fingers, and she knew his hands were warm, and she didn't ask.

### 4.6

*Floors 6 and 7: the assay house and the hot galleries.*

They went down from the bank on the checkpoint, and the first person they found was a girl who wouldn't go home.

Corra Varn was asleep in an assay house on the sixth floor, at a bench of little brass scales, with a notebook held against her chest. She was twenty, thin and sharp, with her father's straight nose and none of his coat. When she woke she held the notebook tighter.

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"Not in my father's house." No pause at all. "Anywhere else. A shed. The assay office, if there is one. Not his."

Then she looked past Wren, at Ash, and kept looking. He was used to people looking past him at Tobin.

"You're the one who looks," Corra said. "I can tell. The others look at me. You looked at the scales." She held out the notebook. "Take it. They're copies. Heat readings off the guild's gauges, every month, from forty-four to the end. I copied them at night." Her mouth went thin. "My father had the real ones burned. He didn't know about these. Nobody did."

Ash took it. It was warm from her chest.

"Why give it to me?"

"Because you'll read it," said Corra Varn, "and they won't," and the warm took her up, and she didn't wave.

He read it on the stairs: columns in a small, furious hand, month after month, everything going one way.

The seventh floor was the hottest place he had ever been. The rails ticked as they expanded. Every room, the stir came up under them like a tide under a door, and Ash counted it, and the rooms to the stair, and didn't like the sum. A lamp-head got its pick-legs round his bow arm and he fell with it and stabbed up under the glass with Jory's sword until the flame went out.

When it went out, the bow in his other hand was warm.

He'd had *The Survey* from it on the third floor, while Tobin pushed carts: Jory, twenty, and Wren's mother, laughing at something in the founders' tunnel with a chain stretched between them, and Ash had never in his life heard what they were laughing at. He'd thought that was the worst the bow could do.

And he was on the scree in early winter, above the treeline. He knew the shelf of rock; he'd know it in the dark. A stripe of bare stone where the snow wouldn't settle, a crack no wider than a knife blade, and kneeling with his ear against it, Jory, twenty, a survey slate under his arm.

Jory listened for a long time. His face didn't go interested. It went the colour of the snow.

Then he stood up and built a little cairn over the crack, flat stones, carefully, the way you'd cover a well, and scratched on his slate with a nail. *Bad air.* Then he was sick in the snow, twice, and walked away down the hill without looking back.

He was lying in a hot gallery with a dead lamp on his arm.

Three years ago, tracking a wounded ibex, he had found a stripe of bare rock where the snow wouldn't settle, and some loose stones on it. He'd moved the stones to see what was under them. He'd thought they were scree.

"Ash. Your eyes went."

"I know," he said. He didn't look at Pip, who was looking at him, and he went on to the stair carrying a guild's crime in his coat and a cairn in his head, and couldn't tell which was heavier.

### 4.7

*Floor 8: the breach. Then floor 9.*

It was still drinking.

The eighth floor was the end of the guild's dig: a long straight drift through red rock, with bronze pipes as thick as a man bolted down both walls, dozens of them, all running the same way. At the far end the pipes stopped at a wall.

The wall was alive. It glowed from inside, and it moved, very slowly, in and out, like the side of something asleep. The pipes went into it the way a farrier's tube goes into a horse, and from inside them came a long slow draw, like someone sucking marrow from a bone.

"They dug toward it," said Ash. "On purpose. They found warm rock and followed it down, and at the bottom they put a pipe in." He held up Corra's notebook. "That's what the furnace was. *Through to the warm.*"

"Free fire," said Tobin.

"Free fire. Endless iron. From forty-five."

Wren sat down on the rail with her back to the breathing wall and worked it, Ash reading the figures out, Pip holding the lamp, her stiff hand going down the columns like a finger down the tally. It was a simple sum. That was the worst thing about it. Every year the pipes drew off heat, and the deep had a little less of itself to digest with, and needed a little more. She set it against Ivo's ledger, the owed going up from a tenth to a half. She did it twice. She wanted it to come out a particular way. She could feel herself wanting it, the way she'd felt on the quay with Pip on the fish crate: *this one's theirs. Not ours.*

It didn't come out that way.

"It was always coming," she said. "The tithe. It was always going to fall short. Ivo's books said so. Every town was cheating and the hunger grew faster than any of them, and it was always going to cross." She closed the book. "This made it cross one harvest early. That's all. One year."

"One year," said Pip.

"The year we were children." Wren looked at the wall. "Without this, it's the feast after. We'd have been eleven, and ten, and nine, and eight. We'd have had one more autumn with them." She heard her own voice, flat, the way Odile gave a report. "Or we'd have sat down and eaten with everyone else. I can't do that sum."

She thought of five ticks in a margin, and her mother's hand, and didn't let herself think the rest.

Nobody could. Ash had his hand flat on the nearest pipe. He took it away and looked at his palm.

"There," said Tobin.

Off the drift an older tunnel went away into the rock, hand-cut, its timbers black and furred. Across its mouth was the shape of a door, and on it, burning dull gold, were letters. Wren knew most of them, from a collar and a trapdoor and a step. She read them with her lips moving, and they went into her like every word of the contract did, and stayed.

"It's a ward. Like the church. It's a lock that opens to a word, and the word isn't written here." She tried the ones she knew. Light. Spare. Not this one. The gold didn't change.

At the stair head behind them a single brass check hung on its nail, and hadn't dropped. Ash turned it over with one finger. It had no number. Somebody had scratched a letter into it with a knife point, quick and crooked. A J.

"He put a J on everything," Ash said. His voice was very calm. "His spoon. His boots. My bow. It's under the binding." He let the check go. It swung on its nail. "He's in there."

"Ash—"

"He's *in there*, Wren."

Tobin hit the door with the axe. He didn't ask. He swung with his wrapped hands and the axe rang off the gold like the bell had rung off the frame, and the letters flared, and under their feet the rock shuddered and the stir went up like a fire with the door opened. He swung again.

"Tobin. Tobin, *stop*—"

The bank was locked behind them and the only way out was down. They went down with the stir at a pitch she had never felt, into heat so bad the air shook, where somewhere ahead picks were ringing on rock, a whole shift of them, and a woman's voice shouted the strokes. They didn't reach it. Halfway down the first gallery the roof came in behind them, and then ahead, and the deep closed like a hand.

Wren woke on the pithead cobbles in the snow, with nothing in her pockets but the notebook, and the haul of two floors lying somewhere on the ninth in a satchel nobody could open. Pip was lying on her back laughing, and it didn't sound like anything good. Tobin sat with his burned hands in his lap. Ash stood at the mouth of the tithe level with his back to them, looking down into the red.

### 4.8

*Hollowmere, the lamp lane, then the high scree. The next night.*

Wynn Cobb lit the lamps on the evening after the wipe, the way he did every evening, and Ash stood at the bottom of the lane and watched him do it.

He told himself the lamp lane was on the way to the scree, which it was, if you went the long way round.

Wynn was a small round man of fifty with a pole and a little brass can, and at every lamp he stopped, and trimmed the wick, and lit it, and said something to it. Ash got close enough once to hear. *There you go.* *Nobody's going anywhere.* Seventeen lamps, from the bottom of the lane to his own door at the top, where the lamps started, and when the last was lit he stood under it looking down at the light he'd made, and Ash saw his shoulders come down.

He'd run, on the Long Night. When the lanterns went out in the square he had run for the town edge and been taken at the last doorstep, alone, and he told that to anyone who asked, very fast, before they could tell it to him.

Wynn saw Ash at the bottom of the lane, and nodded to him, and went in, and shut the door.

Ash went up the scree.

It was a clear night with a moon. Hollowmere lay below him as he climbed, smaller and smaller, and the lamp lane going up from the square like a string of beads. He lay down on the shelf above the treeline with his cheek on the stone beside the crack, where there had been a cairn once, and the warm came up against his face like breath.

"I know where he is," he said. "I know which door."

*Of course you do,* said the voice. *You found it. You always find things.*

It was the same voice it always was, warm and unhurried, like someone at the head of a table who had all the time in the world and was glad you'd come.

"It wants a word."

*It does.*

"You know the word."

*I know most words, Ash.* Fondly. *Your brother is asleep and not in pain. I told you so once, and it was true, and you cried. Do you remember? He's in the Old Seam where you stood. He's very close to you. He's been very close for a long time.*

"Tell me the word."

The voice was quiet for a moment, as if considering a dish.

*I'll tell you,* it said. *I'd like something for it. A small thing. You've taken a great many people from me this winter. You have all been very busy.* There was no anger in it. It sounded almost proud. *Give me one name you've already taken from me. Only one. And I'll tell you the word.*

Ash lay on the rock.

He'd known this was coming. He'd known it watching Wynn trim seventeen wicks; that was why he'd gone the long way round. He hadn't come up here to ask. He'd come to pay, and he'd walked past the price on the way to have a look at it.

He went down the wall in his head. He couldn't help it; it was what his head did. The way Wren went down a list. Nell. No. Hob. Odile. Hollis, Marta, Dun, Tansy, Merrin. Amos and the bell. Bram in his empty schoolhouse. People with people. People somebody would wake up and miss. He went down the names and weighed them, each one, like Corra at her little brass scales, and the thing that frightened him was not that he did it. It was how easy it was. How clear. It was the most interesting sum he had ever done.

"He hasn't got anyone," Ash said. "Wynn. He hasn't got anyone. He ran."

*He did.*

"He slept ten years and it didn't hurt him. You said they don't hurt. The ones who sleep."

*The ones who sleep sweet.*

"And I'll find him again. I'll go down and fetch him. Later. I'll bring him back."

*Of course you will,* said the voice, kindly, the way you'd say it to a child promising to tidy up.

He shut his eyes.

"Wynn Cobb," he said.

*Thank you,* said the voice. *Not taken, Ash. You understand that. I can't take. I can only have what's owed me or what's given.* A pause, warm as a hand on his hair. *Given.*

"Given," said Ash. "Tell me the word."

It told him. It was a short word. It was a name, a woman's name, and he'd never heard it in his life, and he said it back to himself twice so he'd have it.

Then he turned his head on the stone and looked down at Hollowmere.

At the far end of the lamp lane, at the bottom, by the square, the first lamp went out.

Not blown. It didn't flicker. It was lit and then it wasn't, the way a candle goes out when someone pinches it. Then the second. Then the third, a little closer, at an even pace. Like somebody walking up the lane in no hurry, stopping at each lamp the way Wynn had, and putting it out. Ash counted them. He couldn't help that either. Nine. Ten. The lamps went out up the lane toward the top, toward the house where the lamps started, and the dark came up the lane behind them like water up a slipway. Fifteen. Sixteen.

The last lamp, by Wynn's door, went out.

For a moment the window beside it was still lit, a small square of yellow. Somebody inside, by the fire, maybe. Maybe looking out.

Then the window went dark.

Ash lay on the warm rock with his eyes open. The lamp lane was a black line in the town below, and every other light in Hollowmere was still lit, Marta's kitchen, Wren's window, Tobin's. Nobody had noticed. Nobody would, till morning.

He got up. His hands were warm. He put his gloves on anyway.

### 4.9

*Hollowmere, the lamp lane, and Wren's house. The next two days.*

Wynn Cobb's door was barred on the inside, and his boots were by the bed, and the whole town decided he'd run.

Pip was there when Dun Harrow put his shoulder to the door. The bed was made and cold, his pole in the corner, a cup on the table with a skin on it.

"Out the back window," said Tansy Brook, with relish. "Ran. It's what he does. First sign of trouble, there goes Wynn."

"What trouble?" said Merrin.

"He'll have *thought* of some."

The back window's latch was on the inside. Pip looked at it, and nobody else did. Down the lane she opened a lamp. The wick wasn't burned down. It was pinched flat at the tip, the way wet fingers put out a candle. All seventeen.

Somebody said he owed them for lamp oil, and people laughed. By noon it was a fact. At dusk, when Amos rang the bell, the lamp lane stayed dark, and people walked up it with lanterns and grumbled. Wynn Cobb had run.

Pip went home and didn't say anything for two days.

It wasn't hard. That surprised her. It was like having swallowed something cold that sat under her ribs, beside the cracked ones, and didn't melt. She ate round it. She went to the meeting and made jokes, and they came out the right shape, and nobody noticed except Gran, who looked at her once over the broth and said, "You've a face like a short sack."

Ash came to the meeting and was kind to everyone. He'd been kind like that once before, three years ago, after the voice told him his brother wasn't in pain, and Pip remembered it, and wanted to hit him.

Tuppence wouldn't go near him. The first night the cat walked the length of the table, as he did, and came to Ash's place and stopped. His fur went up along his spine in a ridge, as if the collar were pulling it. He looked at Ash's hands. Then he went all the way round, the long way, and lay down on Tobin's bandages instead.

"What've you done to him?" said Tobin.

"Nothing," said Ash. "Cats."

"He's never done that."

"He's a cat. He's allowed opinions."

The second night Tuppence came in, saw Ash, and went out again, and slept upstairs on Nell.

On the third morning Wren caught Pip at the door before the others came, and shut it, and stood with her back to it.

"What is it?" she said.

"What's what?"

"Pip." Wren's careful voice. Then, because Pip had told her not to do the careful voice, she stopped doing it. "You've had a face for two days. Tuppence won't go by Ash. Wynn Cobb's lamps went out one by one, Merrin says, from the bottom of the lane up, like someone walking. Ash was out all that night. I'm not stupid. Tell me or don't, but don't make me watch you not."

Pip looked at her.

She'd kept it for Ash for two years, because it was his, and because she was proud of being the one who knew. All that time it had been a boy listening to a crack in a rock. A sad secret. Now it was a man in a cold bed.

"He goes up the scree," Pip said. "There's a crack. It's warm. It talks to him. It's talked to him for three years. I've known for two." She heard her voice going flat, Gran's report voice, and let it. "He went up the night after we got thrown out. I think he asked it the word. I think it wanted paying. I think Wynn's what he paid."

Wren's face didn't move. Pip watched the colour go out of it.

"The light," said Wren at last, very low. "Over the table. When I first— he put his hand in it. He knew it." She shut her eyes. "He *knew* it was the same."

"Same as what?"

Wren opened her eyes and didn't answer that.

"Does Tobin know?" she said.

"No."

"Don't tell him."

"I wasn't going to."

"No," said Wren, as if she were agreeing with something else, and opened the door, and went out to fetch water, and when she came back she was exactly herself, and put the kettle on. Pip had watched Gran put things away at the gate her whole life. She knew exactly what she'd just seen.

### 4.10

*The Glow Seams, floor 8: the Old Seam. A week on.*

They went down from the entrance, because the checkpoint was spent, and every floor, the deep closed sooner.

Nobody else noticed. Ash had counted the collapse on every floor since the lamp-keeper came home, the extra breath it gave you before the walls came in. Wynn's seconds. Now the count was short by exactly that much, every floor, like a missing step on a stair you've climbed a thousand times. He didn't say anything. He ran faster.

Floors one to five were new and the same. Pushing on from the bank was a quick vote. The eighth floor was the breach, the pipes still drinking, the gold letters on the door, the check with the J on its nail.

Ash walked up to the door. He could feel Pip behind him not looking at him, and Wren looking at Pip.

He said the word. A woman's name. He didn't know how it should be said; he said it the way the voice had, with the weight on the front.

The gold letters went out like the lamps had. All at once, pinched. And the door wasn't there. There was only an old hand-cut tunnel, going back into the dark, with the smell of cold stone coming out of it, the first cold Ash had felt in eight floors.

"How," said Tobin, behind him, "did you know that?"

"Guessed."

"You guessed a—"

"I'm good at guessing." He went in.

It was the founders' tunnel. He knew it in ten steps: the tool marks, the low roof, the smell. It was miles from Hollowmere, under another town, but the dream had made it out of what it remembered, the half-mile and the wall where four children had sat in the dark with a lantern that wouldn't go out. And against the flat wall at the end, with his legs out and a survey slate on his knees, was Jory.

He was asleep, a surveyor's chain coiled round him from his boots to his chest. It hadn't gone to stone. It was only chain. He looked twenty-one, which he was. He looked younger than Ash.

Ash knelt and lifted the chain off, loop by loop. Nobody had made it heavy. Nobody had needed to: there'd been the door. When the last loop came off he put his hand on his brother's chest, and felt it go up and down, and the bow on his back was warm. It was hot. It was hot as a hand.

And he was at the pickers' hut at the top of the orchard, in the low gold light of the feast evening, and he was tall, and his hands were big and brown and holding out a yew bow with green binding and a hunting sword in its sheath. They were Jory's hands. He was looking out through Jory's eyes, down at a boy of nine, thin, dark, all elbows, with his face turned up and lit, and the boy was grinning like an idiot, grinning so hard it must have hurt. *Here,* said Jory's mouth. *Since you're always stealing it.* And Jory's hand went out and ruffled the boy's hair, and stayed there a moment, longer than a moment, and the hand didn't shake. Then Jory turned round and walked down the hill, and didn't look back, and the eyes Ash was looking out of were stinging the whole way.

That was all. No reason. Only the walking away.

When Ash came out of it the yew had gone dark, nearly black, and the green binding was bright as new leaves, and the sword at his hip sat against his leg like a hand on a shoulder, and he knew their name the way you know your own. *Brother's Due.*

Jory opened his eyes, and Ash watched him work it out: the dark, the tunnel, the face in front of him that was the wrong age.

"Ashy?" Jory said. His voice was rusty. "You're tall." He frowned. "Why are you *tall*?"

"It's been a while."

"How long's a while?"

The warm came up out of the tunnel floor under him, and took him, before Ash could answer, and lifted him out of Ash's hands, and Jory went up through the rock with his slate still on his knees and his mouth open on a question, looking down, and called, "Ash? *Ash*—" and the rock closed.

At the stair head behind them, the brass check with the J dropped off its nail and rang once on the stone.

Ash knelt in the dead end of a tunnel that wasn't the founders' tunnel, with his brother's bow in his hands. Somebody close by was crying, quietly, and it took him a long time to understand that it was him.

### 4.11

*Floor 9: the deep shift.*

The shift-boss didn't try to feed them. She argued with them while her crew did it.

The ninth floor was a working face lit red, with Gleaners in rows swinging picks, steady as a clock, chipping toward something they would never reach. On a spoil heap in the middle, masked, an iron bar in her fists, a big woman shouted the strokes. When the four came in she turned her straw face toward them and only changed what she shouted.

"*Hollowmere*. Course it is. Come to look at the animals." Down the face, the picks stopped, and the Gleaners turned, and took plates from the spoil heaps, and came. "You lot think we dug down here for fun? Your town ate off our iron for five years. Your ploughs. Your cart tyres. Your pretty gate. And when the lads came up red in the mouth, *we* were the ones who went too deep."

Wren had her staff up, writing. A Gleaner put a plate of feast pie under her chin and she burned it out of the air. "We know what the guild said."

"You know *nothing*. Your mam watered the grain at your mill and hid it under your inn and you all sat at your fat tables and called us animals." The bar came round at Tobin and he took it on the axe-haft and went down on one knee. "Every town was at it! *Every* town! We were the only ones honest enough to *dig*."

It was the worst thing anyone could have said to Wren, and she found she had no answer for it, because it was true.

Pip had one. Pip went in under the bar while the woman was still shouting, buckler first, the new rim ringing on iron, and got her shoulder into the woman's belly. They went down the spoil heap together in a slide of red rubble. Ash shot two Gleaners off Tobin. Wren wrote, and wrote, until her stiff hand burned. And Tobin got up and put the axe through the straw, not deep, the way he'd split Ivo's.

Under it was a square red face, forty or so, with a broken nose set badly long ago and grey hair cut short with a knife. The black went out of her hands. They were a digger's hands, scarred across every knuckle.

She lay on the spoil with Pip kneeling on her chest and looked up at the four of them, and all the fury went out of her face at once, the way water goes out of a split bucket.

"Is he still on his engine?" she said.

Nobody understood. Then Ash did.

"The Foreman," he said. "Ebben. You're Ferrule. You're his—"

"Dagny. Shift-boss." She closed her eyes. "Is he still on it? Running?"

"We haven't been down yet," said Wren.

"He'll be running. I served so he'd sleep sweet, and what's sweet to my father is that bloody engine going round." Dagny pulled a brass shift whistle on a cord from her neck. "He's deaf on one side and never in his life stopped for a man shouting. But he stopped for this. Three short. End of shift." She held it out to Pip, of all of them.

Pip took it.

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

Dagny Ferrule looked at her for a while, as if the question were stupid.

"A hole to dig," she said, "and somebody honest to dig it for." She laughed. It wasn't much of a laugh. "You don't know anybody like that, do you?"

The warm came up through the spoil and took her. She went up the way the others had, but she didn't call down. She looked at Wren the whole way, and Wren made herself look back.

### 4.12

*Floor 10: the turning pit.*

The engine came round the pit for the third time, and Tobin saw the man in the cab was smiling.

The tenth floor was a great round shaft dropping into red, and round its lip, on a ledge cut in the rock, a single loop of track, with points every quarter-turn where spurs ran off into the dark. On the track, going round and round with no end to it, was the engine: a squat iron boiler on six wheels dragging a tail of ore carts, stoked with the breath till the firebox glowed white through every seam, so fast its wheels screamed on the bends. Nobody had ever told it the shift was over. Every lap it threw a car off its tail at them, spinning, full of red slag.

And in the cab, welded in, sat a grey-haired man in a driver's cap, asleep, with one hand on the regulator and an expression of perfect happiness.

"The charges," said Ash. He was flat on the ledge, looking at the spurs. "The red crates. On the spurs. If it goes down a spur, it goes over them—"

"Then switch it," said Tobin.

"I'm *switching* it, it won't *slow*, it goes past before the points—"

Tobin's bandaged hands hurt every time he closed them, and he closed them on the axe and split the next car that came off the tail, slag and all. Wren was writing cold onto the rails. Ash threw a lever and missed. Threw it again and missed.

"Pip," said Wren. "*Pip*. The whistle."

Pip had it in her mouth already.

She blew. Three short, high and hard, across the pit. *End of shift.*

In the cab, as it came screaming round the bend, the sleeping man's hand eased back on the regulator a finger's width, the way a man eases a lever after forty years, without waking. The engine slowed. Not much. Enough. Ash threw the third points, and the engine went down the spur into the dark with its stack roaring, over the red crates.

The ledge jumped under him. Fire came back up the spur in a wall, and out of the smoke, on its side, grinding, came the engine, blown off the rails and sliding across the ledge toward the lip of the pit.

It stopped with its front wheels over nothing.

The cab hung out over the shaft. The man in it was still welded to the seat, still asleep, still smiling. The whole thing tilted, a hair's width at a time, like the tide wall in the bay.

Ash was on it before Tobin could say his name, along the boiler on his hands and knees and down into the cab. Tobin hooked an arm through the frame and got Ash by the belt, the way he had on the slipway, and his burned hand screamed and he held on.

"The weld," Ash said. "At the seat. I can't—"

"Move."

Tobin leaned out past him over the red, with Ash holding *his* belt now, and set the axe-edge on the seam of the weld where the iron seat met the iron floor, light, the way you score a loaf, and then hit it with the poll, short and sharp. It cracked. Again. The engine tipped another hair. Ash had the man under the arms. Tobin hit the weld a third time and it went, and the seat came free, and they fell back across the footplate together, three men in a heap, and the engine slid off the lip of the pit with a long iron groan and went down.

It didn't hit anything. It didn't need to. The warm came up out of the shaft to meet it.

The grey-haired man opened his eyes. He was sixty or so, wiry, with his daughter's square face under the soot. He looked down into the pit where his engine had gone, and his face crumpled.

"Is it end of shift?" he said. Then, much too loud, the way deaf men talk: "Speak up. I'm deaf this side."

"So am I," said Tobin.

The old man looked at him, and laughed, a cracked delighted sound.

"Ebben Ferrule," he said. "Where's my engine?"

It came back up the pit slow, on the warm, turning over in the red air like a leaf in a pool, carts and all. Ebben Ferrule held out his arms to it as if to a horse, and the warm took him too, and they went up together, the old man shouting down where he wanted it. Rails. A line running. Where he could *hear* it.

Then it was quiet. Tobin lay on the ledge with his hands bleeding into the bandages, and started, despite everything, to laugh. Ash got up and stood at the lip, looking down after the engine.

Then somebody spoke on Tobin's left. His bad side, where everything came through a wall. This came through as if there were no wall at all, close as a mouth by his ear: a man's voice, dry, courteous, a little amused, from the dark beyond the pit where nothing was.

"You have your mother's way of standing, boy," it said. "Tell Hester I asked."

Tobin rolled over with the axe up. There was nobody. Ash had turned at the lip, white, looking at the dark. Wren had her staff up and nothing to write at. Pip had the whistle in her fist.

"Who's *there*?" Ash shouted. His voice cracked on it like a boy's. "Who *is* that? *Who's there*?"

The dark didn't answer. It had said what it came to say.

### 4.13

*Hollowmere, the Calder house. That evening.*

Jory was sitting on Ash's doorstep when they came up the lane, and he got up, and laughed, and picked Ash up off the ground.

He did it the way he had when Ash was six, arms round his middle, though Ash was taller than him now and it made Jory's face go red. He put him down and held him at arm's length.

"Look at the *size* of you. Who said you could get old?" He was laughing. Then he wasn't. "Why are you crying? Ashy. What's the matter?"

"Nothing."

"You're crying on your own doorstep. That's not nothing." Jory looked past him at the house. "Is this ours? Who built this? That wall's out by a hand."

"I built it."

"Well, it's terrible." He put his arm round Ash's neck. "Where's Mam?"

The others had stopped at the bottom of the step. Ash heard Wren breathe in.

"We haven't found her," he said.

Jory's arm didn't move. "Not found her where?"

"Anywhere. Not in the Undercroft. Not Saltwake. Not here. She's on Wren's wall." He made himself go on. "We'll find her. There's other towns."

Jory was quiet for a long moment. Then he took his arm back and sat down on the step, and Ash sat beside him, and the others, without saying anything, went on up the lane to Wren's, and left them.

It was going dark. The lamp lane, two streets over, was dark already. Jory didn't know to look at it.

"How'd you get me out?" Jory said at last. "There was a door. I remember a door. I remember going to sleep at the table, and then a door, and somebody shut it, and I couldn't—" He stopped. "How'd you open it?"

"It wanted a word."

"What word?"

Ash told him.

Jory went still. Not like the others went still. Like a man on a ladder who's felt it move.

"Where did you get that?" he said.

"It was the word."

"Ash. Where did you hear that name?"

"Whose is it?"

Jory looked at him for a long time. Then he looked down at his own hands.

"Gran's," he said. "Mam's mam. Bettany." He said it carefully, as if it were the first time in years. "She died the winter before you were born. You never knew her. She was Bettany Vane before she married."

Ash didn't say anything.

"*Vane*, Ash," said Jory. "Elder Vane. The stone by the churchyard gate. The pageant with the wool beard. He was her father. He went down with the first tithe when she was a girl and never came back, and they made him a saint, and she never once in her life went to the pageant." He rubbed his face. "That makes Mam his granddaughter. That makes us—"

"His great-grandsons."

"Everybody old knows. Amos knows. Odile Ostrander knows. Nobody says it, because the last person who said it to Mam at the pageant got a jug thrown at him." Jory tried to smile. "She told me when I was sixteen. Made me swear not to tell you."

"Why?"

"She said you'd go looking." He looked at Ash sideways. "She said you were the kind who'd go looking for the bottom of a thing and not come back up till you'd found it. I said, he's six, Mam. She said, wait."

Ash thought about a stone by the churchyard gate he'd touched all his life, and been glad, once, that it was cold. He thought about a door under another town locked with his dead grandmother's name, and a voice by a pit saying *Tell Hester I asked*, and who in the whole of the deep could have known his mother's name, and her mother's, and how a man would stand.

He followed it down as far as he could, and at the bottom there was a sixty-year-old grave with no body in it, and he couldn't get further than that. He put it down. He stepped over it, the way Pip had stepped over the thing on the stair.

"You never asked," said Jory. "That's the funny thing. You asked everything. Why the sky, why the lake freezes from the top. You never asked about us."

"I'm asking now."

"Now you know." Jory stood up and stretched, and winced. "I'm stiff as a gate. Listen. I want to go back on the survey. I want a hut up on the scree. There's a good spot above the treeline where you can see the whole valley." He stopped. "Not by the bare rock, mind. There's a crack up there. Warm. Bad air. I put stones on it, years back. Don't you go near it."

Ash looked at his brother.

"I won't," he said.

### 4.14

*Hollowmere, the inn kitchen, then the timber yard. Three days on.*

Marta told him about Wynn while she was gutting a fish, and she didn't stop for it.

"His neighbours say he didn't run," she said. "Bram's opposite, and Bram couldn't sleep. The lamps went out one by one from the bottom up, at a walking pace, and then Wynn's window." *Chock.* The head came off the fish. "And I see the scree path from this window when I'm up for the bread. The Calder boy came down it at dawn that morning. And the next week his brother comes up out of a hole in the ground." She looked at Tobin at last. "I'm not saying anything. I'm telling you what I saw."

Tobin went to find Pip.

She was on the watch-house step mending a strap, and she looked up and saw his face, and he saw hers. He'd known that face since it had milk teeth in it. She opened her mouth to lie and he watched her try, and she couldn't. Not to him.

"Pip."

"Don't," she said. "Tobin, don't. Please."

"How long?"

"He's been going up the scree three years. I've known two." Her voice broke. "The other thing, a week. I didn't— I wasn't—"

"Does Wren know?"

Pip didn't answer, and that was an answer too.

Ash was in the timber yard with Hob, stacking boards for Jory's hut. Hob saw Tobin come in the gate and looked at his face and put down the board he was holding and went into the shed without a word, which was the most fatherly thing he'd done since he came home.

Ash straightened up. He looked at Tobin, and Tobin watched him know.

"Tobin—"

Tobin hit him.

One punch. Right hand, the burned one, because he didn't think, with ten years of everything in it, and he felt the nose go under his knuckles like a green stick. Ash went down in the sawdust. Tobin's hand went off like a firework, every blister bursting at once, and for a moment he couldn't see for the pain.

Ash didn't get up. He lay with blood running down both sides of his face into his ears, and looked at the sky, and didn't put up a hand. He didn't even try.

That was the worst of it. Tobin had wanted him to try.

"He had a *lamp* for every one of us," Tobin said. "He lit the lane so we could see to come home. He said goodnight to the bloody *lamps*, Ash."

"I know."

"You *traded* him."

"I know."

"For your brother."

"Yes."

Tobin stood over him. His hand was shaking. He thought about the trestles, and a small hand pulled out of his sleeve, and how fast he'd been, and how he had never once in ten years told anybody, and how this was different, because it was. It was. It had to be.

He walked out of the yard.

That night he went to the Calder house with a bowl of hot water and a clean rag and some of Tansy's salve. Jory opened the door and looked at him and looked at the bowl, and stood aside without a word.

Ash was sitting at the table with his face swollen black from the eyes down, breathing through his mouth. He looked up.

"Sit still," said Tobin.

He took Ash's head in his hands, the left hand and the wrapped right, and found the break with his thumbs, and set it. He'd set Pip's fingers, and Nell's arm, once, and a dog's leg, and his own wrist. It made a sound like a knuckle cracking. Ash went white and made a noise through his teeth and didn't move.

Tobin washed his face. He did it carefully, the way he'd washed Nell's when she was small. Then he sat down across the table, because his legs had decided, and they looked at each other.

"Why are you here?" said Ash thickly.

"Because you're on the team," said Tobin. "You're still on the team." He looked at his own hands. "I'm not losing anyone else. I've lost enough people. I'm not losing anyone else, not even you."

He got up and went to the door. With his hand on it, he stopped.

"And you go and get him," he said. "Wynn. When we're down there. When it's time. You go and get him first."

"Yes," said Ash.

### 4.15

*Cinderholt, the guild hall. Then Wren's house, Hollowmere. The next day.*

Dagny Ferrule walked into the guild hall at noon, through the polished doors, in her digger's clothes, and told Cinderholt what it had done.

Half the town had come to watch. Osric Varn sat at the head of the long table with his guild in their good coats down both sides, and when Dagny came in he started to stand, and Dagny said, "Sit down, Osric," in a voice that carried to the back wall, and he sat.

She didn't shout. That was what Wren remembered afterwards. Dagny stood at the foot of the table with her scarred hands at her sides and told it the way Odile gave a report. The warm rock in thirty-eight. The breach in forty-five. The pipes. The seven lads Bette washed in forty-six, and what the guild said about them. She named the man who signed every order, and looked at him while she did it. Corra Varn stood by the door with her arms folded and didn't look away from him either.

"I led the dig," said Dagny at the end. "I knew what we were doing by forty-seven. I kept doing it. I'm not asking you to forgive that. I'm handing myself to the guild for judgement. You can have me." She turned to go, and turned back. "And you can have *him*, while you're at it."

Then she walked out to the yard, where Ebben's engine was going round on its new rails with the old man leaning out of the cab, and stood by the track and watched it.

Pip was beside Wren the whole time with her jaw shut, and when Osric began to talk, smooth and quick, about orders misunderstood, Pip's mouth opened. Then it shut again. She had the Saltwake penny in her fist so tight, Wren saw later, it left a ship on her palm.

The meeting that night was the quietest in ten years. Ash sat with his face like a bruised plum. Tobin sat with his hand in fresh wrappings and didn't look at Ash, or at Wren. She knew why. Pip had come to her door white-faced after the timber yard and said, *He knows. He knows you knew.*

Tuppence came in, saw Ash, and stood in the door with his ridge up. Then, slowly, he walked round the long way, and lay down on Pip's hands.

"Even the cat's sulking," said Pip.

"The cat's being a cat," said Wren.

Nobody laughed. Ash looked at the wall.

"Since we're all telling things," he said. His voice was thick through the swelling. "The word on the door. It was a name. My grandmother's. Bettany. She was Bettany Vane." He made himself say it. "Elder Vane was her father. The stone. That's us. Jory and me. And Mam."

Pip stared at him. "The *pageant* one? The wool beard?"

"The pageant one."

"Well," said Pip, after a moment, in a voice that was nearly her old one, "that's nice. That's very grand for you. We've got nobility."

"It's them," said Tobin. He was looking at the wall, at three names in Wren's hand. "Nell in a coal bin, where I'd find her. Gran in a light, where Pip would. Now Jory behind a door that wants a word." His voice was quiet. "One of ours, every time. Laid out on the road where we'd come for them. Like bait."

Nobody answered. Wren was thinking about a voice by a pit that knew how Ash's mother stood, and Ivo Sallow on the kelp pier saying *There's a man who reads the page*, and being cut off. She got as far as that. Then she stopped, because the next step was a door, and she didn't have the word for it, and she didn't want it.

She got up and went to the wall.

*Wynn Cobb*, in her own hand, on the lane list, with a tick beside it she'd painted the night he came home. She took her knife off the shelf, and scraped the tick off. Slowly. The paint came off in curls. It took a long time, because she wanted it to. Nobody stopped her. When it was gone there was only a pale scar in the whitewash where it had been.

She stood there with the knife. In the black glass of the window she could see the three of them at the table, and none of them was looking at anyone else.

"Same time tomorrow," said Tobin at last, to nobody.

"Where else would we go," said Pip, and it wasn't a question.

Ash left first. Wren watched him from the window, because she wanted to know which way he'd turn at the bottom of the lane. Up was the church path and the scree. Down was home.

He went down. He stopped by the churchyard gate where the memorial stone stood, and put his hand on it, and kept it there a long time. Then he went on to the Calder house. There was a light in it. There had never been a light in it at night, not once in a year. Wren watched Ash stand outside his own door for a while, as if he weren't sure he was allowed in, and then go in.

She sat up till the grey to be sure, and nobody went up the church path all night. Somewhere above the treeline, she supposed, the crack lay in the dark with nobody's ear to it, breathing, warm, patient, and in no hurry at all.
