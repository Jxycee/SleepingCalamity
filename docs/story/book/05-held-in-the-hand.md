# Chapter 5: Held in the Hand

*Status: draft, for user annotation.*

### 5.1

*Cinderholt, then the drovers' road to Thistledown. Spring.*

Since the timber yard Tobin had walked on Wren's right, which put her on his left, and by the second day she'd worked out that it was his bad side, and that he'd chosen it.

He hadn't said anything. That was Tobin. For a fortnight he had fetched her water and split her wood and sat at her table every night, and answered when he was spoken to, all from the side of him that couldn't hear her. If she wanted to say something to him she would have to walk round. She hadn't.

The drovers' road went up out of Cinderholt from the top terrace, through a notch in the red cliff, and on the far side the world changed colour as if someone had turned a page. The rock went under grass. The grass went up in long green swells to a sky so blue it looked wet, with cloud shadows sliding across the meadows like boats. There were larks. Wren had forgotten larks.

Ash went in front. His nose had gone from black to yellow-green, and it whistled faintly on the climb, and he pretended it didn't. Pip walked behind with her thumbs in her pack straps, not humming, which was worse.

Wren carried the cost book in her coat, and the staff in her hand, and two things nobody knew.

The first was five ticks in a margin. The second was newer. Twice now the staff had gone warm in her grip and taken her somewhere. On the kelp piers at Saltwake: an orchard on a hill she didn't know, and her mother young, sleeves rolled, squinting down a plumb line at a row of saplings, laughing at something. Wren had never seen her laugh like that. On the seventh floor at Cinderholt: the kitchen at home, and a grey kitten with one good ear chewing the end of a strip of leather while her mother's hand wrote on it.

The others mostly told each other what their heirlooms showed them. Wren had put hers in the box with the ticks, and shut the hasp.

At the top of the first rise she stopped, and waited, and when Tobin came up beside her she walked round behind him and fell in on his right.

He looked at her. He didn't move back.

"You chose that side," she said.

"Which side?"

"Don't."

They walked. A lark went up out of the grass in front of them like a stone thrown upward, singing.

"You knew," said Tobin. "About Wynn."

"A week."

"You let me sit at your table a week and look at him."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She'd had a fortnight to find a better answer, and the true one was still the only one she had. "Because I knew what you'd do."

"And I did it."

"You did it." She looked at his right hand, in its fresh wrappings, and the knuckles that had gone into Ash's face. "And then you went back with a bowl of water and set his nose."

"Somebody had to."

"Somebody always has to," said Wren, "and it's always you, and you hate us for it."

He didn't answer that. A little further on he said, without looking at her, "You'd have told me. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before you had things in boxes." He shifted the axe on his shoulder. "You used to tell me everything. Now you just decide." He said it quite gently, which was worse than if he'd shouted.

The gap between her shoulder and his arm was the width of a hand. She didn't close it. He didn't either. They walked like that up onto the high meadows, and neither of them went back round to the other side.

Thistledown came over the last swell of grass all at once, and Pip said, behind them, "Oh, *no*."

It was blossom. The whole slope below them was orchard, ten years unpruned and grown into itself, and every tree was in flower, white and pink, so thick it looked like snow that had stopped on its way down. The noise came up the hill to meet them, a deep warm hum like a hand drawn round the rim of a glass. Bees, in the trees, in the grass, crawling in and out of the doorsteps.

Ninety or so doorsteps, in the grass between the orchards, with blossom drifted on them. And on a knoll above the town, a square of honey-coloured flagstones with the bases of pillars round its edge like the stumps of teeth: an abbey, and nothing standing but its step and its floor.

Behind the knoll the hill rose steep and green, and in it, where a cart road ran into the slope, there was a door.

It was made of comb. The mouth of the hill had been walled up with honeycomb, from the ground to the lintel, gold and lumpy and alive with bees, and from inside, through the wax, came the red. It glowed through the comb the way a lamp glows through a paper shade, warm and soft, and the bees walked over the glow as if it were a flower.

Wren knew this hill. She knew the angle of the orchard below it, and the shape of the knoll, and the line of the rows going down the slope. She'd seen them through her mother's eyes, down a plumb line, laughing.

"I can smell it from here," said Pip. Her voice was strange. "The honey. I can smell it from *here*."

Wren turned. Pip had stopped in the grass with her hand over her mouth, and her face had gone the colour of the blossom. Wren remembered a wooden spoon on the seventh floor of the Undercroft, heaped with feast honey cake, coming at Pip's shut mouth, and how long the honey had stayed on her cheek. Pip hadn't eaten anything sweet since. Nobody had said so. Wren had noticed, and put it away.

"You don't have to go in first," said Tobin.

"I always go in first."

"You don't have to."

Pip took her hand off her mouth. "Somebody always has to," she said, and looked at Wren, and Wren couldn't tell whether she'd heard.

### 5.2

*The Hive Choir, floors 1 and 2: the cloister and the root halls.*

The trick was breathing through your mouth, except then you could taste it.

The first floor was the abbey's cloister, the one that wasn't on the knoll any more: honey-stone arches round a square garden, carved bosses of bees and apples on the vault. Every door off the walk was sealed with wax, smooth as a candle end, and the garden was all flowers made of wax, pale yellow and perfect, and when Pip touched one it was warm and gave like skin.

She took her hand back and wiped it on her coat.

The smoker was on a stone bench by a dead skep, as if somebody had put it down for a moment and gone to answer the door. A copper can with a spout, dented, black inside, and a little leather bellows on the side. Pip picked it up, and squeezed it, and a puff of grey came out of the spout that smelled of burnt sacking and wasn't sweet. She could have kissed it.

"Bee-smoker," said Ash. "Calms them. Makes them think the hive's on fire, so they fill up on honey and go stupid."

"I know how they feel," said Pip, and puffed it at the nearest door.

The wax went clear like butter in a pan, and slumped out of the frame in a long slow drip. Beyond it a grate in the floor breathed cold air, and where the drips ran across the grate they set again in midair like icicles.

"Smoke softens it," said Wren, writing. "Cold sets it."

"Soft, hard," said Pip. "I can do soft, hard."

She was carrying the haul. She always carried the haul, ever since the Arcade; she liked being the one who walked up out of the bank with it and dropped it on Wren's table. By the second room it held a gilt cup, three lumps of a pale stone that rang, and a wax seal the size of a plate, all out of chests that were sticky inside. The smell came up off the satchel every time she moved.

The comb-drones came in the third room.

They were bees the size of dogs, furred, banded black and a gold so deep it was nearly red. Their stings were sickles of black horn, held forward under the belly the way a reaper holds his blade. And the noise they made wasn't a buzz. It was a long sung note, like a choir holding the last line of a hymn.

There were four of them. All four came for Pip.

Not for Tobin, who was nearest and biggest. Not for Wren, who was writing. They came over Tobin's head as if he weren't there, straight for Pip, and she got the buckler up and the first one hit it so hard her arm went numb to the shoulder and the new rim rang like a bell.

"*Why's it always*—"

"The bag," said Ash. He put an arrow through one of them in the air and it went down singing. "It's the bag, Pip. They want what's in it."

"There's a *cup* in it—"

"It's sweet." He shot again. "Everything down here's sweet. They're following the smell."

Pip stabbed one under the sickle and it fell on her, heavy and soft as a cushion, still singing. She lay under it with honey running down her neck and her mouth shut so hard her teeth creaked, and for a moment she was on the seventh floor of the Undercroft with an arm across her chest and a spoon coming.

Tobin rolled it off her. She got up. She didn't look at anybody.

"Give it here," said Tobin. He meant the satchel.

"I've got it."

"Pip."

"I've *got* it."

By the end of the floor they'd worked it out, because they had to. Wren split the haul into four bags, and the drones came at whoever had the most, and you could watch them change their minds in the air when Ash passed the cup to Tobin. So they passed things round as they went, like a hot potato, and nobody got mobbed twice running. Pip kept the most. Nobody said anything about it.

Windfalls came out of the walls, rotten apples the size of a man's head scuttling on little legs of root, and when you hit them they burst sweet, and every drone in earshot turned toward whoever the pulp had stuck to.

"I hate this place," said Pip, dripping. "Put that on the wall, Wren. Under my name. *She hated the bee one.*"

The second floor was roots: the orchard's roots made into halls, gold-lit and humming, knotted through walls of dripping comb, the floor so sticky every step came up with a sound like a kiss. Halfway along, in the knot of a root wall, a man was asleep.

An apple tree had grown through his coat, in at one sleeve and out at the collar, with blossom on it. He was fifty or so, brown and lean, with a face that even asleep looked as if it had been told something it didn't believe. Tobin cut the tree. He woke scowling.

"Silas Crewe," he said, the way you'd say it to a magistrate. He looked at the graft scar on the cut stem. "That's a Crewe's Late. Somebody's let it go to wood."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"The orchard. A cottage at the top of it, so I can see every tree. Not near the abbey. I don't want to hear a hymn or a sermon for the rest of my life." He laughed, a short bark with nothing in it. "Every feast they sang it. *Who eats at the table is held in the hand.*" He spat into the sticky floor. "I never believed a word of it. Not one. Abbess stood up and sang it every year and I stood at the back with my arms folded." He was quiet. "And then I sat down and ate the pie, same as everybody. Sit down, they said. So I sat."

The warm came up through the roots under him, and took him, still scowling, and he called down something about a ladder and the north rows, and was gone.

Pip stood looking at the place where he'd been.

"Same as everybody," she said. Then she wiped the honey off her neck with her sleeve, and it didn't come off, and she went on to the stair with the most in her bag, singing a little under her breath to keep from tasting anything, and found too late that it was the Gleaners' tune.

### 5.3

*Floor 3: the apiary in bloom.*

The comb was growing, and Ash could see the shape of it before anyone else, which was the only thing he was good for down here.

The third floor was a meadow, the south slope of Thistledown under a roof of gold, with rows of hives down it like white headstones and flowers so bright they hurt. And between the hives, the comb. It came up out of the ground in sheets while you watched, warm and pale, and as it rose it cooled, and as it cooled it set. The way they'd come in was already walled. Down at the bottom of the slope, under the oldest hive, something lay under a moving brown blanket.

"Path," said Ash. He was flat on his stomach at the top of a hive, his nose whistling. "It's growing from the top of the slope down. If we melt a line straight down, it'll close behind us, but it closes from the uphill side. We've got as long as it takes to set." He looked at the walls going hard behind them. "Not long."

"How long's not long?" said Pip, with the smoker.

"Run."

She ran, puffing like a little steam engine, and Tobin went through the slumping comb shoulder first, and Wren wrote small warm marks on the walls to slow the set, and Ash came last, watching them go hard on either side like a throat closing.

Halfway down, Wren's mark went into the wax and the wax didn't stop setting, and she stood with her hand still raised and looked at her fingertips.

"Wren."

"Coming." She put her hand in her coat.

They reached the old hive with the comb closing on their heels. The brown blanket was bees. Real bees, ordinary-sized, thousands, crawling over a woman who lay on her back in the flowers with her hands folded on her chest, and their humming was so even and so calm it was like hearing someone breathe in their sleep.

"Don't swat," said Ash.

"I wasn't going to swat," said Tobin.

"You were thinking about it."

Pip gave the bees one long slow puff. The blanket stirred, and shifted, and ran off the woman's face like water off a stone, and she opened her eyes. She was forty or so, freckled, sunburned, with straw-coloured hair cut short and a nose that had been stung so many times it had given up and stayed slightly pink. She looked at the bees first. Then at the four of them, without much interest.

"You smoked them," she said. "Who taught you to smoke a hive like that? You'll have half of them drunk." She sat up, and the bees went up with her and settled on her shoulders and arms like a shawl. "Clemency. Sister Clemency. Bee-mistress. I'd shake hands but they don't like it."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"My hives in rows on the south slope." No pause at all. "Away from the choir."

"There's no choir," said Ash.

"There's always a choir." Clemency looked at him properly for the first time. "What happened to your face?"

"A friend."

"Get better friends." She picked a bee off her wrist and put it back on her wrist. "We weighed the wax in with the honey. Did you know that? The tithe. Honey and comb, heavy as you like, every cart full to the board. You can't eat wax. Nothing can. The deep can't eat sweetness either, not really; it wants bread and meat, same as anyone." She said it the way she'd have said it about the weather. "Fifty years of carts. I knew from the first year. I kept my hives and my mouth shut."

The warm came up through the flowers under her, and the bees went up with her, a brown cloud round a woman, and she didn't call down where she wanted the hives. She'd said it once.

The comb behind them had set. There was a stair under the hive.

Ash waited until Tobin and Pip had gone down it, and then he said, without looking at Wren, "Your fingers."

"They're cold."

"In here?" It was as warm as an oven. "And on the piers at Saltwake your eyes went. And the seventh floor at Cinderholt. I saw. You didn't say."

Wren was quiet.

"What did it show you?"

"My mother," said Wren. "Working."

"That all?"

"That's all."

Her face was as smooth as the wax doors in the cloister. He knew a sealed door when he saw one. He had a cairn in his head and a man in a cold bed and a word he'd paid for.

"Liar," he said, mildly.

"Takes one," said Wren, and went down the stair.

### 5.4

*Floors 4 and 5: the presses and the Censer's nave.*

The cider-wife was being pressed, and she was enjoying it.

The fourth floor was a press house as long as a barn, great oak presses down both walls with screws as thick as Tobin's leg, juice running brown and fizzing in the gutters, windfalls coming off the press beds in dozens. After the honey, Tobin found he didn't mind the smell.

The woman was in the last press, lying on the pomace under the board, and the screw was coming down on her a hair's width at a time. She was sixty or so, round, red-faced, with her mouth open and a smile on it.

"*Lovely*," she said, in her sleep. "Oh, that's lovely. Another."

Tobin got both hands on the bar of the screw. His palms were new skin under the wrappings, pink and tight, and when he leaned on the bar they split, and he felt them go and kept leaning. The screw went back a turn, and another, and the board came up off her, and the woman opened one eye.

"Is it pressing?" she said. "I was having a lovely time."

"You were being squashed."

"That's what pressing is, love." She sat up in the pomace. "Hep Lowe. Cider. Don't drink it in front of the abbess, she'll take your name."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"My press by the inn road. People should have to pass me." She was looking past him now, round the press house, and up the gutters, the way people looked when they woke up down here and started counting. "Where's our Dickon?"

Nobody answered.

"My grandson. Six. Red hair, can't keep a cap on." She was smiling still, waiting. "He'll be off somewhere. Little tables. They sat the children at the little tables at the end, with the honey. He'll have been at the honey."

"He's not here," said Wren. Gently, the careful voice.

"Not *here*," said Hep. "Well, where, then?"

Wren didn't answer. Tobin watched Hep Lowe look at Wren's face, and then at his, and then at Pip's, which was the worst, because Pip couldn't hide anything, and he watched her understand some of it. Not all. Enough.

"Ah," said Hep. She put her hand on her chest. "Ah, well." And then, because she was who she was, in the same breath: "If you see Her Holiness the Weighing-Scale down here, tell her from me she can kiss my press."

The warm took her, still holding her chest.

The fifth floor was a nave, long and high and gold, with a vault that went up into smoke, and hanging from it on a chain as long as a well rope, swinging, was the Censer.

It had been the great incense pot the abbey swung over the feast to bless the food. The dream had made it the size of a cart, a pierced brass belly with fire inside and a sleepy smiling face in the brass, and it swung the length of the nave and back like a pendulum, and with every swing it breathed out sweet smoke. As the haze spread, the drones came out of the walls and the vault, dozens, their notes joining into one long chord, and every one of them turned in the air toward Pip.

She had the most in her bag. She'd had the most since the first floor.

"*Drop it*," said Tobin.

"No—"

"Pip, drop the bag—"

"It's *everything*, it's two floors, it's the cup and the seal and—"

The drones hit her. She went down under them with the buckler over her face, and the sickles came down on the buckler and the stones beside her head.

Wren got there first. Tobin would remember that. Not him. Wren went into the swarm writing small hot marks that the drones flinched from, and got her hand on the strap of Pip's satchel, and pulled. Pip held on. Wren brought the staff's brass foot down across Pip's knuckles, hard, and Pip yelped and let go, and Wren swung the bag round once and threw it as far as she could, out into the middle of the nave, under the swing of the Censer.

Every drone in the nave went after it.

"Tobin," said Ash. "The chain."

There was a carved gallery round the top of the nave, level with the bracket, and stairs to it. Tobin took them three at a time with his hands bleeding into the wrappings. Below him the drones had piled onto the satchel in a humming heap. He put the axe into the chain at the bracket, and the first stroke only rang. The second opened a link. He hit it a third time with everything he had, and the chain parted.

The Censer came down at the bottom of its swing, a cart's weight of burning brass, straight into the heap. The noise was like a bell dropped down a well. The chord stopped. Burning sweetness ran out across the floor in a slow gold tide and set where it reached the cold stones.

Tobin came down the stairs. Pip was sitting up with her buckler in her lap, looking at the back of her hand, where the brass had split the skin across two knuckles. She looked at Wren.

"You hit me," she said.

"You wouldn't let go."

"You *hit* me, Wren."

"I'd do it again," said Wren, and Tobin heard someone else in it, someone on a doorstep in Tansy Brook's blanket, and saw Pip hear it too.

The satchel was under the Censer. They got half of it out, later, when the brass had cooled: the cup, crushed flat, and some of the stones. The rest had melted into the floor with the wax and the drones and was part of the nave now.

Off the end of the nave, through a carved screen, there was a little chapel with one chair in it, and a woman asleep in the chair with her hands folded and her face lifted, as if listening to singing nobody else could hear.

### 5.5

*Floor 5: the Censer's chapel. Then the bank.*

The abbess woke up the way Wren had always imagined a queen would: slowly, with her eyes already on you.

She was seventy or more, small and straight, in a habit the colour of honey with a plain wooden cross at her neck carved like a bee. Her face was all fine lines and good bones. She looked at Tobin, and dismissed him. She looked at Ash, and Pip, and dismissed them. She looked at Wren for a long time.

"You're Isolde's," she said.

Wren hadn't been ready for it. Nobody had said her mother's name to her out loud in ten years except herself, at the wall.

"Yes."

"You have her chin. And you hold a pen as if it's going to escape." The abbess unfolded her hands. "Morwenna Pyle. I was abbess of Thistledown for thirty-one years, and elder for twenty-four. Your mother came to our scriptorium every spring for eleven of them. Did you know that?"

"No."

"No. She wouldn't have said. She sat under the east window with the old tally pages until the candles went." The abbess smiled very slightly. "She was the only person in the Vale besides one who could read them. She worked it out herself. Cold as a well and twice as deep."

"What was on them?" said Pip.

The abbess didn't look at her. She looked at Wren, as if Wren had asked.

"Names," she said. "Every name in every town, every year, in the old hand. Your mother wrote Hollowmere's. She kept your town's tithe ledgers too, the weighing and the tally both, for nine years. She'd have known to the sack whether the tithe would pass. She'd have known by the evening of the weighing." The abbess tilted her head. "She would have known what it meant."

Wren stood quite still. The staff was cold in her hand. Her fingertips were cold. She thought of five ticks, and a firm line through the fifth, and a hand going down short and up long.

"You knew too," said Ash. "What it meant."

"I swore the oath at the Hearth when I was forty-six," said the abbess, quite pleasantly. "I know what a table is, young man. I wrote the sermon. *Who eats at the table is held in the hand.* I preached it every autumn for twenty-four years, and the people of this town sat down to eat with clean hearts, and for twenty-four years nobody in Thistledown starved, nor in any town on the road." She turned back to Wren. "You'll understand. You're hers. You know what it is to keep a great many people fed with something they'd be happier not knowing."

"I don't feed anyone."

"No? You keep a list." The old eyes went over her, the stitched hand, the staff. "Your mother understood that some truths are a kind of famine. You feed people the other thing. A sacrament, a pageant, a wall of names. They eat it, and they live."

"They didn't live," said Tobin. "They're down here."

"Ten years," said the abbess, "out of fifty. I'd make the trade again." She looked at Wren, patiently, the way a teacher waits for a slow child to reach the end of a sum. "And so, I think, would she."

Wren found she had nothing to say. That was the worst part. She found she could follow the abbess all the way down the column and the figures added up, and she hated the woman for it, and couldn't find the mistake.

"Where do you want to live?" she said at last, because it was what she said.

"The abbey as it was," said Morwenna Pyle. "The choir singing at dusk."

"As it was," said Wren.

"Exactly as it was." The abbess folded her hands again. "You'd understand that, too."

The warm took her up with her face lifted, still listening.

The bank was through the chapel's back wall: a yellow fire of candle ends, a stone font for the cold basin, iron alms chests for strongboxes, a bell rope for the hoist. By the fire, where the other dungeons had kept their lanterns and floats and brass checks, stood a wooden frame of comb, every cell capped with wax. As Wren watched, one of the caps by the top corner split, and a small brown bee crawled out of it, cleaned its face with both front legs, and flew up into the dark after the abbess.

"That's how many," said Pip. She counted the capped cells and stopped, and started again, and stopped.

"Home," said Tobin, holding his bleeding hands in the font.

Nobody argued. Pip was still looking at her split knuckles. Wren was looking at her own fingertips, which were white at the ends, the way fingers go in snow, and didn't warm up in the yellow fire, and when she pressed them together she could hardly feel them at all.

### 5.6

*Thistledown. The weeks after.*

Hep Lowe's press went up by the inn road in four days, before the inn, and Hep said that was the proper order of things.

Pip sat on a barrel beside it most afternoons. She couldn't get the cider past her teeth, it was sweet; but Hep talked, and Pip listened, and it was nearly as good as the gate.

Thistledown came back gently, which Hep said was how it had always been. "Gentle and sure. We were the nicest people in the Vale, chick, and didn't we know it." Silas walked his rows every morning with a knife and a face like a thundercloud. Clemency's hives went in on the south slope, and she spoke to nobody and to the bees all day. And every dusk the abbess walked out to the bare step on the knoll and stood with her hands folded, facing the empty floor where her choir used to stand.

"Every bloody night," said Hep. "Waiting for the hymn. There's no novices home. She knows that. She stands there so we'll all see her doing it."

"Where are the novices?"

"Down there, I suppose. With everyone." Hep didn't say *with Dickon*. She'd stopped saying his name. She just drank faster at the end of the day, when the light went the colour it used to go when the children were called in.

It was too far to go home to Hollowmere every night, so the four of them slept in the cider barn behind Hep's press, and the meeting was a barrel with a lamp on it. The first night Wren wrote the wall on the barn door in chalk, from memory, every name, both columns. It took her an hour. Then Pip watched her look at one name, unticked, near the bottom of the H's, for a long time.

Tobin watched her too. The gap was still a hand. Pip had seen it go to nothing once, on the road to Saltwake, and come back, and now it sat there between them like a third person on the bench.

Ash went out at night.

The first time, Pip followed him with her heart going like a drum, up through the orchard in the dark, her hand on her dagger without knowing who for. He stopped at the last tree and sat down with his back to it, and sat there, looking at nothing, for two hours, while she crouched in the grass and got cold.

"You can come out," he said, at the end.

She came out and sat beside him.

"There's no crack here," he said. "I've looked. I looked the first night." He held up his hands. They were cold. She could see them shaking, in the moonlight. "I promised Jory."

"I told Wren," said Pip. "About the scree. About Wynn."

"I know."

"I'd do it again."

"I know," said Ash. "That's why I didn't stop you." He looked up the hill. "Everyone keeps saying that. *I'd do it again.* Your gran. The abbess. Wren, about your hand." He turned his own over. "I don't know if I would. That's the trouble. I don't know."

They went back down through the orchard together, not talking, and it was the first time since the timber yard.

On the fifth afternoon Hep said, out of nowhere, "Hollowmere's ledger-keeper used to come up here."

Pip kept her face still. She'd got better at that.

"Every spring. Up the drovers' road with a satchel of books, sat in the scriptorium with the old pages till the candles went. Cold as a well. Lovely hands." Hep drank. "Her Holiness thought the sun shone out of her. The last spring or two she brought a big fellow up with her to carry the books. Never said a word to anyone. Looked at her the way a dog looks at a shut door." She snorted. "Your Wren's hers, isn't she? Same chin."

"Same chin," said Pip.

Wren was coming up the inn road with water. She'd heard. Pip saw her hear: not the face, which didn't move, but the hand on the bucket, which went white at the knuckles and stayed white, the ends of the fingers whitest of all. Then Wren walked on past them into the barn, and put the bucket down, and Pip heard the hasp go over on something, as clearly as if it had been a real one.

### 5.7

*The Hive Choir, floors 6 and 7: the almshouse and the wax halls. Five weeks on.*

They went down from the bank, because the checkpoint was there, and Tobin went first, because he always did, and the first thing he found was a man trying to give him a loaf.

The sixth floor was the abbey's almshouse, or the dream's version of it: a long low room with benches and a door at the end that stood open on darkness, and a man asleep in the doorway on a stool, holding out a loaf of bread to nobody. He was thirty or so, thin, with big gentle hands and a habit gone threadbare at the elbows. His arm didn't waver. He'd been holding that loaf out for ten years.

When Tobin took it out of his hand, he woke.

"Oh, good," he said at once. "You're hungry. There's more." There was a basket at his feet, and he started putting bread into Tobin's arms, and cheese, smiling. "Brother Fennick. Almoner. Take it, go on."

"We're not," said Tobin, with his arms full. "We're not hungry."

"Everybody's hungry," said Brother Fennick kindly. "That's the first thing they teach you."

"Where did it come from?" said Ash.

Fennick's smile slipped a little at the corner. "The barns. The tithe barns. In the lean years. I'd go down at night with a cart and take a bit off the top before it was weighed, and bring it here, and open the door." He looked at the door behind him, open on nothing. "We never turned anyone away. Not once. That was the rule."

"That was the tithe," said Wren.

"Yes," said Fennick. "I know. I knew." He looked at the bread in Tobin's arms as if he'd never seen bread before. "But they were *hungry*. You can't say no to someone who's hungry. I never could. I never could say no to anyone."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"The almshouse by the gate. With the door open."

The warm took him up still holding out the last of the cheese. They put his bread in the bags, and the drones came at whoever had the most of it all the way to the stair.

The seventh floor was wax halls, and candle-nuns: sisters of yellow wax standing in alcoves with their hands joined, who turned their smooth featureless faces as you passed and wept fire at you, two slow lines of burning tallow from where their eyes should have been. Tobin took one on the axe-head and it ran down the haft onto his wrappings, and he beat it out against his leg and swore.

Wren wrote.

She'd been writing more than she had to all day, bigger marks, as if she were trying to use something up. Twice he'd seen her put her hand inside her coat, against her ribs, and take it out again.

At the end of the seventh floor, when Pip and Ash had brought down a candle-nun as tall as the vault in a long hot slide of wax, Wren's staff went bright in her hands.

He saw her eyes go, the way he'd seen Pip's and Ash's: the eyes going somewhere and the body staying. Her free hand came up, slowly, all by itself, palm out, at the height of a shoulder.

Like a woman putting her hand up at a meeting.

It was steady as a beam. It stayed up so long that Pip started to say "Wren—" and Ash put his hand on Pip's arm. Then it came down, and Wren's knees went.

Tobin caught her. He didn't think about it. He was on her left, and he caught her under the arms the way he'd caught Nell off a wall once, and she was lighter than he thought, and cold. Her hands were cold as a dead fire.

"I've got you."

"I know." She didn't open her eyes for a moment. "I know you have."

"What was it?"

She opened them. She looked at him from very close, and he saw her decide.

"Nothing," said Wren. "My mother at a meeting. Nothing."

Tobin held her up. He didn't believe her. He didn't know what he didn't believe; he only knew the voice, because he'd used it himself, on Nell, on his father, on Marta, on her. *Da had her.* *I came back.* He'd never in his life heard Wren use it, and now he had.

He didn't let go. Not when she got her feet under her, not when Pip came and looked at her face and then away, not when Ash went ahead to the stair. He kept his arm under hers down the whole length of the stair to the eighth floor, on her left, the side he could hear her from, and she let him, and neither of them said anything about it, and it was the first time in a month that the gap had been nothing at all.

### 5.8

*Floor 8: the scriptorium.*

The staff had shown her the back room of the inn, and seven chairs, and a slate on the table with a figure on it in her mother's hand. *Short: thirty-one sacks.* Aldous Sedge saying the mill could take water in the sacks, nobody would know the difference till spring. Somebody saying *the weighing's after the meal, it always is*. The elder saying, very tired, *Who's for it?*

And her mother's hand going up. The third. Steady as a beam.

That was all. No reason. Just the hand.

Wren walked into the eighth floor with that hand in her head, and the scriptorium was waiting for her.

It was long and high and made of candles: sloped desks down both sides, and candles on every desk and shelf and niche, hundreds, burning down and never burning out, the wax running off the desks in sheets. The windows were wax too, glowing red from behind. Somewhere a pen was scratching.

At the far end, under the east window, at a lectern of wax, her mother was writing.

She sat on a stool with her back straight, the way she sat at the kitchen table. The lectern had grown over her: the wax had run down it and over her lap, and up her arms to the elbow, and held her there, a woman set in a candle. Only her right hand was free, from the wrist. It held a pen it never dipped. It wrote, and wrote, short down and long up, across a page on the lectern, and the page never filled.

Wren didn't run. She would think about that afterwards. She walked the length of the room between the desks with the others behind her, and the candles leaned toward her as she passed.

"Smoke," she said. "Slowly."

Pip came up with the smoker. The wax on her mother's arms went soft and clear, and slumped, and as it slumped, the lectern sagged with it, and the candles on the lectern's lip tipped, one by one, toward the page.

It caught at the corner. A small blue flame, then yellow, running along the bottom edge.

Her mother's hand kept writing. It wrote toward the flame.

Wren put her right hand into the fire and took the page out from under her mother's pen. It burned her. The seven stitches across her knuckles opened like a seam and she felt them go, and she slapped the page against her coat, once, twice, and the flame went out, and the page was in her hand, black along the bottom edge and curling, and before she could stop herself she had read what was nearest her thumb.

Five ticks. A line through the fifth. And beside them, small and neat, initials she knew.

She shut the page into the cost book and put the book in her coat.

Her mother's hand had stopped. It hung in the air above the empty lectern with the pen in it. Then it put the pen down, carefully, the way you'd put down a pen at the end of a day's work, and Isolde Hale opened her eyes.

She looked at Wren, not the room or the three strangers behind her, as if Wren were a figure at the bottom of a column, and she knew her. Instantly, across ten years and a whole other face. Her mother knew her before she'd finished opening her eyes.

"Wren," said her mother. Two syllables. *Wre-en.* Cross.

And Wren saw her face, at last, the face that wouldn't turn round for ten years behind the lantern light, and it was her own. The chin, the mouth, the line between the eyebrows. She'd been looking at it in the black glass of her window every night of her life, and that was why she couldn't remember it. It had been in front of her all the time.

"You came down here," said her mother.

"Yes."

"I told you to hold it."

"I held it."

Isolde looked at the staff in Wren's hand. Then the warm came up through the wax floor and took her, sitting straight on nothing, with her pen-hand in her lap, and she looked down at Wren the whole way up, and didn't say where she wanted to live, because she'd already told it.

The staff was hot in Wren's hand. Hot as a hand.

And she was in the kitchen at home, on the feast evening, in the low gold light, and she was tall, and her hands were long and ink-stained and steady, and they were filling a lantern from the oil jar. Filling it to the brim, past the line, so the oil stood up trembling at the lip. They wiped the lip with a cloth. They hung the lantern on the staff's iron hook, and turned, and held the staff out to a girl of eight in her feast dress, solemn, with her hair pulled back too tight.

*Hold this for me,* said the mouth Wren was speaking from.

The hands bent the eyes down to the girl's forehead and kissed it, quick, dry, and straightened. That was all. The hands didn't shake. Wren waited the whole memory for them to shake, and they didn't, and the eyes she was looking out of were perfectly dry.

When she came back, the staff's bands had gone from black and white to a deep dark gold and a pale one, like honey and wax, and the brass foot shone, and she knew its name the way you know your own. *True North.*

Her right hand was bleeding down the staff. Tobin was holding her by the elbow. She hadn't felt him take it.

"What was that you grabbed?" said Pip. "Off the desk."

"Her page." Wren heard her own voice, smooth as the wax doors. "The tally. Hollowmere's. It burned through."

"Can I see?"

"There's nothing left to see."

Pip looked at her. Then at her coat, where the cost book was. Then she said, "Right," and went to look at the stair, and Wren stood in a room of candles with her mother's page against her ribs, and felt it there like a second, slower heart.

### 5.9

*Floor 9: the choir loft.*

The Gleaners didn't come for them. They sang.

The ninth floor was a loft, a long gallery high under a gold vault, with tiers of benches down one side like the seats in a theatre, and the Gleaners stood on the tiers in rows, forty of them, in their sheaf masks, with their scorched hands folded in front of them like novices. In front of them, on a box, a thin man in a sheaf mask conducted with one hand, and they sang.

It was the feast blessing; Bram had made every child in Hollowmere learn the Thistledown setting for the pageant. *Who eats at the table is held in the hand.* Forty voices, slow and beautiful, and as the hymn went up into the vault, the wax came up out of the floor, round her boots, her ankles, and set. She smoked it off and it came again, faster. Every line of the hymn, it rose.

"It's the *song*," said Ash. He was up to his shins. "It's setting it. Stop the song—"

The thin man turned round on his box. He didn't come at them. He climbed down, and came along the gallery toward them through the rising wax as if it weren't there, with a plate in his hand, and on the plate a piece of honey cake.

"You've been hungry for ten years," he said. His voice was soft and warm and sorry for them. "Sit down. Please. It isn't a trick. It's the only true thing anyone ever told this Vale."

He held the plate out to Pip. Not at her mouth. Out, the way you'd offer it to a guest. Waiting.

Pip looked at the honey cake. The smell came off it into her face, and she was seven floors down under Hollowmere with a spoon at her mouth, and she was also here, with the wax at her hips and a man holding out a plate as kindly as anyone ever had, and the terrible thing was that some small starved part of her wanted to take it. Just to stop. Just to sit.

She knocked the plate out of his hand.

"I'm not *hungry*," she said, and her voice cracked, and she put the dagger through the straw.

It didn't go deep. He didn't stop her. Behind him Tobin had got out of the wax by main strength and was going up the tiers with the axe, Ash was shooting the top row, Wren was writing with her bleeding hand, and every time a voice stopped, the wax sank back. Pip ran along the tiers puffing smoke in their straw faces, and they choked and fell silent one by one.

When the last voice stopped, the thin man was still standing where she'd left him with the cut in his mask.

He reached up and took it off himself.

His face was kind. That was the worst of it. Forty or so, with laugh lines and grey eyes and a long gentle mouth, the face of a man who'd taught children to sing and been patient about it. The ember cracks ran down his neck under the collar.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?" said Pip.

"For trying. You'd be surprised how few do." He looked at the honey cake on the floor. "Lisle. I led the choir. I'm sorry about the cake. I meant it."

"Who did you do it for?" said Pip. "You all did it for someone. Ivo said. Every one of you."

"For all of you," said Cantor Lisle, as if it were obvious. "For everyone at the table. I asked for nothing else." He smiled at her. "It's a good table. You'll see."

Then he turned, and walked to the end of the loft where the gallery stopped and the dark began, and down into it, singing the blessing quietly to himself, and the Gleaners who were still standing on the tiers stepped aside for him like a congregation for a priest.

"*Wait*—" Pip went after him. Ash had her arm. "Let go—he's going *down*, he's going to the—"

"He's not a sleeper," said Ash. "The warm won't come for him. Pip. He's going home."

The singing went down into the dark, and on, and down, long after it should have been too far to hear. Pip stood at the edge of the loft with honey cake on her boot and listened to it go, and found she was crying, and couldn't have told anyone why, except that he'd been kind, and he'd meant it.

### 5.10

*Floor 10: the heart of the hive.*

The queen was being fed, and Ash saw at once that she never ate.

The tenth floor was the inside of a skep, a dome of comb spiralling up into gold, hot as breath. On a dais of wax in the middle was the queen.

She had been Mother Bounty: the gilt wooden woman with a bowl that the abbey carried at the head of the feast procession every year, smiling. The dream had kept her face and her arms holding out the bowl, and added the rest behind, a vast swollen body banded gold and black, wings of stained wax. Drones came to her in a stream and emptied their honey into her bowl, and it overflowed down her arms, and she held it out, smiling, and never drank, and never gave it to anyone.

Round the walls, in tiers, stood the choir: drones with faces. Young men and women, twenty or so, eyes shut, mouths open, furred below the neck. The novices. They sang the loft's hymn, and the walls began to grow inward, cell on cell.

"Smoke the choir," said Ash. "Shut them up and she's open. Pip, left side. I'll take the right."

"The drones," said Wren. "They'll come for the haul."

"Then give it to me," said Tobin.

Ash turned. Tobin was holding out his bag. Then he took Pip's out of her hands, and Ash's off his shoulder, and Wren's, and emptied them all into his own, cups and stones and wax and Fennick's bread, until the satchel bulged sweet and sticky against his hip, and every drone in the hive turned its note toward him.

"Tobin, *no*—"

"Somebody has to," said Tobin, and walked out into the middle of the floor.

The whole hive poured down the walls toward one man with an axe. Ash shot until his string hand bled. Pip went round the left tiers with the smoker, and the novices choked and went quiet, one, two, five; every voice that stopped, the walls slowed. In the middle of the floor Tobin stood in a roaring heap of drones with the axe going like a flail, and they weren't stinging him. They were carrying honey to his mouth.

Then the comb came up round Tobin's boots, and set.

It came fast, out of the floor under him, the way it had in the loft, a column of cells rising up his legs, his waist, his chest, white and hard, and the drones crawled up it with their honey, and Tobin swung until the wax reached his elbows and he couldn't swing any more. It reached his chin. A drone crawled up the white column to his face, and put its honey to his shut mouth.

Wren walked out across the floor.

She didn't write small. Ash had watched her write for a year, small careful marks with the brass foot, and he had never seen her do this: she raised the staff over her head and wrote one letter in the air the size of a door, gold, burning, and then another, and then she said it.

It wasn't a word anyone should have been able to say. It came out of her anyway. Ash felt it in his teeth, the way he'd felt the bell, and in the scree, and in the light over her table a year ago: the same warm. The same voice under it.

The comb round Tobin went to liquid all at once and ran off him like a wave going back down a beach, and the drones went with it, and Tobin fell forward onto his hands, honey to the chin, spitting.

Pip smoked the last singer. The hymn stopped.

In the silence the queen turned her gilt smiling face toward them, and held out her full bowl, and Tobin got up off his hands and walked up the dais, dripping, and put the axe through the bowl.

It cracked down the middle. Every drop of honey ever carried to it came out at once, a gold flood down the dais, and the swollen body behind the gilt woman sagged, and went in on itself like a skin of wine, and the queen sank down into her own spilled honey, still smiling, still holding out half a bowl.

The choir fell off the walls, and the fur came off them like husks, and they were young men and women in white, asleep, and the warm took them all at once, a choir going up. Behind them, out of every cell in the dome, came bees, ordinary bees, millions, pouring up the warm in a roar like a river, going home.

Ash stood in it with bees in his hair.

"Fed by everyone," he said. His voice sounded strange. "Fed nobody. They built a whole faith round feeding something."

"Ash," said Pip. "Look at her hand."

Wren was standing where she'd stopped, with the staff lowered. Her right hand was bleeding from the opened stitches. And the first finger of it was grey. Grey from the nail to the first knuckle, a flat stone grey, the grey Tuppence had been for ten years by the pond. Ash watched her press it with her thumb. It didn't dent.

She looked up and saw him looking, and put her hand in her coat.

### 5.11

*Thistledown, the orchard path. That afternoon.*

The bees came home out of the hill like smoke out of a chimney, and Tobin sat on a doorstep at the bottom of the orchard and watched them go to Clemency's hives, and thought he'd never be clean again.

The whole town was out. Clemency stood among her hives with the swarm coming down round her, laughing, which nobody had heard her do. The novices had come up into their plots and stood about the abbey knoll in their white, blinking, and the abbess went among them touching their faces, and not one of them would look at her. Brother Fennick was trying to give a loaf to Ash.

Then the bees on the orchard path parted, and a man walked up through the blossom.

He came in full daylight, in no hurry: forty-four or so to look at, grey at the temples, clean-shaven, in a long dark coat with worn fur at the collar and silver at the cuffs. An elder's coat, the kind Bram had made the Sedge boy wear in the pageant, with a wool beard. Nothing came with him. Blossom fell on his shoulders and he let it lie. He stopped ten feet from the four of them and looked at each in turn, the way you'd look at people you'd been told a great deal about.

"Corwin Vane," he said. "Elder of Hollowmere."

Tobin knew the voice. He'd heard it once, on his left, by a pit in Cinderholt, close as a mouth at his ear. *You have your mother's way of standing, boy.* He heard it now on his left again, because the man had stopped on that side, and it came through the wall in his head as if there were no wall at all.

"You," said Ash. Very quietly. "By the pit."

"By the pit," Vane agreed. "I apologise for that. It was a poor way to introduce myself." He looked at Ash for a long moment. "You worked out the rest at my stone, I think. Your brother told you about Bettany, and you put your hand on that stone by the gate and did the sum. I'd hoped to tell you myself. But you were always going to find it first. You're the kind who does."

"You're dead," said Pip. "You went down with the first tithe. There's a *pageant*."

"There is. It's very bad." He didn't smile. "I went down with the first tithe. I didn't die. I signed the Bargain, Pip Ostrander. Not six elders together. I did. I was the only man in the Vale who could read the letters, and I read every one of them, the clause at the end too, and I signed it. I wrote the elder's oath that passed it down. I named the feast a thanksgiving." He said it plainly, as if reading out a bill. "I'd buried my wife that winter in ground so hard it took me two days. I'd buried my son. My daughter was eleven and I could count her ribs. I'd sign it again tomorrow, in that winter."

Tobin hit him.

He didn't decide to. The axe was in his raw hands, and he stepped in and swung it into the man's chest with everything he had, the way he'd swung at a root wall, at a door, at a weld.

It went in to the cheek. There was no blood. The axe went into Corwin Vane the way it went into a dead stump, dry and hollow, nothing beating where something should beat, and the cold came up the haft into Tobin's hands. Not cold like snow. Cold like an empty house.

Vane looked down at the axe in his chest. Then he put his hand on the haft beside Tobin's, quite gently, and drew it out, and turned it round in the air, and held it out to Tobin by the haft, head down, the way you hand a man a tool.

"Hob's," he said. "He always kept a good edge. Take it."

Tobin took it. He didn't know why. His hands did.

Vane turned back to Ash.

"Where's my mother?" said Ash. His voice cracked on it.

"Safe. Asleep. Near me." Something moved in Vane's face and went still. "She sleeps sweet, Ash. I've seen to that. I've seen to it for all of you, as far as I'm let." He paused. "You paid for my Bettany's name with a lamp-keeper. I'm sorry for that. Amos Gill would have told it you for a cup of cider. Odile Ostrander would have told you for nothing. It's on the parish roll in your own church." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You went up the hill and asked it instead. It always answers. That's the trouble with it."

Ash had gone the colour of the blossom. He didn't say anything. Tobin watched him not say anything, and thought of a man at the top of a dark lane, and couldn't, for once, be glad.

Then Vane looked at Wren.

"And you've brought your mother home," he said. "Good. I laid her where she'd want to be found: at work. I laid each of them where you'd come for them." He looked from face to face. "You all come for your own. It's the most dependable thing in the Vale. I've built on it for sixty years."

"We know," said Tobin. "We worked it out. Bait."

"Bait," said Vane, as if trying the word. "Yes. Bait draws you. That part's simple. It's what you carry home off the hook that matters." He looked back at Wren. "Your mother keeps excellent books. Far better than mine ever were. You kept her page, I hope."

Tobin felt Wren go still beside him.

"It burned," she said.

Vane looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face that wasn't a smile came and went.

"Of course it did," he said.

He turned and walked back down the orchard path, in no hurry. Nobody followed. All up the slope people stood in their doorways and watched: Hep with a cup halfway to her mouth, Silas in his tree, Clemency with bees on her sleeves. On the knoll among her silent novices, the abbess had gone white as her own wax, with her hand over her mouth. At the bottom of the orchard the man in the old coat stepped off the path into the long grass, and was gone, the way the warm closes.

Pip was looking at Wren. Tobin saw it, and saw Wren not look back, and didn't understand it, and put it away, because Ash had sat down on the doorstep with his face in his hands, and somebody had to.

### 5.12

*Hollowmere, Wren's house. Two days on, at dusk.*

The lamp was lit in her window, and Wren hadn't lit it.

She stopped at the bottom of Mill Lane, and the others stopped behind her. Two streets over the lamp lane was dark, as it had been since Wynn. Her own window was lit, and somebody had trimmed the wick right, which she never did.

"Go on," said Tobin, behind her. "We'll come after."

She went up the lane alone and opened her own door.

Her mother was sitting at the table in front of the wall, with Tuppence in her lap.

The cat's fur was up along his spine in a ridge, and he was purring anyway, kneading her mother's skirt under her long ink-stained right hand. The lamp was turned toward the wall, and her mother sat the way she sat over the cost book, with her eyes going down the columns.

"You put the door back where it was," said her mother, without turning round.

Wren stood in it.

"That's where it was."

"I always meant to move it. It faces the wind." Her mother turned her head then, and looked at her. "You wrote everybody. In order. Lane by lane."

"I wanted to remember."

"You did." Isolde looked back at the wall. Her eyes went down it, and stopped, and Wren knew exactly where, because she'd stood there herself so many nights. Then her mother said, quietly, as if checking a list, "Odile Ostrander."

"Home."

"Hob Marsh."

"Home." Wren's throat was tight. "At the timber yard. He drinks."

"He would." Something passed over her mother's face and was gone. "Jory Calder."

"Home. Last month."

Her mother nodded, once, as if three figures had come out right. She didn't say the fourth. She looked at the column headed *At the table*, all the way down, the Pell twins and the Stroud boys and Tilly Rowe, and her face didn't change, and her hand on the cat didn't stop.

"I told it where I wanted to live," said her mother. "Your house. My own room in it. A desk under the window."

"There's no desk," said Wren. "There never was."

"No. I always wanted one." Her mother looked round the room, the crooked walls, the thatch, the plaster cracked where Tobin's beams had settled. "You built it exactly. I can see you did. Every stone where it was."

"I wanted you to come home to it."

"Wren." One syllable this time. "It wasn't like this. It was never as you remember it. You were eight. I was this the whole time."

The door opened behind Wren, and Tobin came in, and Pip, and Ash last. Her mother looked at each of them. She looked at Tobin longest.

"You've got your father's shoulders," she said, and Tobin didn't know what to do with that, and said, "Yes, Mrs Hale," as if he were ten.

Wren watched her mother's hand on the cat. The first finger of it, the right forefinger, was grey to the second knuckle. Flat stone grey. Wren had seen that finger every day of her childhood: on the cost book, on her collar buttons, on her forehead once on a feast evening. She'd thought it was ink. Her whole life she'd thought it was ink that wouldn't wash.

Her own finger, in her coat, was the same grey to the first knuckle.

Her mother saw her looking. She took her hand off the cat and folded it in her lap, under the other one, and Wren knew that gesture. She'd made it herself, in a hot gallery, an hour ago, a year ago, every time.

### 5.13

*Wren's house. That night, and the nights after.*

She read the page in bed, by a candle, with the door shut and her mother asleep on the other side of the wall.

It was a tally page. The children's block of Hollowmere, every child's name in plain ink in her mother's hand, Tilly Rowe and the Pell twins and the Sedge boy, the way it had looked on the eighth floor of the Undercroft before the ember seals. In the margin beside five of them, five small ticks. The fifth struck through with one firm line.

Beside the ticks, small, the initials, and the names they belonged to.

*O.O. — P.*

*H.M. — T., N.*

*J.C. — A.*

*I.H. — W.*

Below that, the weighing. *Short: thirty-one sacks. Council: water the mill sacks. Carried five to two. I.H. for.* And under that, in the same steady hand, as if it were another item on a bill: *Five is mischief. Ten is a search.*

Wren read it four times. Then she read the last line, which wasn't in plain ink.

It was in the script, and it had burned. The bottom edge of the page was black and curled, and the line ran into the black and stopped. She could read the start of it, because she could read nearly everything now. *Whoever stands at the table unbound—* and then char.

He'd laid her mother at a desk writing this. The one page in the Vale that said who had chosen whose child, in a hand Wren would know anywhere, with Tobin's father on it and Nell struck through. He'd laid it where Wren would come, and set the candles so she'd have to take it out of the fire with her own hand. *It's what you carry home off the hook.*

She knew exactly what she was supposed to do with it. She was supposed to read it, and keep it, and not tell them.

She folded it into the cost book, between the oil prices and her own figures, and put the book under her pillow.

Three nights later Hob Marsh had built her mother a desk under the window, and her mother was teaching her to write.

"Again," said Isolde. "Your downstroke's slow. It isn't a picture. It's a word. It wants to be said."

Wren wrote it on the slate with her fingertip, the good one. A faint light, gone.

"You taught yourself this."

"From the old pages at the abbey. From tallies." Her mother watched her hand. "You taught yourself from a cat."

"From the collar and the step and the staff's foot. You left it everywhere."

"I left it where it was needed." Isolde turned Wren's right hand over on the desk, and looked at the grey finger, and didn't touch it. "And you wrote big."

"Twice. The cat, and Tobin."

"A cat is small to undo. A man the dream has hold of isn't." Her mother laid her own grey finger beside Wren's on the desk, two hands, one older. "They're not ours, Wren. The letters. You write them on its account, and it takes something back each time from the hand that writes. A little warm. The step, the year you were born. The collar. The staff's foot." She touched her second knuckle. "Then a ward on a church, the week before the feast. That took it to here."

Wren looked at the two fingers in her mind, side by side.

"The page," she said. "In the scriptorium. Five ticks. Odile, Hob, Jory. And one crossed out."

Her mother was quiet for a moment. "Nell."

"You could have taken ten."

"I could have taken none. Ten gone from the table is a search before the meal. Five is mischief. I did the figures. It's what I do."

"Tilly Rowe sat next to me at school."

"I know whose names are on your wall."

"You could read the contract. All of it." Wren made herself go on. "The line at the bottom. About someone unbound. Standing at the table."

"I could read it."

"Did you save me because I'm yours?" Wren's voice didn't shake. She'd made sure of that. "Or because you needed someone who could stand there?"

The candle burned. Downstairs Tuppence knocked something off a shelf and walked away from it.

"Both answers are true," said her mother. "Pick the one you can live with."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only honest thing I have. I'm giving it to you instead of a kind one."

Wren looked at the slate. "My lantern stayed lit. Everyone else's went out."

"I filled the oil," said Isolde. "That's all I did."

"Is that true?"

Her mother picked up the chalk cloth and wiped the slate clean.

"Again," she said. "Your downstroke."

### 5.14

*Hollowmere. The week after.*

The desk was the best thing Tobin had ever seen his father make, and he found out about it by accident.

He went up to Wren's at dawn with water, the way he did, and there it was under the back window: oak, plain, the top planed so smooth it shone, the joints dovetailed so tight you couldn't have slid a hair into them. A slope for writing. A drawer with a brass pull. It hadn't been there the night before.

"Who made that?" he said.

"Your father," said Mrs Hale, from the stair. "In the night. I didn't ask him."

Tobin went down to the timber yard. Hob was asleep on a pile of offcuts with a bottle by his hand and oak shavings in his hair.

"You made her a desk."

Hob opened one eye. "Somebody said she wanted one."

"Who?"

"Heard it." Hob shut the eye again. "Go away."

Since he came home his father had built back what he was asked to, well and grudgingly, and drunk. He'd never made anyone anything unasked. Tobin couldn't think of a reason, so he didn't, and went back up the lane.

Something was breaking in Wren. He didn't know what. He knew what it looked like, because he'd seen his own face do it in a bucket of water: everything held very still on top so nobody would see the cracks going down. She kept the wall and answered when spoken to. She didn't eat much. Her candle was lit under the eaves at three in the morning, every morning, when he came up the lane on his way to nothing.

He didn't ask. He'd asked once, on the seventh floor, and she'd used his own voice on him, and he'd heard it.

So he did what he knew. He brought the water. He split the wood. He stood on her left at the pump, so she was on his good side, and the third morning he found her there in the grey with her right hand under the spout, holding it in the cold water, the grey finger and the opened stitches, staring at nothing. He took the hand out of the water, and dried it on his shirt, and wrapped it in both of his, bandages and all, and held it until it warmed. Except the one finger. That one didn't.

She let him. She leaned her shoulder against his arm and didn't say anything, and he didn't, and the gap was nothing, and he didn't know why, and decided he didn't need to.

Over her head, down the lane, Pip was sitting on the watch-house step mending a strap and watching them. Not him. Wren.

### 5.15

*Wren's house. The meeting, the seventh night.*

Pip had been counting, because Gran had taught her to, and she was up to three.

*It burned through.* That was one, in the scriptorium. *There's nothing left to see.* That was two. *It burned.* That was three, in the orchard, to a man with no heart, who'd looked at Wren the way Gran looked at somebody at the gate she'd already decided to let past.

Three wasn't mischief. Three was a search.

Mrs Hale didn't come down to the meeting. They could hear her chair creak upstairs when she turned a page. Tuppence came down and walked the length of the table and stopped at Ash's place, the way he had every night for a month, and his ridge went up. Then he sat down a foot from Ash's hand, with his back to him, which from Tuppence was nearly a kindness, and Ash looked at the cat's back as if it were a letter he'd been waiting for.

"Thistledown sent word," said Tobin. "Hep sent it with the carter. The novices won't sing."

"At all?" said Ash.

"Not for the abbess. She stands on the step every dusk and they don't come." Tobin turned his cup. "Hep says they sing in the orchard. For Silas. While he prunes."

"Good," said Pip, and meant it.

Wren went to the wall. She had the brush and the soot paint, and she painted the tick by *Isolde Hale*, small and neat, the way her mother ticked the cost book. Her right hand was bandaged, and the brush was in her left, and the tick came out shaky, and she looked at it a long time.

Then she turned round, and said, in the voice she used for plans, "Stillhollow next. The pass road. Ash, you'll want the maps," and sat down, and opened the cost book to the back where her own figures were, and wrote something, with her left hand, very slowly. Pip watched the book. She could see the edge of a page in it that wasn't the same paper as the rest, browner, curled, black along the bottom.

She had it in her mouth. *What's in the book, Wren?* She could feel exactly how it would go: the room going quiet, Tobin's face, Wren's. She'd have said it beautifully, like a bell.

She kept it behind her teeth, like the honey, and didn't swallow it and didn't spit it out.

"Same time tomorrow," said Tobin.

"Where else would we go," said Pip, and looked at the book in Wren's lap, and at Wren's hand on it, flat, holding it shut, the grey finger lying across the cover like a latch.
