# Chapter 6: Whoever Stands

*Status: draft, for user annotation.*

### 6.1

*The pass road above Thistledown, then Stillhollow. Early summer.*

It was summer at the bottom of the climb and winter at the top, and Ash watched the line between them come down the mountain to meet them like a tide.

They had left Thistledown in larks and clover. By the second day the clover had gone to heather, and the heather to scree with snow lying in the shadow of every stone, as if the mountain had been salted. The pass road went up in long zigzags, its edge marked with stone posts every hundred paces. Each post had a number cut in it, a little smaller than the last.

"They're counting down," said Pip. "To what?"

"The top," said Ash.

"That's very Stillhollow." Pip blew on her hands. "Gran says they count the steps to their own privy."

Nobody laughed, but Tobin's mouth moved, which was something. He walked on Wren's left. He'd walked there since the pump in Hollowmere. Pip walked behind them both, and Ash, at the front with the maps, had stopped looking back, because every time he did Pip was looking at the same place on Wren's coat, low on the left side, where the cost book was.

He knew what a sealed door looked like. He knew what it looked like when somebody stood outside one with the word in her mouth.

At the top of the last zigzag the wind came over the shoulder of the mountain and hit them in the face, and there was Stillhollow.

It was white: frost-white, the hard glittering white of a thing that had been cold so long it had forgotten anything else. The town sat in a high bowl of rock with the peaks round it, and it was doorsteps, like every town. Two hundred or so, in neat terraces, swept bare by the wind. Not one was crooked. Even the ruins here were in rows.

Above the terraces, on a knob of rock, was a great ring of stone footings, fifty paces across, with a stub of a pier in the middle of it like the hub of a wheel. "The dome," said Wren, behind him. "The observatory." Beside it a square stump, knee-high. "Clock tower." And behind both, cut into the cliff at the back of the bowl, with steps going up to it and a stone lintel carved with stars, a door.

It was frosted over, an inch thick. Through the frost came the red, faint and pink, like a fire seen through a frozen window from a long way off down a lane.

"The archive," said Wren. "They kept the records in the rock. The fire couldn't reach it."

"Neither could anybody else," said Ash.

The road ran on past the bowl, up toward a notch between two peaks where the snow lay blue. A mile above the town, in the mouth of that notch, sat a row of long stone sheds with turf roofs, and a walled yard beside them.

They went up to look, because Ash wanted to.

The sheds were full. Furs in bales. Salt pork in barrels, frozen to stone. Flour in chests sealed with wax. Tents rolled and strapped, a crate of bone snow-goggles. And on the long wall of the last shed, on pegs, pack-saddles. Wren walked down the row of them with her hand out, not touching.

Every saddle had a name chalked on the flap. The chalk had frozen into the leather and stayed.

"Two hundred and six," said Wren at the end of the row. "Everyone. Every name in the town."

"They were going," said Tobin. "All of them. Over the top."

"When?"

Ash looked at the empty mule pen, its trough a block of green ice, and down at the dome's footings on their knob. "When they knew it was coming," he said. "They had a dome. They watched things coming. That's what it was for."

Pip stopped at a saddle near the end, put her finger on the chalk, and said, "*Provost.* With a capital. Not even his name. Just *Provost.*" She looked at Wren. "Funny what people keep to themselves."

Wren didn't answer. Tobin looked from one of them to the other and didn't know what he was looking at, Ash could see, and Ash did, and said nothing, because he'd had a word in his mouth once too, and his mouth had paid for it.

They went down to the archive steps in the blue dusk. The frost on the door had a shape melted in it at about the height of a man's chest, and when Ash put his hand flat on it he felt, through an inch of ice, the warm.

### 6.2

*The Orrery, floors 1 to 3: the dome, the clockwork halls, the grinding room.*

The first floor was the dome, the one that wasn't on the knob any more, and it was turning.

Tobin stood in the door with his breath going up in front of him and watched it go round. Under a ribbed roof of brass and glass the floor wasn't a floor: it was rings, one inside the next like the rings of a cut tree, iron walkways with little stone offices and stairs built onto them, each turning at its own pace with a low grinding like a mill wheel. A stair went by with nothing at the top of it. In the middle, on a pier, sat a lens as big as a cartwheel, gone milky with ice.

"I'm not going on that," said Pip.

"You'll go on it," said Ash, "because the stair down's on the third ring and it's going the wrong way."

The key was on a desk in the nearest office, frozen to the blotter beside an astronomer's cold tea. It was brass, the length of Tobin's forearm, with a bow shaped like a star of eight points, and when Wren put it in the lock by the door the outer ring stopped dead with a clank that went up through Tobin's boots, and the second ring went on turning.

"One at a time," said Wren. "It stops one, and the others carry on." She turned it again, and the outer ring went backward a notch. "Or it turns it. Ash."

"Two notches back," said Ash. "Then stop it. Then the second. Then wait."

They crossed the dome like that, and things came at them off the turning rings. Knights of brass and frost with a sundial for a head and the dial's blade for an arm, who swung when their ring brought them past. Spiders the size of shields, made of nested brass circles that turned against each other as they walked, so they seemed to be counting something with their legs. When Tobin split one, it went on turning in two halves on the floor.

The cold got into the new pink skin of his palms until he couldn't feel the axe-haft except as a weight.

"How are your hands?" said Wren.

"How are yours?"

She didn't answer. She kept her right hand in its bandage, and turned the key with her left, and he stood on her left side and wished he couldn't see her hands at all.

The second floor was clockwork, iced and ticking, a hall of wheels the size of millstones turning in the walls, and they found the clockmaker in a clock. He was the pendulum. He hung in a long glass case, upside down, a thin old man in a waistcoat with a bob of brass at his feet, swinging, with his arms folded over his chest and a look of fierce concentration, and every swing the whole hall ticked.

Tobin stopped him with one hand, the way you'd stop a swing.

The man opened his eyes upside down, and glared. "I was *counting*," he said.

"Sorry."

"You don't just *stop* a man." He got himself right way up and brushed his waistcoat down. "Jasper Tock. Clocks. Where's my tower?"

"Gone," said Pip. "There's a stump."

"A stump." He took that like a death. "I want it back. I want to lie in bed and hear it strike and know it's right, because I made it right." He looked at the iced wheels in the walls, all running slightly wrong. "I built this. The real one, a model of the heavens for the Provost's dome. Never a second out. Not like this. This is what it'd look like if you'd never seen the sky."

The warm took him while he was still complaining about it, and he called down that the tower's bell had a crack in it and they were to tell nobody.

The third floor was the grinding room, and Pip found the lens-wright.

It was a mill of glass: flat stone wheels grinding slowly against each other, and wherever they met, a grey slurry of ground glass and water that took your boots. The wheels had to be turned so their dry tops met, a path at a time, while the knights came round on them, and Ash got two turns wrong and Pip went into the slurry to the knee and came out swearing in words Tobin hadn't known she knew.

At the centre, on the last wheel, stood a pane of glass taller than a man, perfectly clear, and inside it, set in it like a fly in amber, a woman.

She was forty or so, spectacles on a cord, and even asleep inside glass she had the look of somebody who has just noticed you're standing in her light. Tobin put the axe-butt to the pane, gently. It cracked top to bottom as if scored and came apart in two clean halves, and she stepped out between them and opened her eyes and said, "Thumbs."

"What?"

"You touched the glass with your thumbs. I can see the prints. Edda Quill. Lens-wright. Don't touch anything. Where's my lens?"

"On the first floor," said Ash. "In the ice."

"Is it scratched?"

"I didn't look."

"Nobody ever looks." She put her spectacles on. "I want the dome back. The lens clean. And nobody in my light." She said it the way Clemency had said it, as if she'd had it ready for ten years. "Ever. Not the Provost, not anyone."

"Why not the Provost?" said Pip.

Edda Quill looked at her properly for the first time. Her eyes were very pale behind the spectacles, and very clear.

"I ground the lens they charted it with," she said. "The curve. I asked once what the line was, going up and up for forty years. He said, 'Weather, Edda.' I said, weather doesn't go up for forty years, Provost, weather goes round. So he told me." She held the two halves of her pane against her chest. "And then he gave me a saddle. With my name on it. In chalk."

The warm took her, and took the lens on the first floor too, Tobin found later, up out of the dome like a moon rising: the first piece out of the Orrery, and the biggest.

### 6.3

*Floors 4 and 5: the drawers and the Curve. Then the bank.*

The archivist was filed, and Wren envied her.

The fourth floor was a vault of drawers, thousands, down halls that turned on the rings, each labelled in a neat clerk's hand: *Weather, PY 12. Harvest returns, Saltwake, PY 30-35.* Between them walked the frost-clerks, men and women of clear ice in clerks' sleeves, who breathed on doors and sealed them. Wren burned the frost off the first two with small warm marks. After the second she put her right hand in her coat and let Ash find the long way.

Prudence Ames was in a drawer labelled *Ames, P., archivist*, on her back with her hands folded and a ring of keys on her belt, and when Tobin pulled it out she opened her eyes and said, "That's not your drawer," and then, "Oh," and then, after a long look at Wren, "You'll be a Hale."

"Yes."

"Your mother wrote to the archive once. Asked for the copy. The Provost said no." Prudence climbed out of the drawer with great dignity, and put a hand to the keys, and counted them by feel. "I want the archive door, and the key on my belt. That's all. That's always been all."

"What's in the archive?" said Ash.

Prudence looked at him with the pleasant blankness of a woman who'd been asked that by better people. "Everything," she said, "in order." And the warm took her, still holding her keys.

The fifth floor was the Curve.

It was a round chamber under a painted sky, and it turned, all of it, so you felt it in your stomach before your eyes caught up. Round the walls lay a serpent of brass.

Not a serpent. Wren saw it first, because she'd spent her life with figures: a line on a chart, beaten into brass and given a head. Along its back, scale by scale, were numbers. *PY 1. PY 2.* It lay low for its first coils and then began to rise, steadily, until past *PY 40* it reared off the wall in a great arc, and at its head, where the line went highest, it was *PY 50*, and the head had a mouth.

And every time the room turned, it grew. A new scale clicked out behind its head. *PY 51.* Higher.

In its lowest coil, near the tail, wrapped neatly round as if tucked into bed, slept an old man in a fur-collared robe.

"The hunger," said Wren. "That's what they charted. Every year's tithe, against the year." She heard her own voice, very level. "They knew. They knew exactly when."

The Curve came off the wall the way a line comes off a page, faster than anything that size should move, and took a bite out of the floor where Tobin had been. The room turned. *PY 52.* It came again, and Pip went flat under her shield and it went over her with a sound like a saw.

"It grows when the room turns," said Ash. He was half up the wall on a carved star, shooting at the joints. "Every turn. If we leave it, it'll fill the room—"

"Then turn it back," said Wren.

The lock was in the floor at the hub of the room, a star-shaped hole, and Wren got to it on her knees with the key, and put it in, and turned it the wrong way. Against the room.

It went hard, like a frozen tap. The room stopped. Shuddered. Went back a notch, groaning. And at the serpent's head, a scale clicked *in*.

*PY 51.*

"Keep it there," said Wren. "Tobin. *Keep it there.*"

He put his hands over hers on the key and leaned on it, and the Curve screamed, brass on brass, and pulled in another scale. *PY 50.* Pip and Ash kept it off them. *PY 40, 30, 20.* With every scale it got lower and slower, until it was a thin bright line lying flat along the floor, and the last number at its head said *PY 1*, and Pip stood on it.

It didn't move. They stood in the still room breathing smoke.

"That's what it looked like," said Ash. "At the start. Flat. Nobody would have worried."

Wren took her hands off the key. The left one shook. Tobin let go of hers last.

The old man woke up when Tobin unwound the brass from him, and he did it all at once, the way Wren did, with his eyes already working. He was seventy or more, white-haired, clean-shaven, with a long thin nose and long thin hands, and he didn't look at the room or the dead line on the floor. He looked at each of them in turn, quickly, and Wren had the sense of something being added up behind his eyes.

"Ambrose Wyck," he said. "Provost of Stillhollow." His voice was dry and courteous and quite calm. "And you are the four from Hollowmere. I'd heard there were four of you." He paused, a hair too long. "From the clerks."

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren.

"My study in the dome," said the Provost. "The pass road visible from the window." He was looking at her still. At her bandaged hand, and the staff, and the brass foot of it. "I'd like to see the pass road."

The warm took him up with his long hands folded, still looking at her.

The bank was a little round observatory: blue coals, a basin of meltwater that steamed when you touched it, iron map chests for strongboxes, a lift on a chain. And where the other dungeons had kept their lanterns and floats and checks and comb, a celestial globe the size of a barrel, rimed with frost, pricked all over with tiny lights. As Wren watched, one went out.

"Stars," said Pip. "One each." She looked at it for a long time. "There's a lot."

"Home," said Tobin.

Wren put her right hand in the steaming basin. She couldn't feel the heat in the first finger at all, and when she took it out the others were pink and that one was the same colour as the frost on the globe, and Pip was watching her, and then Pip wasn't.

### 6.4

*Stillhollow. Ten days on. The night of the signal.*

Pip was on the dome steps when the lens moved, because she couldn't sleep, because she was at three, and three was a search.

Ten days. Stillhollow had come up out of the frost in neat rows, the way it had lain down. The dome went back on its knob, and Edda's lens into its cradle with Edda underneath shouting *left, your left*. Jasper Tock's tower went up beside it, with a cracked bell that made every hour sound faintly embarrassed. Prudence got her archive door, new oak in the old stone frame, and the key on her belt. The Provost got his study high in the dome under the lens, with a window that looked straight up the pass road to the stone sheds full of saddles.

The people were polite. They came up their shafts and thanked the four exactly, as if checking a bill. *Thank you. That's adequate. You've put the step a hand too far east.* Nobody wept. Nobody asked about the children's table, though eleven of their children were at it; Pip had watched Wren add the names to the column in a hand that shook. They asked what day it was, and corrected their clocks.

On the eighth day Prudence had shown them the archive, long cold rooms back into the rock, all labelled, smelling faintly of the red, which came up somewhere at the back where she didn't take them. She showed them the curve in the Provost's own hand. She showed them a box labelled *Correspondence: Hollowmere, the Elder*.

The first letters were signed. *Corwin Vane.* About the famine, and the ground, and a woman called Ellen, and a grave that took two days. Pip didn't read those. She could see Ash reading them, and that was enough.

The rest weren't signed. One a year, folded small, on good paper. They'd come back up the tithe road in the empty carts, Prudence said, every autumn for fifty years, and the Provost read each one and burned it, and Prudence copied each one first, because that was what an archive was for. The last had come in the weeks after Hollowmere fell, with no cart at all; it had simply been on the archive step one morning. The Provost had kept that one.

They all said the same thing. Wren read one aloud. *The account is in order. Thank you for your light.*

"Light?" said Tobin.

"The signal." Prudence had been surprised he didn't know. "When our tithe went down, the Provost flashed it from the dome. Three long and one short. So the steward would know it was on its way." She'd put the letter back in its box, in its place. "We were the only town that told him. We were very proud of that."

That night at supper in the Provost's study, high under the lens, the Provost had asked Wren to read him a line from one of his own old papers. Politely. A line in the script, written very small at the foot of that last letter, under the plain ink. *I was only ever the clerk. I could copy the old hand, never read it. I've always wondered.* And Wren had looked at it, and at him, and said, "It's a date. The day it was sent," which Pip knew was a lie the moment it left her, because Wren had read it too fast and put it down too slowly. The Provost had thanked her, exactly. He hadn't looked at the paper again. He'd looked at Wren's right hand.

Now it was past midnight, the town below silver and blue under a sky so full of stars it looked spilled, and Ash came out of the porter's lodge at the foot of the steps where the four of them slept, and sat two steps below her.

She had it in her mouth again.

*Ash. Her book. There's a page in it from the scriptorium, and she said it burned, and it didn't, and I've seen the edge of it three times now, and the Provost knows something, and she knows he knows—*

"Don't," said Ash, without turning round.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to." He pulled his collar up. "Whatever it is. It's hers to say."

"And if she doesn't?"

Ash was quiet a while. "Then it'll get said anyway," he said. "Things do. I'd know." He touched his nose, which had set crooked, the way people touch a scar to see if it still hurts.

Pip swallowed it. She felt it go down, like the honey, sweet and wrong. Then she looked up because the stars over the dome had moved.

It was the lens, turning in its cradle with a lamp behind it, throwing a beam out of the dome as thick as a tree trunk. It swung across the snowfields and came to rest pointing south and west, over the shoulder of the mountain, to where Ash's maps said the road went down into the mountain's lap. To the Hearth.

It went out. It came on. Long. Out. Long. Out. Long.

Out.

Short.

"Three long," said Ash, very quietly, "and one short."

Pip was already on her feet and up the steps, and the dome door was locked, oak and iron, and she beat on it with her fist. High above her, in the study window under the lens, a thin figure in a fur collar stood with its long hands folded and looked down the pass road, and didn't look at her at all.

The Gleaners came at the next stroke of the hour.

Jasper's cracked bell rang one, sounding sorry about it, and at the back of the bowl Prudence's new oak door opened. Pip saw the red come out of it first, a long pink glow on the snow, and then them: coming down the archive steps in a slow file, one by one, out of the rock into Stillhollow's streets. Thirty. Forty. The frost settled on their sheaf masks and turned them white, and their ember cracks steamed in the cold so each one walked in a little cloud of its own, and every one of them carried a plate, and on every plate something steamed.

It was the feast. Pip could smell it from the steps. Pork in cider. Baked apples. Dark bread.

Ash was running for the lodge. Pip ran after him, and then stopped, in the middle of the street, because the Gleaners weren't coming for the lodge.

They were knocking on doors.

They went down the terraces, neat as Stillhollow, one to a house, and each climbed a doorstep and knocked, politely, twice, and stood holding out its plate. Pip watched one at a door with a comet for a knocker. Behind the shutter a lamp went out. The Gleaner waited with the plate steaming in its scorched hands, then went down the step to the next house, and knocked there.

"Go *away*," shouted Edda Quill through her door, up by the dome. "I'm *working*."

Nobody opened. All down the terraces, in every house the four had built, the lamps went out one by one, and the doors stayed shut, and the Gleaners knocked and waited and went on. One stood at Jasper Tock's door holding a plate of baked apples up to the window, and the curtain twitched, and Jasper's thin old hand drew it shut.

They could have broken the doors; a sickle through a shutter was nothing. They didn't. They knocked, and waited, and when nobody gave them anything they went on.

"They can't," said Ash beside her. He'd come back out with his bow. His voice was strange. "They can't take anybody. They're not owed any more. It had them, and it let them go. All it can do is ask." He laughed, a short horrible sound. "And nobody here's ever given anybody anything."

Then the Gleaners reached the porter's lodge, and knocked, twice, and Tobin didn't open it, and they opened it themselves.

The door came off its hinges as if it were paper. Pip understood it all at once, with her whole body, like falling through ice. *They only knock where there's nothing for them.*

Tobin came out of the lodge backward with the axe going, and Wren behind him, and the Gleaners came in from both sides, and not one of them so much as looked at Tobin. They went round him. They went round Ash, round Pip, the way water goes round stones, all of them turning their frosted straw faces the same way, holding out their plates. To Wren.

"*Wren*—"

They had her down in the snow by the third breath. Pip saw it between bodies: a Gleaner bigger than Tobin kneeling on the staff across Wren's chest, gently, almost kindly, with a plate in one hand and a wooden spoon in the other, and on the spoon a piece of dark feast bread soaked in gravy, coming down toward Wren's shut mouth.

Pip went through them.

She didn't remember how, after. She remembered Gran's shield going edge-first into straw, and a sickle past her ear so close it took a lock of her hair, and not caring. She remembered a plate coming at her own face, held out, *you've been hungry for ten years*, and knocking it into the snow without looking at it. She remembered being at three, and three was a search, and none of it mattering. She got to the big one as the spoon touched Wren's lip and put Gran's dagger through its wrist, and the spoon went into the snow, and the big one turned its white mask and looked at her, and she put the shield-rim under its chin and it went over backward.

Wren was up on one elbow, spitting, wiping her mouth with her bandaged hand over and over. "Did any—did I—"

"No," said Pip. "No. You didn't. I saw."

Wren looked at her. Just for a moment, in the red light and the snow and the steam off the plates. Pip didn't know what was in her face. She didn't want to.

Then Wren was on her feet, and writing.

She wrote small and hot, marks that made the Gleaners flinch back with their plates, and Tobin came down on them from her left like a falling tree, and Ash shot from the lodge roof, and it was bad, for a long time. They didn't try to hurt anyone. They just kept coming round the others to Wren, holding out food, and every time one went down another stepped up with a plate. Pip took a sickle across the forearm and didn't feel it until later. Wren wrote until a mark went into a mask and nothing happened, and she stared at her hand.

And all round them, in the houses they'd built, the shutters were open now, just a crack, and Stillhollow watched. Nobody came out.

The whistle came down from the archive steps.

Three notes, going down: every autumn of Pip's childhood, at the head of the tithe oxen. The Gleaners stopped as if a string had been cut. Then they lowered their plates and walked back through the snow toward the red door in their little clouds of steam, the way the carters walked home after the weighing.

Abner Stroud stood at the top of the archive steps with the whistle at his mouth, in his mask. He let the others file past him into the rock. Then he looked down the length of the town at Wren, standing in the trampled snow with her hand over her mouth.

He stood there longer than he needed to. Pip saw his hand go up to his mask, to the straw at his chin, as if he meant to take it off.

He didn't. He turned and went in, and the red door shut behind him, and the red went out of the snow.

They had taken no one. Pip counted the houses twice, the way Gran counted at the gate, and every door was shut, and every lamp came back on, one by one, all down the terraces. Stillhollow had given nothing. It was the only thing in the Vale, she thought, that Stillhollow had ever been right about.

At dawn Edda Quill came down the dome steps in her nightgown and spectacles with a lamp in one hand, white with fury.

"Somebody," she said, "has had my lens off its mark."

### 6.5

*Stillhollow, the square below the dome. That morning.*

The Provost told the town himself, in the end, because Wren asked him to in a voice Ash had never heard her use on anyone, and because, Ash thought, he'd already done the sum and seen that it came out the same either way.

He stood on the dome steps in his fur collar with the whole town below him in the trampled snow, among the dropped plates, frozen, with the feast on them. Nobody had touched those either.

"I flashed the signal," said Ambrose Wyck. He didn't raise his voice. Stillhollow's square had been built so you didn't need to. "Three long and one short, as I did every autumn for twenty-one years. The steward received it. You saw the result."

"Why?" said Edda Quill. She was at the front, in a coat over her nightgown, with her arms folded.

The Provost looked at Wren. He'd been looking at her since he came out.

"Miss Hale," he said. "The line at the foot of the letter. What did it say?"

Wren didn't answer. Then she did. "*Four places are still laid.*"

A sound went through the square, not loud, like wind through a frozen hedge.

"Four places," said the Provost. "At Hollowmere's table. Four children who never ate, so never paid. While those seats are empty the account stays open, and an open account, as anyone in this square can tell you, accrues. I have spent my life on curves. Fill four seats and the account closes, and perhaps it sleeps. Perhaps for good. Four, against six towns. I did the arithmetic. I'm not ashamed of the arithmetic."

"You'd have fed her," said Tobin. His hands were shaking on the axe-haft. Ash watched them. "You'd have held her down and fed her."

"I wouldn't have touched her. I made a light." The Provost turned his eyes to Ash, of all of them, and something in his face eased, as if he'd found someone who spoke his language. "You'll understand, I think. You're his. You've the look."

Ash didn't say anything. He was thinking of seventeen lamps going out up a lane at a walking pace, one by one, and how reasonable it had all sounded on the scree.

"There's something else," said the Provost, to Wren again. "Since we're being thorough. I was twenty-two when I copied the Bargain into the archive. I was the elder's clerk. Your Elder Vane stood at my shoulder the whole night while I copied it, letter by letter, not one of which I could read. When I came to the last line, he put his hand flat on the page and told me I was done." He folded his hands. "I've wondered for fifty years what was under it. Whatever it was, Miss Hale, the steward wants you at that table more than he has wanted anything in sixty years. A sensible man gives the steward what he wants."

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then Edda Quill unfolded her arms, and turned round, and walked away up the street to the dome, and went in, and shut the door.

Jasper Tock went next. Then Prudence, her keys at her belt. Then a woman with a baby, and a row of muleteers in sheepskins, and the schoolmaster. They didn't look at him. They turned their backs on the Provost of Stillhollow one by one and walked up their swept steps and shut their doors, until the square held nothing but frozen plates and four people from Hollowmere and an old man on a step.

Nobody touched him. Tobin wanted to. Ash could see exactly how much, in his shoulders. He didn't.

"That's all, then," said the Provost, to the empty square. His voice was quite steady. "That's how it's done here." And he turned and went up into his dome, under his lens, to his window that looked up the pass road.

Wren was standing very still with her bandaged hand against her coat, on the left side, low.

"I'm going home," she said.

"Wren—" said Tobin.

"I'm going home. I need to ask my mother something." She didn't look at any of them. "The floor will turn anyway. We'll come back."

Pip was looking at Wren's hand on her coat. She didn't say anything at all, and that frightened Ash more than anything she could have said.

It was four days down to Hollowmere, by Thistledown and the drovers' road. Nobody talked much on any of them.

### 6.6

*Hollowmere, Wren's house. The night they came home.*

Tuppence sat on the cost book and wouldn't move.

Wren had gone straight upstairs to her mother, and somewhere in that the cost book had been left on the table, and the cat had got on it. He sat square on the cover with his paws tucked under and the fur along his spine up in a ridge from his ears to his tail.

"Off," said Pip.

He looked at her with his one good ear back.

"It's not *yours*," said Pip, and then heard herself, and stood there in Wren's kitchen with the fire going down and the wall of names behind her, and Mrs Hale's chair creaking overhead, and Wren's voice up there, low and fast, the way it never was.

She picked the cat up. He let her, which he never did. He went stiff in her arms, and his fur stood up under her hands, crackling, as if the book had been a ward stone.

The book had fallen open where it was thickest. The brown page was there, curled, black along the bottom.

She told herself afterwards that she only meant to look at the edge. She read it by the last of the fire, slowly, her lips moving.

The children, every one in plain ink, in a hand like Wren's but steadier: Tilly Rowe, the Pell twins, the Stroud boys. Five ticks in the margin. One struck through.

*O.O. — P.*

Gran. Gran and Pip, two letters and a dash, as if Pip were a sack. She'd known. Since Gran's doorstep in the winter she'd known Gran had told her the tunnel story on purpose. She hadn't known she was on a list. She hadn't known there was a list, in somebody else's hand, with other names on it.

*H.M. — T., N.* Tobin. Nell. *J.C. — A.* *I.H. — W.*

*Carried five to two. I.H. for. Five is mischief. Ten is a search.*

And at the bottom, in the other letters, the ones only Wren could read, a line that ran into the black and stopped.

She was still standing there with it when the stair creaked and Wren came down.

Wren saw her and stopped on the third step. Pip watched her see: the page, the open book, the cat on the floor with his fur up. Her face did nothing at all.

"Put it back," said Wren.

"*It burned*," said Pip. "In the scriptorium. *There's nothing left to see.* That's two. In the orchard, to him, *It burned*. Three. And in Stillhollow you told the Provost his letter was a date." She held it up. "That's four, Wren. Four's not mischief. Gran'd have had you in the watch-house at two."

"Pip. Put it back."

Pip looked at the page in her hand, at two letters and a dash. She thought of Gran at the gate, giving a man one night to come back with what he'd taken, because that was fair, because you gave people the chance to be better than you'd counted them.

She put it back in the book, carefully, and shut the cover, and laid the book on the table where it had been.

"Tomorrow," she said. "At the meeting. You tell them. All of it, the ticks, the vote, whatever that line is. Or I lay it on the table myself." Her voice wobbled and she let it. "One night. That's what Gran gives at the gate. That's what you get."

Wren didn't say anything. She stood on the third step with her bandaged hand on the rail.

Pip went out. On the step she heard Tuppence go back up onto the table, and sit down on the book, and the hasp of something in Wren's head go over, so loud it might have been real.

### 6.7

*Wren's house. The meeting, the next night.*

Wren didn't tell them, and Tobin would remember afterwards that he'd seen it coming and done nothing, because he was used to waiting for her to decide.

She talked about the Orrery, with Ash's maps on the table and the cost book beside them, shut, her bandaged hand flat on it, in the voice she used for plans, smooth and with no edges anywhere. Tobin knew that voice now. Gideon Hask had had one. He'd had one himself, for his father.

Pip sat on the hearth and said nothing at all. Tuppence lay on the dresser with his back to everyone.

When Wren had finished, there was a silence.

"Same time—" said Tobin.

Pip got up. She walked to the table and put out her hand, and Wren's hand on the cost book didn't move, and Pip didn't try to take it. She reached past it, into Wren's coat, which was over the back of the chair, and took out a folded brown page that Tobin had never seen, and laid it flat on the table between the cups.

"She moved it," said Pip. "Last night. After I'd seen." She pushed it across to Tobin with one finger. "Read it. The ticks."

He could read plain ink; Wren had taught him at this table, years ago. He read his sister's name, and the five little ticks, and the line through the fifth, and then, beside them, the letters.

*H.M. — T., N.*

He read it four times. It didn't change.

"Da," he said.

Nobody answered. Ash had pulled the page round to him. Tobin watched him find his own: *J.C. — A.* Ash's face went very still, the way it had in the timber yard with blood running into his ears.

"Jory walked the tunnel," said Ash. "The day before. He told me. He said he'd been surveying." He looked up at Wren. "It was a list. It was a plan. Your mother's."

"Five children," said Pip. "Five out of twenty-eight. Gran got me. His da got him and Nell, only Nell didn't come. Jory got Ash. And she got—" She pointed at the last line of initials without looking at it. "She knew the tithe was short. She voted to water it anyway. She knew what that meant and she picked five of us and let the rest of them sit down. Tilly Rowe sat next to you at school, Wren."

"I know whose names are on my wall," said Wren.

Tobin heard who'd said that first. So did Pip; he saw her flinch.

"When?" said Tobin.

Wren looked at him. "The scriptorium. I read it in the fire."

"And the ticks. In the Undercroft. On the tally. You saw five." He was hearing himself say it, slowly, the way you put your weight on a board you think is rotten. "A year ago. I stood at that wall and told you about Nell, and you said *I know*. You didn't know. You'd seen five ticks and a line through one, and you said *I know*."

"I didn't know what they meant."

"*Liar*," said Pip. "That's five."

"I didn't know *then*." Wren's voice was rising, and Tobin had never once heard Wren's voice rise. "I worked it out. In the orchard, in the scriptorium. And she did the sums, Tobin. Ten children gone from the table is a search before the meal. Five is mischief. If she'd taken more, nobody would have—"

"Don't," said Tobin. "Don't do her sums at me."

She stopped.

"And the line at the bottom," said Pip. "The burned one. In her letters. Yours." She leaned on the table. "What's it say?"

"It's burned."

"Six," said Pip. Her eyes were very bright. She had her mouth open, and Tobin knew that look. He'd known it since she had milk teeth. It was the look she'd had on the crate on Saltwake quay with Gideon Hask's words in her mouth, when she knew exactly where the true thing would hurt most and was going to put it there because it would feel good. "You know what you are? You're her. You've got her face. You'd have put your hand up. You'd put it up *tomorrow*, you'd pick us four over the whole table and do the sums after and tell us it *burned*, you'd—"

Wren stood up, and lifted the staff from where it leaned by her chair, and wrote.

It was small. That was the worst of it, after, when Tobin thought about it, which was every night for a long time. Not a door-sized letter of gold like the one in the Hive. One short mark, the brass foot going down short and up long, as neat as a tick in a cost book, and she said it under her breath, and it went across the table like a draught under a door.

Pip's mouth went on moving, and nothing came out of it. Not a breath. Tobin watched her understand. He watched her hand go to her throat, and her whole face strain, Pip who had never in her life been able to bear a silence, who sang under her breath in the dark so she'd know she was there. Her eyes went to Wren. Tobin had seen Pip frightened in a hundred places. He had never seen her look like that.

One breath. Two. He counted them, and so did Pip; he saw her count.

Three.

Pip's voice came back as a small cracked sound in the bottom of her throat, like a door hinge. She closed her mouth on it.

Nobody spoke. Ash had gone white. Tuppence had come off the dresser and was standing on the floor in the middle of the room with every hair on his body standing straight out, looking at Wren.

Wren put the staff down. Her bandaged hand was shaking. The forefinger came out of the bandage, and the grey on it, which had stopped at the first knuckle since the Hive, crept past it now while he watched, flat and dead, and stopped at the second. Tobin had seen that finger before. On Mrs Hale's hand on the cat, the night she came home.

Pip turned round and walked out of the house. She didn't slam the door. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to.

Ash got up, and looked at Wren for a long moment, and followed her.

"Tobin," said Wren.

He stood up. He looked at the page on the table, at two letters and a comma and a single letter after it with a line through its tick, and at Wren standing by her wall with her mother's finger on her hand. He thought of a hundred things to say. Every one of them was true and every one of them would have felt good, and he understood for the first time, standing there, why Pip did it.

He didn't say any of them. He walked out, and shut the door behind him quietly, the way you shut it on someone asleep.

### 6.8

*Wren's house, upstairs. The next night.*

The lamp stayed dark. Wren had lit it every night for ten years, before she'd had a house to put it in, and she sat upstairs and let it stay dark, and looked out of her mother's window at the lane, and nobody came up it.

From the window she could see most of Hollowmere. A lantern at the bottom of the gate lane, where Odile's chair stood by Odile's door. A light in the timber yard, low, the kind you drink by. Up above the church, where the scree began, nothing at all, which told her nothing, because Ash had never needed a light up there.

Behind her, her mother was packing.

She did it in order. A satchel on the bed. Two shirts, folded in thirds. A small knife. The good pen, wrapped in a cloth. A list on Hob's desk, each thing ticked as it went in.

"You didn't light it," said her mother.

"No."

Isolde didn't ask why. She looked at Wren's right hand on the windowsill. Then she put down the shirt she was folding and came and took the hand and turned it over in the candlelight, and looked at the finger, grey now to the second knuckle, the same as her own. She didn't touch it.

"Who?"

"Pip."

"How long?"

"Three breaths."

"It's the smallest word there is," said her mother. "It doesn't want much." She let go. "But it's a word you can't take back. You can stop saying it. That isn't the same."

She went back to the satchel.

"Where are you going?" said Wren.

"Over the pass. When this is done. Your Stillhollow road." Her mother folded the shirt in thirds. "I've been a list in this town long enough. I don't want to be one at every door for the rest of my life."

"You'll leave me."

"You'll have the house," said her mother, "and the wall, and the cat." She said it as if checking a column. Then she stopped with her hands on the satchel and didn't say anything for a moment, and Wren saw her back, straight as a beam, and couldn't see her face.

"The last line," said Wren. "On your page. Where did you read it?"

"At the foot of every tally I ever wrote." Isolde turned round. "It's at the foot of every page that ever went down. The form came up from below the first autumn, and every ledger-keeper in the Vale copied it out by eye for fifty years, the names in plain ink and that line at the bottom in its own hand, like a seal. Not one of them could read it. I could."

"Then tell me what it says."

"No."

"*Why?*"

"Because if I tell you," said her mother, "then I put you there. And if you read it yourself, you chose."

Wren stared at her.

"That's for you," she said. "Not for me."

"Yes," said Isolde Hale. "Most things are."

Wren turned back to the window. The lane was empty. The lantern at the gate lane hadn't moved.

"He burned it," she said. "The page. He set the candles in the scriptorium so the line would go."

"He would. He's been hiding it for sixty years. He hid it from his own clerk." Her mother tied the satchel shut, and set it on the floor by the bed, ready, and sat down at Hob's desk. "One more thing. Since you keep books now. You should have the figures."

Wren waited.

"When it ends," said her mother, "if it ends, the letters end with it. They're its hand. When the hand's gone there's nothing to write with. Every ward in the Vale. The church. The cat's collar." She laid her own grey finger on the desk. "That. You'll write the last thing you ever write in its letters, and it'll go out under your hand like a candle, and you'll be a girl with a stick."

The candle burned.

"Have you told anyone?" said Wren.

"I've told you."

Wren looked out at the dark lane, and the dark lamp in her own window below, and the light in the timber yard where two shapes sat, one big and one bigger, not talking. She thought of Pip's face with no sound coming out of it. She thought about telling them, all of it, every box she had.

She put this one in a box of its own, and didn't close the hasp on it. She left it there, open, and found that it was the only one she had that didn't feel like a lie. It was hers. Nobody else would ever have to carry it.

Nobody came up the lane. At three in the morning she went down and sat at the table in the dark, in front of the wall, and didn't light anything.

### 6.9

*Wren's house. The night after.*

Pip saw the lamp from Gran's chair, and for a long while she didn't get up.

She'd slept in it the night before, by Gran's door, under Gran's old watch blanket, and in the morning Gran had come out and looked at her and gone back in and come out again with tea, and put it on the step where Pip could reach it. They didn't talk much, since the doorstep. The tea was Gran's whole vocabulary, and Pip had drunk it.

Now Gran stood in the door behind her, looking up the lane.

"Lamp's lit," said Gran.

"So?"

"So somebody's sat up there on their own." Odile Ostrander folded her arms. "I'd want to know who, if I were on the gate."

Pip went.

It was Tobin, alone at Wren's table with the lamp trimmed too high and his big burned hands flat on the wood. He looked up, and didn't say anything, and she sat down in her place.

It was a long time before Ash came in. He sat down, and after a while took his hands out of his coat and put them on the table, and Pip saw they were warm, and saw him see her see it.

"I took Jory's stones off," he said. "Last night. The cairn." His voice was flat. "I sat up there till the grey with my hands on it, and it didn't say anything. Not one word. All night. It just waited." He looked at his hands. "That's worse. I didn't know that'd be worse."

"I was in the yard," said Tobin. "With Da." He turned the lamp down, carefully. "He was drinking. I asked him about the page. What she'd written. He said it wasn't his to tell." He breathed out. "Then he cried. I've never seen him cry. Not for Mam, even."

The stair creaked. Wren came down it. She'd been up there all along; it was her house. She stopped on the bottom step and looked at the three of them, and then she came and sat down in her own chair, with her bandaged hand in her lap, and the lamp lit all four of them, and the wall.

Tobin didn't wait for anyone.

"I left Nell," he said.

Nobody moved.

"At the trestles. On the feast night. Da gave me the axe and told me to take her, three times, and I said she was slow. She had my sleeve. I pulled it out of her hand and told her to go and sit with Da, and she cried. The real kind, with no breath. And I didn't look round." He was looking at the wall, at his sister's name on the Marsh line, with its tick. "I told Da she wandered off. I told her I came back for her. I never came back. She was five and I was faster. That's the whole of it. That's all it ever was."

Pip had known. Since the coal bin on the seventh floor, a year ago, *you're a good liar*, she'd known the shape of it. It didn't help. It was different hearing it in his voice, in that room, with the lamp.

"I've told Wren," said Tobin. "Last year. Now I've told you. And I'm done." He looked round at them, one after another. "I'm done being the only one. Thirteen years I've been the grown-up in every room I'm in, since Mam. I carry it and you let me and then I hate you for it, and Wren's right, I do, I hate you for it. I'm not doing it any more." His voice cracked and he let it. "So you can all carry some of it now. That's what I lit the lamp for. You can all bloody carry some."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"I'll carry the haul," said Pip. Her voice came out rusty. It had come out rusty all day. "I always carry the haul."

Tobin made a noise that wasn't quite a laugh.

Wren reached into her coat and took out the brown page, and unfolded it, and laid it flat in the middle of the table, face up, where anyone could reach it. Then she put the cost book down beside it, open, at the back, where her own figures were.

"It's all in there," she said. "Everything I've written. The ticks. The staff memories I didn't tell you about. Saltwake and the Seams and the vote." She didn't look at the book. She looked at Pip. "I took your voice."

"Three breaths," said Pip. "I counted."

"So did I."

"Don't ever."

"No," said Wren. "Never."

Pip didn't forgive her. She'd have liked to, and she sat there and looked for it, and it wasn't there yet. But she found she could sit at the table, and that was something, and she found that she'd stopped counting, and didn't know when.

Tuppence came in from the stair and walked the length of the table, past the page, past Ash's warm hands, and lay down on the open cost book with his paws tucked under and his fur flat.

"Same time tomorrow," said Tobin.

"We're going back up tomorrow," said Ash.

"Then same time up there."

"Where else would we go," said Pip.

### 6.10

*The Orrery, floors 6 to 8: the halls, the muleteer, the sealed records. A week on.*

The floor had turned while they were gone, and Ash had known it would and still stood at the bottom of the first stair and hated it.

They'd gone down from the bank, because the checkpoint was there. Last time the sixth floor had a route chalked on every door. Now the halls had swung round on their rings like gates, and the chalk pointed at frozen stone. Everything he'd learned had been put away in a drawer by somebody who didn't care.

"It's only a floor," said Wren beside him.

"I know it's only a floor."

"Then read it again." She held out the map board. "You're good at that."

He read it again. On the seventh floor, where an iced mule train went round and round a ring with nobody leading it, they found the guide asleep in the lead pannier, a lean brown woman in sheepskins with a purse on a string round her neck.

"Sorrel Kett," she said, waking, and felt for the purse at once. "Still there. Good. I was paid in advance." She looked at the frozen mules going round. "To take them over the top. The whole town. Spring of fifty-one." She laughed. "Never been paid for a job I didn't do before. I want my yard at the bottom of the pass. And the mules."

The eighth floor was the dream's archive, a vault of drawers going up into the dark. Nothing came here. At the end of the main hall, on a lectern of ice, lay a long sheet of parchment weighted with four brass stars.

*The Bargain. Copy. A. Wyck, clerk, PY 0.*

Wren read it aloud at the lectern. The letters were a clerk's careful copy of the script, every stroke made by somebody who didn't know what it meant and was terrified of getting it wrong. Most of it they knew. Rich fields. A yearly tithe, carried into the deep. The tithe to grow with every feeding. Whoever eats at the feast is party to it, from that meal to the next. The tithe weighed after the meal. *If the tithe falls short, the table is forfeit.* Wren read that line in the same voice as the others, and Ash watched Pip's face when she heard it said plainly in a cold room, and then Tobin's.

Then there was a little more, about the one who signs, and keeping, and a heart. Wren read it and stopped and read it again to herself, and didn't explain it, and Ash didn't ask, because he'd understood.

Below that, the parchment was blank. And in the margin, beside the blank, in plain ink, a young man's hand, very small:

*Here the Elder of Hollowmere put his hand on the page and told me I was done. A.W.*

"That's where yours burned," said Pip.

"Yes," said Wren.

Past the lectern, at the very end of the vault, there was an alcove with a chair in it.

A woman was asleep in the chair with a blanket over her knees. It had been tucked in. Someone had tucked it in round her feet the way you tuck in a child, carefully, so the draught wouldn't get at her, and it had stayed tucked for ten years. Beside the chair, on the stone floor, there were two shallow hollows, side by side, a stride's width apart. Worn into the stone. The shape of a pair of feet that had stood in one place, not moving, for a very long time.

Ash knew her before the lamp reached her face: dark hair going grey at the parting, a mouth that even asleep looked about to tell somebody off. He went down on his knees by the chair. He'd thought about this for ten years and never once got as far as this part.

"Mam," he said.

Hester Calder opened her eyes, and looked at him, and he watched her find him in his own face, the way Isolde had found Wren, a boy of nine somewhere under all of it.

"Ash?" She put her hand up and touched his cheek, then his nose, frowning. "What's happened to your nose?"

"Tobin Marsh broke it."

"*Did* he." She sat up, and the blanket slid off her knees, and her eyes went past him to Tobin, who stood with the lamp and looked as if he'd like the floor to take him. "I'll have his ears. Where's Jory?"

"Home. He came home at the thaw. He wants to build a hut on the scree." Ash heard his voice going and couldn't stop it. "He says my walls are out by a hand."

"They will be." She was holding his face in both hands now, warm. Then her face changed. She looked past him, at the hollows in the floor beside the chair. "There was a man," she said. "Standing there. The whole time. I couldn't see him. I couldn't open my eyes. But I knew he was there." She frowned, as if at a draught. "I could hear him not breathing."

Ash didn't say anything. Behind him he heard Wren's breath go in.

"Where do you want to live?" said Wren, gently.

His mother didn't look at her. She looked at Ash.

"Home," said Hester Calder. "Both my boys under one roof, and I don't care whose."

The warm came up out of the floor under the chair and took her, still holding his face until her hands lifted away from it, and she went up into the dark of the drawers calling down that he was to eat something, he was all bones, and the shaft closed.

Ash stayed on his knees. Then he got up and stood looking at the two hollows in the stone. Without meaning to, he stepped into them.

His feet fitted. Exactly. As if they'd been made for him.

He stepped out again fast, as if the stone were hot.

### 6.11

*Floor 9: the mirror floor.*

The floor turned while they were standing on it, and when it stopped, Pip was alone, and Wren was standing in front of her with a burning face.

Not Wren. It had her coat and her chin and her mother's staff, and her way of standing, as if about to correct your sums. But its face was cracked all over like a dropped plate, glowing red from inside, and when it smiled it was Wren's small tight smile with fire behind the teeth.

The room was round, walls of polished brass that showed Pip a hundred small frightened Pips, turning slowly. Through the brass she could hear the others.

"You liked it," said the thing with Wren's face, in Wren's voice. Quite gently. "The crowd. Him on the cobbles."

Pip's hand went tight on the dagger.

"You stood on the crate and you said it like a bell, and they went for him, and you watched his leg go. And it felt good. It felt better than anything. Better than finding the tunnels." It tilted its head. "You'd do it again tonight if anybody gave you a crate."

Pip went for it.

It lifted the staff and wrote, short down and long up, a tick in a cost book, and Pip's voice went.

It went the way it had at the table, all at once, a door shut. She opened her mouth to scream and nothing came out, and the thing smiled Wren's smile and came at her with the brass foot, and Pip took it on Gran's shield and the rim rang like a bell and made no other sound at all, and she fought it in silence.

One breath. Two. Three. Her voice didn't come back.

She stopped counting. She didn't need to count. She needed the shield up and the dagger low and her feet under her, and Gran had taught her that before Gran had taught her to talk properly, on the gate, in the dark: *watch the hands, not the face. The face is for liars.* So she didn't look at its face. She watched the hands on the staff, and when they went up to write again she went in under them, and put Gran's dagger through the coat where the cost book would have been.

It went in like a knife into a loaf from the oven. The thing looked down at the hilt, surprised, with Wren's face. Then it fell apart into embers, from the edges in, the way paper goes in a grate, and was a heap of red on the brass floor, going grey.

Her voice came back with a sob she hadn't known she was holding.

The floor turned. A brass wall slid aside, and for a moment another room lined up with hers through the gap, and Tobin was in it, with the axe hanging from his hand, not lifted.

In front of him stood Pip.

Small, in a watch-cap, with a shield on her arm, Gran's shield, burning at the rim. Her face cracked and glowing. Her mouth open in a grin Pip had seen in a hundred puddles and windows and the black glass of Wren's window every night, and out of it, in Pip's own voice, high and clear and carrying like a bell:

"She was five and you were *faster*."

Tobin just stood there and let it say it.

"That's not *me*," Pip screamed. "Tobin, that's not *me*—"

The thing with her face turned its head and looked at her through the gap, and smiled her smile.

Tobin lifted the axe and brought it down, once, and the thing with Pip's face came apart in embers, and he stood looking at the heap with his arms hanging. Then the wall slid shut.

It turned on. Through the next gap she saw Ash with his sword up in front of a big burning Tobin who was saying, slow and steady, the way Tobin said things, "You traded a man for your brother." Through the one after, Wren, standing very straight with the staff lowered, in front of a burning Ash with a bow, who was saying softly, "You'd write the whole Vale in its letters if it got your mother back," and Wren wasn't fighting it. She was listening.

They met at the stair. Nobody said what theirs had said. Nobody had to; Pip could see it on all of them, the way you can see who's been crying.

She went to Tobin.

"I never said that," she said. "I never said it. Not once."

He looked down at her.

"You thought it," he said.

Pip opened her mouth to lie, and couldn't. Not to him. "Yes."

"So did I." He put his hand on top of her head, the way he had when she was seven. "Every day. It's all right, Pip. It was true."

Wren was standing at the top of the stair with her face very white. Pip looked at her. Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Yours said I liked it," said Pip at last. "Saltwake. Gideon."

"Did you?"

"Yes," said Pip.

Wren nodded, slowly, as if a figure had come out right. "Ash's said I'd write the whole Vale in its letters to get my mother back."

"Would you?"

"I don't know," said Wren. "That's the trouble. I don't know any more."

### 6.12

*Floor 10: the still centre.*

The whole sky was turning, and in the middle of it, where nothing moved, Corwin Vane stood beside a laid place, and Wren understood that the chair was for her.

It was the dome again, the size of the sky: ring inside ring of iron walkway going out further than the lamp could follow, each turning at its own pace, and overhead a chart of stars, every star a point of fire, all wheeling. The Gleaners stood on the rings in their sheaf masks, fifty of them, carried round like figures on a town clock.

At the centre there was a disc of plain stone that didn't turn. On it stood Corwin Vane, in his dark coat, with his hands behind his back. And beside him, a small table laid for one. A white cloth. A chair. A plate with a slice of Hollis's dark feast loaf on it, and a cup.

"Sit down, Wren," said Vane. His voice came across the turning rings as if he were beside her. "You've walked a long way."

The rings moved. The one under her went left and the one under Tobin went right, and in four breaths there were three turning walkways between her and anyone, and the whistle went, three notes down, and Abner Stroud came across the rings to her with his sickle in his belt and his hands empty.

She wrote at him. He walked through it, caught the staff in one big carter's hand, put it down, and picked her up like a sack of meal and carried her across the turning iron, stepping over the gaps the way he'd stepped over the traces at the top of the tithe ramp for twenty years. She could hear Tobin shouting.

He set her down in the chair. He put his hands on her shoulders from behind, heavy, and held her there, facing the plate.

"Thank you, Abner," said Vane.

The bread smelled like the feast. It smelled like being eight, in a dress that was too tight, with the lanterns not lit yet. Stroud's hands stayed on her shoulders. She could feel them shaking.

Then they lifted.

She turned round in the chair. Stroud had stepped back. He reached up and took off his mask and dropped it on the stone, and under it was the broad weathered face with the grey moustache and the scar through the eyebrow, and he wasn't looking at her. He was looking at Vane.

"My boys can wake up hungry," said Abner Stroud. "They'll live."

He pulled the sickle out of his belt, turned, and put it into the nearest Gleaner on the ring behind him, and the next, and the third was turning to him with its plate up when Vane spoke.

One word. In the script. He didn't raise his voice. Wren heard it the way she heard the letters on the page, all at once, a shape more than a sound, and it went past her, not into her, the way a cart goes past on a road.

Stroud stopped with the sickle lifted.

The ember cracks on his neck and his hands went bright, then white, then brighter, the light coming up through them from inside the way the red came up through the wax door at Thistledown, until it was coming out of his eyes, his open mouth. He made no sound. He was dead before he hit the stone. Wren knew it before he landed; she saw it go out of him. What lay on the disc afterwards was very small, and grey, and didn't look like anybody at all.

No one would carry him home. She knew that too.

Wren stood up and took the table by its edge, and turned it over, plate and bread and cup and cloth, onto the stone, and the plate broke.

"That was unkind," said Vane, looking at it. "He chose."

"So will I," said Wren, and picked up her staff.

### 6.13

*The still centre. Then the dome.*

Wren called the rings, Pip turned them, Tobin held them, and Ash ran, and when he got to the middle his great-grandfather was waiting for him, as if he'd been the one invited all along.

It had cost. Edda's charts told them which rings turned next, and Wren read the wheeling stars against them and shouted the turns. Pip worked the star key at the outer rim with her whole weight. Tobin held the gap where two rings met against Gleaners with plates. Ash ran the rings as they lined up, gap to gap, and when a ring swung wrong he had to stand on it and be carried round past the Gleaners like a man on a millwheel. He'd been cut twice. He didn't feel it.

Vane watched him come the whole way.

When Ash stepped onto the stone disc at the centre, beside the overturned table and the small grey thing that had been Abner Stroud, the steward said one word to him in the script. Ash felt it. It came at him the way the bell had, through his teeth.

And went past him. Like wind past a post.

Vane looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in his face and went still.

"Of course," he said. "You never ate."

"Why her chair?" said Ash. His voice was cracking. He let it. "Why Wren? Why not all four?"

"Because she can read it," said Vane. "And because she would." He looked past Ash, across the rings, to where Wren stood calling the turns with her grey hand on the staff. "The rest of you are only owed. She's dangerous. You'll see."

Ash put his hand flat on Vane's chest, on the dark coat, where a heart should have been. There was nothing under it. Cold, like an empty house. He pushed.

Vane didn't move. It was like pushing a church.

"I've stood a long time, Ash," he said, quite kindly. "Sixty years, one way and another. You'll not move me on your own."

Then there was another hand beside Ash's on the coat, big and burned and bandaged. Tobin, bleeding from the scalp, breathing like a bellows, who had come across the rings behind him and left the gap to look after itself. Then Pip's hand, small, under Tobin's arm. Then, last, Wren's, the grey forefinger lying flat on the old man's coat beside the others.

Ash didn't look at any of them. He pushed, and they pushed with him, four of them, the way you'd push a cart that's stuck in a rut, and for a long moment nothing happened at all.

Then Corwin Vane's heel slid off the stone.

It was only a hand's width. But the Orrery stopped.

Every ring stopped at once, with a sound like every clock in the Vale striking together and then nothing. The Gleaners on the rings stood where they were. The iron didn't creak. And overhead the stars stopped wheeling, and hung, and as Ash looked up at them they ran together, the way ink runs on wet paper, point into point, line into line, and they weren't stars any more. They were letters.

The whole dome was written. From rim to rim, in fire, in the script, one long page across the sky.

And Ash could read it.

He'd never read a word of the script in his life. It was as plain as a sign over a shop. Tobin's lips were moving. Pip had her head right back. Vane had gone down onto his knees, slowly, like a much older man, and was looking up at it too.

Wren read it aloud anyway, from the top, in the voice she used at the wall, because somebody had to say it, and it was hers.

*Here is a bargain between the Host under the mountain and the people of the Vale, made by the hand of the one who signs.*

*The Host will make the fields rich.*

*The Vale will carry a tithe into the deep each autumn, and the tithe will grow with every feeding.*

*Whoever eats at the feast is the table, and is held from that meal until the next.*

*The tithe is weighed after the meal.*

*If the tithe falls short, the table is forfeit.*

*What is owed will be taken, and what is given will be had, and nothing else.*

*The one who signs will keep the table for the Host. His heart is held against it. When the last table is closed it will be given back to him, and he may sleep.*

*Whoever stands at the table unbound, awake before the Host, may speak against this writing, and what is spoken will end it.*

Wren stopped. She read the last line again, to herself, and Ash saw her hand go to her coat, where her mother's page was, with that same line running into the black.

"Unbound," said Pip.

"Nell ate," said Tobin, very low. "Everybody ate. Everybody we've carried up."

"They're awake," said Ash. "But they ate. They're still held." He heard himself say it and understood it as he said it. "Nobody's unbound. Nobody in the whole Vale."

"Except us," said Pip. "That's us. That's just us four."

Nobody answered her. Ash looked down at the man on his knees.

Corwin Vane looked smaller. He looked, for the first time, like a man who had been standing up for ten years and would like very much to sit. He said a word in the script, quietly, to the Gleaners on the stopped rings, and nothing happened, and he didn't seem surprised.

"*He may sleep*," said Ash. "That's what you're owed. That's all of it."

"That's all I asked for." Vane looked up at the line. "I'd buried my wife and my boy. I wanted my girl to live, and when it was done, to be let lie down. That's the whole of my villainy, Ash. It's very small written out." He looked at Ash properly. "Ash," he said, as if trying a word, and waited.

Ash didn't call him anything.

Not *great-grandfather*, not *Vane*, none of the things he'd tried in his head on the road up. He looked at him, and looked away, and that was all.

On the stopped rings the Gleaners laid down their plates, then their sickles, and walked off the iron into the dark, down, south and west, the way Lisle had gone. Nobody called them back.

"Get up," said Tobin to Vane. "You're coming with us."

Vane got up and let Tobin bind his hands with Pip's spare strap, and stood where they put him, off his stone, and over all their heads the bargain burned.

### 6.14

*The drovers' road, then Hollowmere. Ten days on.*

Hester Calder was waiting at the top of the lake road with Jory, and Wren saw her see who was walking behind the cart, and saw her know.

Ash had sent word ahead with Sorrel Kett's mules. Hester had never seen her grandfather; he'd gone down before she was born. She knew him anyway. He walked like Ash. He stood, when the cart stopped, the way Ash stood at the edge of things.

Vane looked at her. His bound hands moved a little, as if to reach for a blanket's edge and tuck it in.

Hester walked past him. She didn't look at him and she didn't say a word. She walked straight past her grandfather down the road to her son, and took Ash's face in both hands, and Wren looked away because it wasn't hers to watch.

They put Vane in the watch-house at the bottom of the gate lane, with Odile on a stool at the door and her old pike across her knees. Half of Hollowmere came to look at the martyr from the pageant through the bars, and he sat on the bench and let them. Bram Tolley looked longest and went away white. Nobody spat. Wren thought that was worse for him than spitting.

The Provost had left the pass charts on the dome step the morning they came up, without a word. They showed the road down off the mountain's shoulder into its lap, where Ash's maps had always been blank. To a village with a great hearth at the mouth of a cave.

On the fourth night, Wren painted out a name.

She used her own brush, the one she'd painted every tick with, and the white lime she mended the plaster with. *Abner Stroud*, on the lane list, in her own hand from ten years ago. She painted over it stroke by stroke until there was only a pale square where a name had been, and wrote nothing in it. It wasn't a tick. It was the first name on the wall that would never come home, and she did it with the grey hand.

When she turned round, Marta Fenn was standing in the open door.

Marta looked at the pale square for a long time, with her arms folded. Then at the column beside it, *At the table*, at two names near the bottom, *Davy Stroud, Col Stroud*, unticked. She nodded once, the way she nodded at a loaf that had come out right, and went back down the lane, and Wren heard the cleaver start in her kitchen, *chock*, *chock*, before she was halfway there.

The others came in after. Tobin trimmed the lamp too high. Pip sat on the hearth. Ash sat with his crooked nose and his warm hands and Tuppence a foot from his elbow, back turned, which was nearly a kindness. The cost book lay open on the table where Wren had left it, with her mother's page beside it, for anyone.

All but one thing. Wren kept that one in its own box, open, and was proud of it, and that was new.

"We take him down with us," said Ash. "To the Hearth. He knows the way."

"No," said Tobin.

"He's never lied to us once. That's the trouble with him."

Tobin looked at him a long time. "Then I'm coming to watch him."

"You were coming anyway."

"Somebody has to," said Tobin, and then, almost smiling, "Somebody. Not just me."

Pip laughed. It came out rusty and surprised, the first time since the table, and Wren heard it go round the room like something let out of a box.

Behind the church the mountain stood black against the stars. Somewhere in its lap was a village with a cold hearth, and under it a long table, and every child in the Vale asleep at it with sweets in front of them, and a lamp-keeper who was afraid of the dark. The road went there now. It was on the charts.

"Same time tomorrow," said Wren.

"Where else would we go," said Pip.
