# Chapter 7: Clearing the Table

*Status: draft, for user annotation.*

### 7.1

*The old oath road above Hollowmere, a night camp. High summer, PY 61.*

The road to the Hearth went up behind the church and over the first shoulder of the mountain, and nobody in Hollowmere had walked it in ten years, and the grass on it was waist-high and full of moths.

They camped at the lip of the mountain's lap with Hob's timber cart and one of Sorrel Kett's mules, lent at a price Sorrel said she'd think of later, which Pip said was the most frightening thing anybody had said to them all summer. Vane sat with his back against the cart's near wheel. Tobin had chained him to it with a length of Dun Harrow's trace chain, and padlocked it, and then tested the padlock four times, and then lain down on the far side of the fire with his good ear up and his eyes shut.

Ash fed the fire. He didn't need to. It was warm enough, and the stars were thick, and below them in the dark the lap of the mountain was a bowl of blackness with a faint red smudge at the bottom of it, like the last coal in a grate.

"You're not asleep," said Ash.

"I don't sleep." Vane's voice was the same as ever, dry and courteous, as if they were sitting in a parlour. "You've been walking up to a question since the church. Ask it. Tobin will want to hear the answer, and he can only hear on one side."

On the far side of the fire, Tobin didn't move.

Ash turned a stick in the coals. "Did you love them?"

"Whom?"

"Anyone. Ellen. Your boy. Bettany." He made himself go on. "Mam. Us."

Vane looked at the fire for a while.

"I loved my wife," he said. "I dug her grave myself. I loved my son, and he was in it with her. I loved Bettany enough to sign, and when she was grown and married and had a child, I loved her from under a mountain, which is a poor way to do it." He paused. "You I've met four times. Once by a pit, once in an orchard, once on a stone, and now on a road, chained to a wheel. I'd like to say I love you, Ash. I don't know any more what the word costs. I've not had the heart for it."

He said it without a smile, but it was a joke, and Ash understood that it was a joke, and laughed before he could stop himself, and hated himself for it.

"You read the clause," said Ash. "Every word. Before you signed."

"Every word."

"And you thought what? That we'd keep up?"

"I thought it was sixty harvests off," said Vane, "and I'd be dead." He looked at his chained hands. "I was right about one of those."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the slope an owl went over, and the mule stamped.

"It talked to me," said Ash. "Up on the scree. Four years, now."

"I know. It talked to me for sixty." Vane's eyes came up. "Did it ever lie to you?"

"No."

"No. It never does. That's the trouble with it. It tells you the true thing, and then it lets you do the sum." He leaned his head back against the cart's wheel. "You did a sum once. A lamp-keeper. You know everything about me there is to know, Ash. You've only paid once. I paid every autumn."

"Don't."

"I'm not comforting you. I've forgotten how." Vane closed his eyes, which Ash had never seen him do. It changed his face. It made it older. "I'm telling you what it does. It finds the one you'd pay anything for, and then it asks for something small."

Ash put the stick on the fire.

On the far side of it, Tobin said, without opening his eyes, "We should leave him down there."

"Tobin is right," said Vane.

Nobody said anything else. Ash sat up with the fire until the sky went grey.

In the morning they came over the lip of the lap and saw the Hearth.

It was doorsteps, like everywhere: thirty or so, set in a rough ring on a shelf of grey rock, round a black scar of old fire twenty paces across. Six roads came into it, from six ways round the mountain, and every one of them was worn down a foot into the rock, the way a step is worn in a church, by feet that had come the same way for years. Behind the black scar, in the face of the mountain, was a cave mouth, tall as the church and wider. The air coming out of it moved. In, slow. Out, slow. Warm.

"Every oath in the Vale ended here," said Wren. She stood with the staff and looked at the six roads. "Every elder walked down one of those and swore."

"I swore the first," said Vane, behind them, at the tailboard. "On that stone. The rest swore to me." He was looking at the cave. "I'll tell you something true, since it's the last place to tell it. You're going down there to fetch every child in the Vale. When the last one leaves that table, it'll wake. It'll wake starving, with sixty years of hunger and nothing in its belly, and there'll be nothing between it and the Vale but the four of you." He turned his head. "That isn't a threat. It's what's written. It's no help to you at all, I know."

"Then why say it?" said Pip.

"Because you asked me to guide you, and that's the way." Vane looked at Ash. "I'd like one thing. I've no right to it. I'd like to be there when it ends."

"No," said Tobin.

Ash didn't say anything. He was looking at the cave. The air came out of it and touched his face, warm, and for a moment, under it, he could almost hear somebody at the head of a long table, clearing their throat.

### 7.2

*The Hearthdeep, floors 1 and 2: the kitchen door and the brine larder.*

The first floor was the Hearth itself, folded into the mountain like a cloth into a drawer, and the fire burned downward.

Vane had told them the floors at the cave mouth, all seven, in order, without being asked, and every one of them had been true. Then Tobin had chained him to the wheel of Hob's cart on the black hearth-stone and left him there in the sun. "You're not going down where the children are," he'd said, and Vane had said, "No. That's fair," and that was worse than an argument.

Tobin had seen a lot of wrong things in a year. He'd seen a bell that tolled names, and a bakery underwater, and a honey queen who never ate. He stood at the top of the first stair with the axe on his shoulder and watched the great hearth of the Hearth village hanging upside down from the roof of the cave, its stones in a ring overhead, and the fire in it pouring down out of the ring toward the floor like water out of a tipped bucket, and he felt nothing at all except tired.

"There's a draught," said Wren. "It's drawing down. Like the flues."

"Dampers," said Pip.

"There aren't any dampers."

There weren't. There were houses. The Hearth's houses had been taken apart and hung on the walls of the cave in their pieces, doors and shutters and chimneys, and the fire went where the doors let it. Open a door, and the draught went that way, and the fire bent after it. It was the Undercroft's bellows floors all over again, only nobody had given them bellows. Wren called the doors and Tobin opened them, and they crossed the floor in the fire's shadow, one door at a time.

The Gleaners stood along the walls like waiters at a wedding, and didn't come. "Nobody's telling them to," said Ash. Every time the four went by, they lifted their plates an inch, as if to say, *if you change your mind*. The nightmares fought: ladles on legs, a cauldron full of teeth, iron fire-dogs that ran on four legs and were dogs.

Ned Tallow was asleep under his own cart, at the bottom of the floor, with the shaft across his chest and a pair of reins wound round his fist. He was a broad dull man of forty with a face like a well-meaning potato, and when he woke he said, "Is it time to go up?" and then, "Who are you?" and then, worried, "Am I late?"

"Ten years," said Pip.

He thought about that. "That's late," he said. "I want my shed. And my wagon. And the road open, so I can get up and down." He looked round at the hanging village, at the fire pouring down. "I carried the share up to the hall every autumn," he said, to nobody. "Off the top. I never asked what it was for. You don't, do you. You just drive."

The warm took him, cart and all.

The second floor was a larder, and it was flooding.

It came in and went out. Tobin felt it before he saw it, the floor tilting under his boots the way Kip's barge tilted, and then the brine came in round his ankles from somewhere below, cold and green and smelling of the Saltwake slip, and went out again, and came in higher. The shelves of the larder went up into the dark, and on them were barrels, Hollowmere barrels with Hollowmere marks, rusted through and bulging, and in the barrels were things that had been pickled and weren't food: shoes, mostly, and hats, and once a child's wooden horse.

"It's a tide," said Pip. "It's Saltwake's tide."

"How long?"

She watched the brine come in and go out twice, and counted under her breath, the way Teague had taught her on the quay with his hat over his heart. "Long enough," she said. "Not very."

It was Ash who saw the light.

High up at the back of the larder, above the brine, on a shelf among the barrels, a lamp was burning. A little brass lamp on a pole. Then another, further along, and another, a line of them going back into the dark along the top shelf, seventeen lamps, small and yellow and brave as anything, and at the end of them, curled on the shelf with his knees up and his hands over his head, a small round man.

Ash went.

He didn't wait for the tide. He went up the shelves with the brine coming in under him, barrel to barrel, his hands slipping on the rust, and Tobin, standing knee-deep, watched him go and thought of a timber yard, and a kitchen table with blood on it, and his own voice through a broken nose that wasn't his: *you go and get him first.*

He had. First. Before the carter, before anything. He'd made Wren draw the map so this floor came first.

Tobin didn't say anything. He stood in the brine and held the stair for them, and let the tide come.

Ash reached the end of the shelf. Wynn Cobb lay among his lamps with his eyes open. He'd been awake, Tobin thought. Some of the time. He had the look. His face was turned up to the nearest lamp like a plant to a window.

"They keep going out," said Wynn. His voice was very small. "I light them and they go out. It's dark between. It's so dark between."

"I know," said Ash.

Wynn's eyes moved. He looked at Ash for a long time. Tobin, below, couldn't see Ash's face. He could see his back.

"I heard my name," said Wynn. "Up the hill. In the night. Somebody said it. Like being called in for supper." He swallowed. "Was that you?"

"Yes," said Ash.

The tide came in round Tobin's thighs. Wynn Cobb lay among his lamps and looked at the boy who had given him away, and didn't say anything for so long that the tide went out again.

"Lamps," said Wynn at last. "That's what I want. Any lamps. Just light them." He shut his eyes. "Just light them. Before it's dark. Not after."

The warm came for him, and took him up off the shelf with his knees still drawn up, and every one of the seventeen lamps went with him, one by one along the top shelf, like somebody walking up a lane.

Ash came down the barrels slowly. At the bottom he stood in the brine beside Tobin, and didn't say anything, and Tobin didn't either.

Then Tobin put his hand on the back of Ash's neck, once, the way you'd steady a horse, and took it away, and they went down.

### 7.3

*The Hearth, ten days on. Then floors 3 and 4: the gut-seam and the comb.*

The Hearth came up ugly, and Pip liked it better than anywhere.

It wasn't pretty and it didn't try. The houses went back on their doorsteps square and grey and thick-walled, with turf on the roofs. Ned Tallow drove anything anybody asked him to, pieces from the bank, stone for the hall, and never asked why. Wat Hesketh had the drovers' yard full of oxen in a week, great burnt-red beasts that had come out of the deep with him blinking; he kicked them and swore at them and fed them before he fed himself, and was gentle as a nurse to people, and Pip couldn't work him out and liked him for it. There was a hall, and a ring of houses, and the black scar of the hearth in the middle, cold. It felt like a camp. It felt like the night before a thing.

The third floor had been carts.

Pip had gone down it with Dagny's whistle on its cord round her neck. The gut-seam was the Glow Seams turned round: the same rails and switch-points, but the pressure ran out of the rock instead of into it, so the carts came up at you red-hot with smoking tithe sacks, and the bronze pipes the miners had driven in to steal its heat ran backward, breathing out. They'd lost a lot of skin.

At the bottom, Wat Hesketh had been asleep in the last cart, on a heap of burning sacks that didn't burn him, and the cart was coming up the rails at them with a sound like the end of the world. Pip had put the whistle in her mouth and blown three short. End of shift.

She hadn't known it would work. It did. The whole seam stopped, every cart on every rail, the way Ebben Ferrule's engine had stopped, and the pressure sighed down in all the bronze pipes like a man sitting down after work.

"Huh," Pip had said, round the whistle, in a voice that was nearly her own.

The fourth floor was the comb, and it was singing.

It was the Hive Choir's wax, gold and warm, going up in cells and arches, and the hymn was in the walls. Not sung: in them. *Who eats at the table is held in the hand.* Thistledown's setting, the one Bram had made them all learn. Every cell hummed a note of it, and wherever the hymn was loudest, the wax set, and wherever it went quiet, the wax ran. Pip stood in it up to her shins in a passage that was closing like a fist, and Wren behind her raised the staff and Pip said, "*Don't*," sharper than she meant.

Wren lowered the staff.

Pip had no smoker. Nobody had a smoker; there were no tools down here, Vane had said so on the step, and Vane never lied. She stood in the closing wax with the hymn humming in the walls all round her, and for no reason she could have given, she opened her mouth and sang over it.

Not the hymn. Not anything holy. *The Ferryman's Wife*, which Kip Rudd had sung on the barge across the lake every night of the winter, all eleven verses, all of them about what the ferryman's wife did when the ferryman was on the far bank. She sang it loud and flat and badly, in her cracked rusty voice, the voice that had come back as a hinge-squeak in Wren's kitchen and hadn't been right since, and the wax in front of her shivered and ran.

"*Pip*," said Wren.

"It's a song," said Pip, between verses. "You sing over a song. Gran says it's how you clear a drunk out of a tavern."

Behind her she heard Ash laugh, properly, for the first time since the brine. Then Tobin joined in, who couldn't sing at all and knew only the chorus, and then Ash, and then, gods help them all, Wren, who knew every verse, it turned out, and had never let on. They went down through the singing comb four abreast, bawling about the ferryman's wife, and the hymn in the walls couldn't hold a note against them, and the wax went soft and ran away from their boots like water off a duck.

Old Bryn was at the bottom, asleep inside a charcoal clamp made of wax: a great beehive mound of comb, smoking gently from its vents. When Tobin broke it open he sat up in a cloud of honey-smoke, eighty if a day, black to the elbows, and looked at the four of them with total contempt.

"Who's been singing that?" he said. "That's filth, that is."

"Sorry," said Pip.

"Don't be. I've heard worse at a keepers' meeting." He got up, creaking. "Bryn. Charcoal. I want my glade, and my clamp smoking, and *nobody tidying it*. Gerda's lot tidied it every spring. Put it in rows." He spat. "You can't make charcoal in rows."

The warm took him while he was still spitting.

That night in the Hearth, Pip found him in his glade on the slope above the houses, already building a clamp, turf on top of wood on top of turf, in no kind of row at all.

"You were the one," she said. "Who voted against. Ned told us. The keepers' share. Every year."

"Every year." Bryn didn't stop working. "Fifty years. One hand up against, mine, and thirty-odd for. Every spring." He thumped a turf down. "And you know what it got me? Same as them. Ten years in a heap of wax with the rest of them, listening to that hymn." He looked at her sideways. "Being right's not a roof, girl. Nobody tells you that. I was right fifty years running, and I ate the bread with the rest of them, because it was there, and I was hungry. Write that on a wall."

Pip thought about Gran on the gate, with her back turned at exactly the right moment, and five children going past.

"I don't write things on walls," she said. "That's Wren."

"Then tell her," said Bryn, and lit his clamp.

### 7.4

*Floor 5: the turning room and the Keepers' Wagon. Then the bank.*

There was no key on the fifth floor, and Wren had known there wouldn't be, and still looked for one.

It was the Orrery's dome, smaller, and hotter, and made of the wrong things. The rings that turned in it weren't iron walkways. They were roads: six rings of worn rock, each one rutted a foot deep, going round at six speeds about a hub of black fire. And on the outermost ring, going round and round with a sound like every autumn of her childhood at once, was the Wagon.

It had been carts. You could still see that, if you looked, and Wren looked, because she couldn't not. Every tithe cart that had gone down every ramp in the Vale in sixty years, Saltwake's and Cinderholt's and Thistledown's and theirs, fused at the axles and the shafts into one long thing that bent like a caterpillar, a hundred wheels turning, the sacks on its back smoking. It was drawn by oxen that weren't oxen any more: teams of ember in the shapes of oxen, burning as they went, their horns two curls of flame. On the high seat at the front sat nothing. On the sacks behind it, far up on its back, lay an old woman in a keeper's grey, asleep, with her hands folded on her chest like a carving on a tomb.

"Gerda," said Ash. "The Keeper."

"How do we stop the rings?" said Pip.

Nobody answered. There was no key. Wren stood at the edge of the outer road with the Wagon coming round toward them, and her hand was on the staff, and she could have written. She could feel exactly what it would take: three letters, maybe four, a word for *stop* that wasn't *stop*, the size of a cart. She could feel what it would cost, too. The grey was at her second knuckle. The next word would have the finger.

She took her hand off the staff.

"We push," she said.

Tobin looked at her.

"The way we did on the stone," said Wren. "It's only a road. Roads don't move on their own."

They pushed. They put their shoulders to the inner edge of the second ring, the four of them, where the rock was carved like a cartwheel's rim, and pushed against its turning, and for a long moment nothing happened, the way nothing had happened under Vane's coat. Then it went. It ground, and slowed, and stopped, and the third ring went on round and lined up with it, and they went across and pushed again. The Wagon came round on the outer road behind them, and the burning teams turned their heads to them as they passed, and the Gleaners rode the Wagon's back with their plates held out.

Pip stopped the teams. She stood on the stopped fourth ring with Dagny's whistle and blew end of shift, three short, and the burning oxen stopped dead in their traces, and the hundred carts behind them didn't. They came on, folding up against the stopped teams like a concertina, axle into axle, the sacks bursting and spilling burning grain, and the whole long thing reared up off the road and came down across the rings.

Tobin cut the traces. All of them, one after another, running along the fallen spine of the thing with the axe. Ash shot the Gleaners off its back. And Wren climbed up the side of the Wagon by its wheels, cart to cart, sixty years of autumns under her hands, to where the old woman lay on the sacks.

Gerda Brand woke when Wren touched her, and looked up at her with fierce pale eyes in a lined, handsome face.

"Is it the meeting?" she said. "Have they come up the roads?"

"No," said Wren. "It's ten years on."

The Keeper sat up on the burning sacks. She looked round at the broken Wagon, the stopped rings, the four of them. She looked for a long time at Ash.

"You've the Elder's look," she said.

"So I'm told."

"Keeper Gerda Brand." She got down off the Wagon the way a woman gets down off a cart she's driven for forty years, without help, and stood on the road. "I want the hearth lit. With honest wood. Not this." She looked at the black fire on the hub. "Ours was always paid. Every autumn. Our share was always on time."

"Your share," said Ash, "was off the top of everyone else's."

Gerda looked at him. She didn't answer that. Wren had seen the abbess not answer things, and the Provost; she knew the shape of it now.

"Keeper," said Wren. "The elder's oath. You swore every elder in. Say it for us."

"Word for word?"

"Word for word."

Gerda Brand put her hand flat on her chest, where you'd put it on a stone, and said it, in a carrying voice made for six roads:

"*I take the Vale's bread, and I carry the Vale's tithe. What is owed I will see paid. What is written at the foot I will copy as it stands, and I will not ask. What I am told at this fire of the table's forfeit I will tell to no one but the next who swears, for the table's sake. On this stone. By this fire.*"

There was a silence on the stopped rings.

"You were told," said Wren. "At the fire. All of you. Every elder." It came out quite level. "*If the tithe falls short, the table is forfeit.* You told each other, one at a time, for sixty years, and nobody else."

"For the table's sake," said Gerda Brand. "Yes." And the warm took her, standing straight, with her hand still on her chest.

The bank was a scullery, low and warm, with a stone sink and a pump that worked and a fire in a range, and on the long wall a dresser.

It went up into the dark. Shelf on shelf of plates, all sizes, Hollowmere's brown ware and Saltwake's blue and Stillhollow's plain white, hundreds of them, standing on their edges in the racks, face out. As Wren watched, one near the bottom turned itself face down on its shelf with a small click. Gerda.

The top shelf was all small plates. Children's plates, the kind with a rim to stop the peas going over. Wren counted them, because it was what she was for.

Ninety-three. Every one face out.

"That's the table," said Tobin.

"That's the table."

They went home from the bank. Nobody said they should push on, and nobody said they shouldn't; they stood in the scullery looking at the top shelf, and then Pip said, "I want to see Gran," in her rusty voice, and that was the decision.

### 7.5

*Hollowmere. The last night before the last dive.*

Wynn Cobb lit the lamp lane an hour early, and Tobin stood at the bottom of it with Ash and watched him do it.

He did it the way he always had: the pole, the little brass can, a word to every lamp. But he did it in full daylight, with the sun still on the roofs, and he didn't look down the lane at Ash once, all the way up. Seventeen lamps. At the top he stood under the last one and looked at the light he'd made, which you could hardly see against the sky, and his shoulders didn't come down at all.

Wren had painted the tick back by his name that morning. She'd done it slowly, over the pale scar where she'd scraped the first one off.

"He won't look at me," said Ash.

"No," said Tobin.

Hester Calder came down the lane from her end of it with a basket on her arm. She didn't say anything either. She walked up to her son, and took his arm, and turned him round, and walked him up the lamp lane in the early light past every one of Wynn's lamps, at a walking pace, the way the dark had gone up it once, all the way to the top. Wynn watched them come from his doorstep. Hester nodded to him. He shut his door.

Tobin went home.

Nell was on his step with the cat. She sat on it every evening, in the shawl that went round her three times, with Tuppence across her knees like a loaf, and watched the lane, and the lane was mostly him. She was five. She'd been five for eleven years.

He sat down beside her. Tuppence opened one eye and closed it.

"I didn't come back for you," said Tobin.

Nell didn't say anything. She was stroking the cat's one good ear, backward, the way he hated.

"At the feast. I told you I came back. I didn't. Da gave me the axe and told me to take you, and you were holding my sleeve, and I pulled it out of your hand and ran. Because you were slow. That's all it was." He looked at the lane. "I've told everybody now. I wanted you to have it too. It's yours more than anyone's."

Nell thought about this for a long time, the way she thought about everything.

"I know," she said. "I was there."

He laughed. He didn't mean to. It came up out of him like a cough, and he put his burned hands over his face, and laughed into them until it wasn't laughing, and Nell sat beside him and stroked the cat the wrong way and let him.

"You can carry me," she said at last. "To the pump. Tomorrow."

"I'm going down tomorrow."

"When you come back, then." She looked at him sideways, her weather look. "You have to come back. Or you've told two."

From the timber yard, across the lane, there was the sound of a bottle being put down on a board. Tobin looked up. His father was standing in the dark of the yard gate, where he must have been standing a while.

"I gave a boy of ten an axe and a girl of five," said Hob Marsh, "and told him to run." His voice was thick, but not with drink, or not only. "I knew which he'd keep. I knew when I gave it him. I'm not a fool, Tobin. I let you tell me she wandered because it was easier for me than for you." He looked at the step, at the two of them. "That's mine. Not yours. I'll carry it."

He went back into the yard. Tobin heard him not pick up the bottle.

### 7.6

*The watch-house at the bottom of the gate lane. The same night.*

The watch-house was empty, and Gran was sitting outside it anyway.

She had her chair out by the door, the one from her own step, and her old watch pike across her knees, and she was looking up the gate lane toward the square, where the feast gate stood, two posts and an arch and nothing hanging on it. The cell behind her had a bench and a bucket and nobody. Vane was up in the Hearth, chained to the wheel of Hob's cart, not sleeping, with Ned Tallow sitting up beside him because Tobin had asked and Ned did what he was asked.

"You've no prisoner," said Pip.

"I've a door." Odile Ostrander didn't look round. "Somebody should sit by it."

Pip sat on the step by the chair. Her voice still wasn't right. It came and went, rusty, like a gate in the rain. Gran had never once asked about it. She'd looked at Pip's throat the first night, and at Wren's hand, and done the sum, and said nothing, which from Gran was a whole speech.

"There's ninety-three," said Pip. "At the table. Wren counted the plates."

"Twenty-three of ours."

"Twenty-three."

Gran was quiet a while. Down the lane the lamps were lit, Wynn's, a whole hour early, and somewhere the bell would go soon, Amos ringing dusk late and loud.

"I could count to twenty-three on that gate with my eyes shut," said Gran. "Every one of them. I knew whose they were and how fast they ran and which ones would cry. Tilly Rowe. The Pell twins, who I'd have taken both of if I could have got them to stop fighting." She turned the pike over on her knees. "I took you."

"Why?" said Pip. "Of all of them. I was seven and I talked all the time. Tilly was quicker."

"Because you were mine," said Odile. "That's not a reason. It's the only one I had." She looked at Pip for the first time, the gate look. "You'll want a better one. There isn't one. That's what being on the gate is. You let past who you let past and you know it every night."

Pip didn't say anything. She'd stopped needing to fill a silence, some time in the spring. She didn't know when.

The bell went. Late and too loud, as it always was now, and all down the gate lane people stopped in their doors to let it finish. When it was done Gran stood up, and put out her hand, and tapped Pip once on the shoulder.

Tap.

Pip knew it. Everyone in Hollowmere over the age of five knew it. It was how Gran counted the grown-ups in at the gate on feast day. Children ran in and out like hens. Grown-ups were touched on the shoulder and counted.

"One," said Gran, and went inside.

### 7.7

*Floor 6: the pantry of names.*

The tallies hung from the vault like hams, and they were burning, and Ash's name was on one of them.

The sixth floor was a pantry, the biggest Ash had ever seen: a long stone vault going down a slope into steam, with hooks in the roof, and on every hook a tally page. Not a page. Sixty years of pages, each town's, sewn into long hanging sides and cured black, swinging a little in the heat. They were all burning slowly from the bottom up, the way a wick burns, and as each line went the name on it went up in a little curl of ember, and the ash came down like snow.

The water was rising underneath. It came up the slope out of the steam, hot, the Undercroft's cistern water and the Undercroft's oven heat together, so the bottom of the vault was a kettle and the top was a chimney, and the only way down was between.

Ash found Hollowmere's side halfway down, by the stamped mark on its hook. He couldn't help it. He stood under it in the steam and found the children's block, and the four plain names in it that had never been sealed. *Tobin Marsh. Wren Hale. Pip Ostrander. Ash Calder.* Brown ink, cold, while every name round them burned.

"Ash," said Wren, below him. "The water."

"I know."

He went on.

Linnet Brand was asleep at a kitchen table at the bottom of the slope, just above the water, in an apron, with her head on her folded arms and a keepers' loaf in front of her, cut. She was thirty or so and very thin, and when Tobin woke her she looked at the loaf before she looked at any of them.

"Is that mine?" she said. "Did I bake that?" Then, sharp: "Where's Hal?"

"At the table," said Wren.

Linnet didn't cry. She looked at the loaf, the keepers' bread, made from the share off the top of the Vale's plate. "I made it every year," she said. "Gerda's bread. I knew what was in it. I'm the cook; you always know what's in it." She stood up. "I want the hall kitchen. A proper oven. Not that fire." And then, to Wren, very low: "And bring my boy. He'll be hungry. He's always hungry."

The warm took her, and the water came up over the table where she'd been.

At the very bottom of the pantry, where the slope went into the dark, there was a door. A plain kitchen door, painted cream, with a brass handle, and in front of it stood Cantor Lisle with a white cloth over his arm.

He had no mask now. He wore a waiter's long apron over his choir white, and he stood the way the waiters had stood at the feast, very straight, a little bent at the neck, attentive. His kind face was thinner. The ember cracks had come up his throat to his jaw.

"Good evening," he said. "You've come a long way. Will you sit?"

"No," said Pip.

"There's room." Lisle said it as if it were the best news in the world. "There's always room. Every child in the Vale is through this door, and they're warm, and they're fed, and they're happy, and there are four chairs. I've laid them myself." He looked at each of them. "I'll ask you once more, and then I'll stop asking. It's not a trick. It's the only true thing."

"We know," said Ash. "That's why not."

Lisle looked at him a long time, and nodded, as if Ash had said something he'd been waiting to hear.

Then he lifted the cloth off his arm, and under it was a tray, and on the tray was a course: a covered dish, silver, steaming. He took the cover off. Out of it came the Undercroft's rat-king, and the Weigh-Crab, and a slag-hound, and three comb-drones, all at once, all small and getting bigger, the leftovers of every town they'd been to, served.

They fought the course. It was long, and it was bad, in the steam with the water coming up and the burning names coming down on their shoulders like snow. When it was done, the tray was on the floor in two pieces where Tobin had put the axe through it, and Lisle stood in front of the cream door with his empty hands folded.

"Thank you," he said, again. The same as on the choir loft. "For trying."

He opened the door. Warm yellow light came through it, and the smell of honey cake, and the sound of a great many children breathing in their sleep.

Lisle stepped aside to let them pass. Then he didn't wait for them. He went through ahead of them, and down, past whatever was beyond it, singing the blessing under his breath, and kept on going, until the song went somewhere they couldn't follow, and stopped.

### 7.8

*Floor 7: the table.*

There was no boss. There was a table, and Wren had painted every name at it on her wall.

It was a long hall, warm as a kitchen in winter, and lit gold from no lamp anyone could see. Down the middle of it ran a table, longer than the square, longer than anything, under a white cloth. And at it, on benches, in their feast clothes, sat the children.

Every one was asleep. Every one was smiling. In front of each of them was a plate with sweets on it, honey cake and sugared plums and the little gingerbread men Hollis had made for the feast, and every few breaths a child's hand would move on the cloth in its sleep, and close on something sweet, and bring it to its mouth, and the smile would deepen.

Tilly Rowe was the sixth from the door. Wren knew her from the back. The plaits.

"*Welcome.*"

The voice came from everywhere: from the walls, the table, the warm air. Not loud. Pleased. A host who had heard the door and come out wiping his hands.

"*Do come in. Sit down, there's room. There's always room.*"

Tobin looked at Ash. Ash's face had gone very still.

"That's it," said Ash. "That's the voice."

"*Hello, Ash,*" said the voice, fondly. "*You've grown.*"

They didn't answer it. Wren went to Tilly Rowe and stood behind her, and waited for the warm to come up out of the floor and breathe her home, the way it had for every sleeper since the cellar under the church.

It didn't come.

She waited. Nothing. The children breathed and smiled and ate in their sleep. Wren understood it all at once, the way she understood a column: you didn't send the best course back to the kitchen. Nobody had been released from this table because nobody was ever going to be. If they wanted them, they would have to carry them.

"We carry them," she said. "Up the hall. One each."

"To where?" said Pip.

At the top of the hall, back by the door they'd come through, there were carts. Wren hadn't seen them coming in. A row of tithe carts standing empty along the wall, the way they'd stood at the top of every ramp in the Vale on the morning after the weighing, waiting to go back up.

Wren lifted Tilly Rowe off the bench. She was heavier than eight. She was warm, and sticky with honey, and her head fell on Wren's shoulder, and in her sleep she said, quite clearly, "*Not yet*," and reached back toward the plate.

Wren carried her up the hall and laid her in the first cart.

When she straightened, the hall was a little colder. Not much. As if somebody had opened a door, somewhere, a long way off.

"*Oh,*" said the voice, mildly. As if it had noticed a smudge on a glass.

They carried. There was nothing else to it. Down the hall, lift a child, back up the hall, lay it in a cart, down again. Tobin took two at a time when they were small. The Pell twins came apart from each other only in the carrying; they'd been holding hands under the table. The Sedge boy who'd played Elder Vane in the pageant still had a bit of wool beard stuck to his chin with honey. Ash carried a boy of seven with Linnet Brand's chin, Hal, who woke halfway up the hall and said, "Is it pudding?" and went back to sleep. Pip carried Dickon Lowe, Hep's grandson, with a bee embroidered on his collar, who held on to a sugared plum all the way up the hall and wouldn't give it to the cart.

Every child they carried, the hall went colder. The gold light thinned. The sweets on the plates began to look like what they were. The Gleaners came out of the walls, as the stir rose, and stood along both sides of the hall in their sheaf masks with their plates, and watched. Some of them followed. Wren saw one, a woman by the shape of her, walk up the hall beside Ash all the way to the carts with her scorched hands out, not touching, and stand by the cart he laid a Saltwake boy in, and stay there.

On the fortieth child or so, Pip stopped halfway up the hall with a girl from Thistledown in her arms, and said it.

"They're happy." Her voice cracked on it. "Look at them. They're the only people in the Vale who are. We're taking them out of the only good dream anybody's had in sixty years, so they can wake up hungry."

Nobody answered her. Tobin went past her with two Stillhollow boys, one on each arm, and didn't look at her. Ash went by. Wren went by with a Cinderholt girl in a miner's cap too big for her and didn't say anything, because Pip was right, and they all knew she was right, and saying so wouldn't put the child down.

Pip stood there a moment longer. Then she hitched the girl higher on her hip and kept walking.

"*It was a good table,*" said the voice. Not to them. To itself, a little sadly. "*It was the best table I've ever laid.*"

Ninety. Ninety-one. The hall was cold now, cold as the Orrery, their breath going up white. The plates in front of the empty places were bare wood. At the far end, the last two children sat side by side on the bench, close together, two small dark heads, the same head twice: the two boys who had kicked their heels on the lead ox's back every autumn while their father walked beside it whistling.

*Davy Stroud. Col Stroud.* Unticked, near the bottom of the column, in Wren's hand.

Pip took Davy. She didn't ask. She went down the cold hall and lifted him off the bench and carried him up past all of them, and her face was wet, and she didn't stop.

That left Col.

Tobin went down for him alone.

### 7.9

*The table. Then the collapse, and the Hearth at noon.*

Col Stroud was the only one left at the table, and the table was a plank on trestles.

That was all it was, now, with the gold gone out of the hall: rough boards, a stained cloth, benches, ninety-two bare plates and one with a gingerbread man on it, half eaten. The boy sat in front of it with his hands in his lap. He was small and dark and he had his father's eyebrows, both of them whole, without the scar. He wasn't smiling any more. In his sleep he was frowning, the way Nell frowned when the lamp was put out.

Tobin's breath went up white. His left ear rang, the way it had since the bell, and under the ringing he could hear his own heart going, slow, and nothing else in the whole cold hall.

He lifted Col off the bench. The boy weighed nothing. In his sleep he said, "Da?"

"No," said Tobin. "Sorry. Only me."

He carried him up the hall. It was a long way, and the Gleaners along the walls turned their straw faces after him one by one as he passed. He didn't hurry. He thought about a feast square, and a sleeve pulled out of a small hand, and how fast he'd been. He wasn't fast now. He was the slowest thing in the hall.

At the top Wren and Pip and Ash were standing by the carts, ninety-two children asleep in them in rows like loaves. There was one place left, in the last cart. Tobin laid Col Stroud in it, beside his brother, and Davy's hand moved in his sleep and found Col's, and held on.

The hall went dark.

Not dim. Dark, all of it at once, the way the lanterns had gone out over the square on the Long Night, so that Tobin could see nothing at all, not his own hands on the cart's side, not the boy. And in the dark, from everywhere, very close and very quiet, the voice:

"*Oh.*"

It wasn't angry. It sounded like somebody who has come down in the night to the larder and opened the door.

"*Oh,*" said Mawrrow. "*You've cleared the table.*"

Then it woke.

The floor didn't shake; it lifted. The whole hall rose under them like a held breath, and the long table at the bottom went end over end into the dark. Something enormous, under everything, under the mountain, drew in a breath that went on and on and on, and Tobin understood with his whole body that it had been sixty years since it had eaten, and that it was starving, and that it knew exactly where the food was.

"*Carts*," Ash was shouting. "Get on the—"

The tithe road ran down. It had always run down; every ramp in the Vale ran down to here. Now the floor tipped, and kept tipping, and the road ran up, and the carts went up it. Backward. Tobin got onto the last one with the Stroud boys and held on, and they went up the tithe road the wrong way the way the Curve had gone back into its coils when Wren turned the key, scale by scale, year by year, the whole Hearthdeep unwinding upward under them and folding shut behind.

They went up through the pantry of names, every tally bursting into flame. Through the scullery, where the great dresser came down in a roar of breaking plates, every small one on the top shelf face down. Through the turning room, the rings breaking up like ice on a river; the comb, running like candles; the gut-seam, every stolen pipe screaming as it breathed in; the brine; the kitchen door, the great hearth hanging dead from the roof. Then up the throat itself, the cave mouth a white slot above them getting bigger, and Tobin heard Pip laughing, high and mad, and realised it was him.

They came out of the mouth of the Hearthdeep into noon.

The carts shot out across the black hearth-scar and fetched up against the doorsteps of the Hearth's grey houses. The sky was blue from rim to rim, high summer, and the light was so bright after the dark that Tobin sat with his hands over his eyes.

In the carts behind him, the children were waking. Ninety-three of them, one and then dozens, and it wasn't laughing, and it wasn't crying, not yet. It was one word, over and over, in every voice from every town in the Vale, the way you'd say it waking from a long sleep on a feast night.

"*Hungry.*"

Tobin took his hands off his eyes. Behind the Hearth, the mountain was opening.

### 7.10

*The oath road down to Hollowmere. The fight in the open.*

Wren saw it come up out of the mountain from the back of a tithe cart, with Tilly Rowe on her lap asking for bread, and later the thing she would remember most clearly was that it was beautiful.

It came up the way a fire comes up through a stack of wood: slowly at first, then all at once. The mountain behind the Hearth didn't break so much as open, the cave mouth stretching, the rock folding back from it like the petals of something, and out of it, into the blue, rose Mawrrow.

It was the colour of harvest. Ember and dark gold, like a field of wheat at sunset with the light going through it, and it went up and up into the clear sky until its head was above the peaks, and it didn't have a head. It had mouths. Where a face should have been there were mouths, many, opening and closing gently, the way a sleeper's mouth moves, and its arms came out of the mountain after it, long and gold and burning at the joints, and its hands were the size of the square.

It wasn't in a hurry. It looked down at the Vale, at the six towns round the mountain on their one road, the way a man looks down at a table that has been laid for him.

"*Oh, look,*" it said. Not loud. Wren heard it in her teeth. "*You've rebuilt everything.*"

"Drive," said Vane.

He was on the tailboard of Hob's cart ahead of hers, chained to it, where Tobin had put him without a word when the carts came out. He had his head back, looking up at the thing he'd made a bargain with sixty years ago, and his face was quite calm.

"The church," he said. "Isolde's ward. It held on the Long Night, and it'll hold now. It's the only roof in the Vale it can't put its hand through." He looked at the children in the carts. "They're owed. They're all owed. They ate. So is every soul in every town. Only you four aren't. Drive."

They drove. Wat Hesketh's burnt-red oxen went down the old oath road at a pace no ox should go, Wat running beside the lead pair kicking them and weeping, and Ned Tallow on the first cart's seat with the reins round his fist, and Pip already gone, out ahead, running, because the bell in Hollowmere's tower would have to be rung and Amos Gill couldn't hear anybody tell him.

Behind them, on the shelf of the Hearth, Gerda Brand lit the hearth.

Wren looked back and saw it go up: Bryn's charcoal and split oak, honest wood, roaring into a pillar of clean yellow fire. Out of the carters' sheds came every old cart the Hearth had, loaded with fire from it, and Linnet and Bryn and Gerda herself ran them by the shafts to the edge of the ledge and tipped them off, into the mouths, as it rose, the last tithe, the only one ever paid in honest fuel, and every one that went in, the thing above them flinched, and coughed smoke, and said, with real surprise, "*Oh, that's hot.*"

Then the Vale began to fight.

It came from every side of the mountain at once. From the shoulder high above them, a white beam stabbed out of Stillhollow's dome, out of Edda Quill's great lens, and laid itself across the demon's mouths like a bar, and held. From far to the west, out on the estuary at the edge of the world, another light: Agathe Voss's lamp, turned inland for the first time in its life, stopping on the upper mouths, so the thing turned its head like a man from a lamp held in his face. From the red cliffs of Cinderholt, smoke, and then the sound, arriving late, a long double boom, and two bore-cannon shots went through the gold body and sparks came out of the far side like a struck forge. From the high meadows, a dark cloud moving against the wind, fast and low across the orchards: every bee in Thistledown, going into the demon's mouths like smoke into a chimney, and Mawrrow coughed.

And from Hollowmere, from down in the valley ahead of them where the lake shone, the bell.

Pip had got there. It tolled, slow and huge and late, and Wren felt it go through the air the way the Bell-Warden's tolls had gone through the bell chamber, and above them the great gold arm that was reaching down toward the oath road slowed. Slowed. Every toll, slower, like a hand in honey.

"*Oh,*" said Mawrrow, with pleasure. "*The bell.*"

The lens didn't move. Wren watched it, on the oath road, with a child crying for bread in her lap, and it never moved. It held on the same mouth until the gold there went black at the edges, long after the beam should have been put out, long after anyone with eyes would have looked away. She didn't know then whose hands were on it. She found out afterward.

The hand came down anyway. Slowly. Tolled down, a stroke at a time. Not on the oath road.

On Hollowmere.

### 7.11

*Hollowmere, the bell tower and the feast gate.*

Pip saw her grandmother die from the top of the bell tower, and couldn't hear a thing, because the bell was going.

She'd run the oath road down from the Hearth in less time than anybody had ever run it, and gone through the feast gate shouting, and across the square where the people of Hollowmere were going into the church in a long frightened line, and up the tower stair, and Amos Gill had been there already. Standing under his bell with the rope in both hands and his whole weight on it, looking up at the sky through the louvres, ringing, because the sky had a demon in it and nobody had needed to tell him.

"*I know*," he'd bellowed, at her open mouth, before she'd said a word. "*I can see it, girl. I'm deaf, not blind.*"

So she'd helped. She'd taken the second rope and hung on it, and the bell went, and went, and every stroke came up through her feet into her teeth, and through the louvres she could see the great gold arm in the sky over the mountain slow with every toll. She'd laughed. She'd been laughing when the hand turned.

It came down over the lake. It was as big as the square. It came down slowly, tolled down, a stroke at a time, burning gold, with its fingers spread, and its shadow went over the water and up the lane and over the roofs, and the people in the line at the church door started to run.

And down the Hearth road, out of the orchard, came the carts.

Pip saw them from above, small as toys: the first of Ned's carts through the feast gate into the square, then the next, Wat's oxen bellowing, children standing up in the carts in their feast clothes, and Marta Fenn running across the cobbles from the church door toward the last one with her arms out. The church door was open. The children were being got out of the carts and in through it, handed down, carried, dragged. The hand came down over all of it.

Gran was on the gate.

Pip saw her come out of the watch-house at the bottom of the lane with the pike, not running, Gran never ran, and walk up to the feast gate, two posts and an arch, the post where she'd counted heads on the Long Night. She stood in the arch. The carts went past her, one, two, three. She touched each one on the tailboard as it went, Pip could see her do it. Counting.

Then a child came out of the church, against the stream. Small, in a shawl that went round her three times. Running the wrong way, toward the gate, toward Hob's cart, where Tobin was on the seat with Vane chained to the tailboard behind him, and Wren's cart, the last, still coming down the lane.

*Nell.*

Pip screamed. She didn't hear it. The bell was going.

Tobin was off the cart. She saw him go. Nell got to him at the gate and caught hold of his sleeve, the way she must have caught it eleven years ago, two small fists in the cloth, and Pip watched from the top of the tower and waited for him to pull it out of her hands.

He didn't. He bent and picked her up with one arm, and the axe in the other hand, both, the way he'd known he'd have to, and ran.

He was slow. He was carrying both. The hand came down over the gate. Wren's cart went under the arch beneath it, the last, with Gran's hand flat on its tailboard as it passed, and Tobin was behind it on foot, with Nell, and the shadow of the fingers was on the cobbles all round him.

Gran stepped out into the lane under it with the pike.

Pip saw her put the pike up, into the gold, into the palm of the thing, the way you'd put it into a man coming at the gate, and set her feet. The fingers closed. They closed on the arch, round it, on the post where she'd counted, on her, and on nothing else, and behind her Tobin went through the gap she'd made and across the square with Nell and the axe, and in at the church door, and Marta slammed it behind them.

The bell went. Amos was still ringing. He couldn't hear her.

The hand lifted.

The arch was gone. The posts were gone. Where the feast gate had stood there was a heap of broken stone on the cobbles, and a pike-haft snapped in two, and under what had been the arch, a shape, quite small, that didn't move. Pip couldn't see more than that from the tower. She never let herself see more than that, afterwards, when she thought about it, which was every time she walked through that place, which was every day.

"*Thank you,*" said Mawrrow, very gently, to the gate.

Pip let go of the rope. Amos looked round at her. He looked down through the louvres at the square, at the broken gate. Then he looked back at her, and his old deaf face did something she'd never seen it do, and he put his hand on her shoulder, and kept ringing with the other.

She took the rope again. She rang it. She rang it until her hands bled, every stroke for Gran, every one for the gate, and the great gold hand in the sky moved slower and slower, and the people of Hollowmere were in their church, all of them, and the door was shut, and the four were outside it.

The four.

She looked down. Ash was on the church steps with his bow. Wren was coming across the square from the last cart with the staff. And Tobin came out of the church door, alone, and shut it behind him on Nell, and looked up.

The sky was full of mouths. One of them was coming down.

### 7.12

*The square. Then inside the maw.*

The mouth stopped above the church roof and opened like a door, and Ash heard the voice he'd listened to for four years say, warmly, "*Do come in.*"

It hung there over the square, a cave of ember and gold as wide as the church, its lip resting on the cobbles in front of the steps like a ramp. Warm air came out of it. The smell of the feast: pork in cider, baked apples. Inside, a long way in, there were lights.

"It can't put its hand through the ward," said Vane, from the tailboard of Hob's cart in the middle of the square. "So it's asking. It can't take you. It can only ask."

"Then we're going in," said Wren.

Tobin looked at her. Then he went to the cart, and unlocked Dun's chain from the wheel, and locked it round his own left wrist instead, with Vane on the other end of it.

"You wanted to be there," he said.

"Yes," said Vane.

They walked up the lip of the mouth into Mawrrow, the four of them and an old man on a chain, and behind them it closed, gently, the way you close a door on a room where somebody's sleeping.

Inside was the square.

It was Hollowmere's square on the feast night, the first one, before any of it: the banners in wheat-gold and berry-red, the lanterns on their lines not lit, the long tables down the middle with the cloths on and the plates laid. Every place was empty except one. Cantor Lisle sat halfway down the near table in his choir white with his eyes closed and his hands folded on the cloth, quite still, at peace, like a man after a good meal. Nobody went near him.

At the head of the tables, where the elders had stood to say the words, was the heart.

It was as big as the bell. It hung in the air above the elders' place, ember-red and slow, beating, and round it in rings of fire, like the hoops round a barrel, ran the Bargain in the script. Every line. And deep inside it, in the middle of all that red, small as a fist and dark as a stone, something else was beating too, out of time. A man's heart.

Vane made a sound beside Ash. Only one.

"*Sit anywhere,*" said Mawrrow. "*There's room.*"

Then it served.

It was the worst fight of Ash's life and he could never tell the order of it afterward. The heart sent courses down the tables at them, covered dishes that opened on the Brazen Ox, the Lamp-Lure's light, the Curve uncoiling from a tureen, every nightmare they'd beaten coming back on a plate. The written rings round the heart turned and caught everything they threw. Ash's arrows went into the fire letters and stopped there, hanging. Tobin's axe rang off the word *forfeit* like a bell.

And between the courses, when the hall went quiet and the lanterns flickered, it made each of them an offer.

It was polite. Ash heard the others'. He couldn't help it.

To Tobin it showed a woman at a table, alive, with flour on her hands and a cross mouth, who said *Tobin, wash*, and Tobin stopped dead in the middle of the hall with the chain hanging off his wrist. "*Jessamy,*" said the voice. "*Only one seat. Any one of theirs. You choose.*" Tobin stood a long time. Then he said, "No," and the woman went away, and he didn't watch her go.

To Pip it showed the feast gate, whole, and Odile on it with her pike, alive, turning to look, and said, "*Today. Now. She's still warm.*" Pip didn't say anything at all. She put Gran's shield up, and walked through the gate, and it was smoke.

To Wren it showed Isolde Hale at her desk, young, before the vote, before any of it, with no grey on her hand. Wren looked at her for a long time. "I know whose names are on my wall," she said, and the woman looked up, startled, and was gone.

Then it was Ash's turn. And it didn't show him anything. It only talked to him, the way it always had, up on the warm rock with his ear to the crack.

"*Everything,*" it said, gently. "*You know I can. Jory with no cairn to sit on. Your mother with no grandfather. Him, with a heart. The rock warm whenever you want it. Me, whenever you want me.*" A pause, fond. "*And every answer, Ash. Every question you've ever had and all the ones you haven't thought of yet. You love answers. You'd have them all.*" Quieter still. "*All you have to do is let her word fail. Just let it go small. You won't have to do anything. Only don't help.*"

Ash stood in the hall and thought about it.

He did. He'd always be ashamed of that, and he'd always be glad he'd done it honestly. He went down it the way he'd gone down the wall in his head on the scree, weighing, and it was the most interesting sum he'd ever been offered, and he knew the answer before he'd finished, which was new.

"No," he said. "I don't want to know."

"*Oh, Ash,*" said Mawrrow, with real sorrow. "*Yes, you do.*"

"Yes," said Ash. "I do. No."

Then the heirlooms.

Tobin went first. He went up the middle of the long table, not round it, on it, with the chain dragging Vane after him over the plates, and brought Marshfell down on the boards with both hands, and the table split. End to end, the whole length of the square, with a sound like a tree coming down, and every plate went into the crack, and there was no table. Nowhere to sit. No place laid.

The heart flinched. Every ring of writing round it shuddered, and in the shudder it struck at Wren. It sent its whole weight down the broken hall at her, a fist of ember the size of a cart, and Pip was there. Ash didn't see her get there. She was simply in front of Wren with Last Watch up, Gran's shield, Dun's rim, and the blow came down on it, and the shield broke. It broke in half with a crack like the bell's headstock, and Pip went down on one knee under it, and didn't move, and Wren behind her didn't have a mark on her.

The rings were open. Only for a breath.

Ash went in with Brother's Due. Not the bow; the sword, Jory's, the one he'd always been stealing. He went into the heart where the writing had parted, into the red, and it was like putting his arm into an oven, and he found the small dark beating thing in the middle of it and cut round it, once, the way you'd cut a stone out of a plum.

It came away in his hand. It was cold. It was beating.

He came out of the fire with his sleeve burning and Corwin Vane's heart in his fist, and his great-grandfather was standing at the end of the chain looking at it, with Tobin's arm across his chest holding him back.

"Not here," said Vane. His voice shook, for the first time Ash had ever heard. "Please. Not in its mouth. Outside. In the air."

Ash put it inside his coat, against his own, and turned round.

Wren was walking up the broken table toward the heart with her mother's staff, and the brass foot of it had begun to glow.

### 7.13

*Inside the maw. The end of the Bargain.*

True North burned the colour of a lantern seen through fog, and Wren knew it, because she'd carried that colour down a tunnel at eight years old and watched it not go out.

It wasn't a word's light. Every mark she'd ever made had burned gold, the demon's gold. This was yellow, small, warm, the colour of oil in a tin lantern on an iron hook, and it came up through the staff from the brass foot as she walked, past the black and white bands of her mother's survey marks, until the whole staff was lit with it like a wick. *I filled the oil. That's all I did.*

She stood at the head of the broken table, in front of the heart, awake.

Behind her Tobin was holding Vane on the chain, and Ash was standing with his hand on his coat and his sleeve smoking, and Pip was on one knee in the wreck of her grandmother's shield, looking up. In front of her the heart beat, slow and red, with the rings of writing turning round it. She could read every word. She'd been able to read every word for a long time.

"*Wren,*" said Mawrrow, quite kindly. "*Sit down. You've walked such a long way.*"

She read it the forfeit clause.

Aloud, in the script, in its own letters, the way her mother had said you had to: not a picture, a word, a thing that wants to be said. *If the tithe falls short, the table is forfeit.* The ring of fire it was written in went still as she said it. Then the last line, the one Vane had put his hand over and burned off her mother's page. *Whoever stands at the table unbound, awake before the Host, may speak against this writing, and what is spoken will end it.*

"I'm standing at it," said Wren. "There isn't one."

Then she wrote.

Under the forfeit clause, in the air, in the demon's own hand, with her mother's staff and her own grey finger along the shaft: four words. She said them as she wrote them, each one as it went, because that was the only way she'd ever known how to do it. Short down, long up.

*The table is empty.*

She felt it go out on the last word.

Not the word. The word was said, and stayed said; the whole hall heard it. But the letters. Halfway through the last stroke of the last mark, the light went out of the line under her hand, the way a candle goes out when it's pinched, and she was standing in front of a heart drawing in the air with a stick. Her forefinger went cold. Not the knuckle; the whole of it, to the root, all at once, stone. She felt the warm go out of it for the last time, and knew it wouldn't come back, and was surprised to find she didn't mind as much as she'd thought.

The writing round the heart went dark. Every ring. The letters stopped burning and were just marks, strange shapes she looked at and couldn't read. She tried. She looked at the word *forfeit*, which she had known for a year, and it was a scratch.

*You'll be a girl with a stick.*

"*Oh,*" said Mawrrow.

It said it quietly. It said it like a host who's been told, at the end of the evening, very politely, that there's nothing more in the kitchen.

"*Oh. I see.*"

Then it began to go out.

It didn't fight. It didn't scream. It went the way a fire goes when there's no more wood: smaller, and quieter, and politer. The hall drew in round them like a tent with its pole pulled. The banners came down, the lanterns, the long broken tables, Lisle in his chair, all folding away into the dark without a sound, and the heart shrank with it, the size of the bell, the size of a cart, a barrel, a dog, and then they were standing on cobbles, in the open, in the evening, in Hollowmere's square, where the long tables had stood on the Long Night.

The sky was empty and turning violet. The mountain stood behind the church, closed. There was a smell of smoke and bees.

On the cobbles in front of Wren lay something the size of a dog, curled, red, glowing gently, like the last coals of a good fire. It lifted what might have been its head. It had one mouth now, small, and it was smiling.

"*Thank you,*" it said. "*It was a lovely meal.*"

And it was a stone. A warm red stone, the size of a loaf, on the bare cobbles. The glow went out of it while they watched.

Wren stood with her mother's staff in her hand, which was a staff. It had black and white bands on it, for measuring, and a brass foot, and a bent iron hook at the top with a cracked lantern on it that she'd never once mended, and nothing else.

All round the square, in the violet light, the Gleaners were lying down. Wren saw them: on the church steps, by the fountain, in the lane where the feast gate had been, scorched shapes in sheaf masks lying down on the cobbles like tired harvesters at the end of a day, and the ember going out of the cracks in them, and their masks coming off. Under them were faces. Ordinary ones.

Across the lake, in the last of the light, the fields round Hollowmere were moving. She'd seen the barley in them that morning, heavy-headed and gold, rich as it had been every summer of her life. It was settling now, the way a loaf settles out of the oven, thinning, the gold going out of it, until it stood in the evening like barley anywhere, short and patchy, with weeds in it.

Then the church door opened, and the children came out, because inside it every lantern that had burned without oil for eleven years had gone out at once, and they were frightened of the dark.

### 7.14

*The high scree above Hollowmere. Dawn.*

Ash gave his great-grandfather his heart on the scree at sunrise, beside the fissure, which was cold.

He'd felt it on the way up: the stripe of bare rock where the snow never settled, the crack no wider than a knife, the place where he'd lain with his ear to the stone for four years. He'd put his hand on it in the grey without meaning to. Cold as any rock on any mountain. Nothing under it. Nobody clearing their throat.

Vane sat on Jory's unstacked cairn-stones with his hands in his lap, unchained. Tobin had unlocked the chain from his own wrist in the square at midnight, and held it out to Ash, and gone home to Nell without a word, and he hadn't come up, and he never would, and he never asked.

"You'll want it now," said Ash.

"I've wanted it sixty years." Vane looked east, at the line of light coming over the downs. "I'd like to see the sun first. I've not seen it come up since the year nought."

They waited for it. It came up over the downs, small and white, and then gold, and the whole Vale filled with it at once like a bowl filling with water, and Ash watched his great-grandfather watch it, and saw his face, and looked away.

He took the heart out of his coat and put it in Vane's hands.

It was beating, still, cold and out of time, the way it had in the red. Vane held it in both palms, the way you'd hold a bird. For a moment nothing happened. Then it slowed. Ash watched it slow. It went quiet in the old man's hands the way a frightened animal goes quiet when it's held long enough, and stopped, and was still.

"There," said Corwin Vane.

He looked older. Not all at once; it came into his face like the light was coming into the Vale, gently, and his hands went thin and spotted, and his hair went white, and he was a hundred and four years old, sitting on a pile of stones on a mountain in the morning, and he didn't seem to mind at all.

"Ash," he said.

"Yes."

"Tell Hester nothing kind about me." His voice was going. "Don't let anyone make a stone. No pageant. I've had a pageant." Something moved in his face, and it might have been the beginning of a smile, or of something else. "It's very bad."

Ash didn't say anything. He sat down on the rock beside him, and the sun came up, and inside a minute Corwin Vane was dead, sitting up, with his face to the light and his heart in his lap.

Ash buried him beside the fissure. He did it himself, with Jory's survey spade and his own hands, in the thin ground between the rocks, and it took most of the morning, and nobody came. He didn't put a stone on it. No churchyard in the Vale would have taken him, and Ash wasn't sure he'd have asked.

He didn't call him anything. Not at the end, not after. He thought about it, standing over the grave with the spade, and found there was nothing to call him that was true and that he could bear, so he left it.

Then he went down the scree into Hollowmere, and across the square, to where a stone the size of a loaf lay on the cobbles where the long tables had stood. Nobody would touch it. The children had been walked round it on the way out of the church. It was cold now. He picked it up, and it was only heavy, and he walked down to the lake road and threw it in the lake as far as he could, and watched the rings go out on the water until there weren't any.

When he came back up through the churchyard, Bram Tolley was standing by the gate with a mason's hammer in both hands, in front of a memorial stone. CORWIN VANE, WHO CARRIED THE FIRST TITHE INTO THE DEEP TO SEE IT SETTLED. Bram was white to the lips. He'd written the pageant. He'd made the Sedge boy wear the wool beard.

He looked at Ash. Ash didn't say anything.

Bram lifted the hammer and broke the stone in half, and then in quarters, and stood there in the dust shaking, and Ash thought it was probably the bravest thing Bram Tolley had ever done, and went home.

### 7.15

*Hollowmere. The weeks after.*

Pip sat in Gran's chair by Gran's door, because somebody should sit by it, and found out that a chair can be the heaviest thing in a town.

They buried Odile Ostrander in the churchyard on the third day, and the whole of Hollowmere came, and half of Saltwake, rowed across by Kip in shifts. Pip didn't cry at it. She stood at the front with her hands behind her back the way Gran stood at the gate, and watched the faces, all of them, and counted them in. Afterward people told her things. They kept telling her things for weeks: that Gran had let them past once, that Gran had caught them once and let them go, that Gran had thrown a man in the harbour off Saltwake quay forty years ago for a remark about her father's cheeses. Pip listened to all of it and said thank you. She didn't tell any of it to anyone. It was theirs.

She put what was left of Last Watch on two nails over Gran's hearth: the two halves of the shield side by side with a finger's gap between them, Gran's dagger crossed under. She looked at it every night. It looked like a broken thing. It looked like what it was.

Dun Harrow came for it in the second week. He stood in Gran's doorway with his arms folded, and didn't come in, and looked at the shield on the wall the way he'd looked at Pip all her life, like a light that hurt.

"I can mend that," he said.

"Can you?"

"I made the rim." He came in, then, and took it down, both halves, carefully, the way you'd lift a hurt dog, and went off down the lane with it under his arm without asking, and she followed him, because it was hers.

He mended it at his own anvil, with Hilde Saxby's alloy, over three days, and she sat on the pig iron in the corner of his forge the whole time and didn't talk, and he didn't either. On the third evening he fitted the last rivet and held the shield up to the light. You could see the mend. A bright seam down the middle of it, a different metal, where it had broken. He didn't try to hide it.

He stood there holding it, and Pip watched him decide.

"Your mother," said Dun Harrow, to the shield. "Before she went downriver." He stopped. "I never claimed you. I should've. Odile wanted me to, and I didn't, and then you were hers, and it was easier." He put the shield down on the anvil. "You're mine, Pip. That's what I'm saying. I'm your—"

"I know," said Pip.

He looked at her.

"Since the cistern," she said. "You looked at me like a bill you'd hoped had got lost." Her voice was rusty still. It would be, now; she'd stopped expecting it not to be. "Gran knew I knew. She never said. Neither did I."

Dun Harrow stood in his forge with his big burned hands hanging, and his face did something, and then he picked up the shield and held it out to her.

"It'll take a knock," he said. "Better than before. Mended places do."

She took it home and hung it back on the two nails, whole, with the seam showing.

Marta buried Abner Stroud the same week, in a coffin Hob made, with nothing in it. Davy and Col stood on either side of her at the grave in borrowed black, holding her hands, and asked in loud whispers where their da was, and Marta said, "He chose," and the cleaver was in her apron pocket the whole time. Pip saw the handle. Nobody said anything about it.

That night Pip watched Wren paint the wall.

Every name was ticked now. Every one, all the way down both columns, *At the table* too, ninety-three ticks in the second column alone, Wren doing them with her left hand and her stone finger held out stiff from the brush like a lady with a teacup. Only two names were different. Abner Stroud's pale square, with nothing in it. And Gran's. *Odile Ostrander*, ticked like the others, and round the tick, very carefully, a black ring.

"Is that all right?" said Wren, not turning round.

Pip looked at it for a long time. Then she went out and sat in Gran's chair, by Gran's door, at the bottom of the gate lane, where there was no gate any more, and watched the lane, and kept the watch.

### 7.16

*Hollowmere and the pass road. The end of summer.*

Ash told her on the step, a month after, in the dark, and he told nobody else, and Wren never did either.

"I miss it," he said. "The voice."

She didn't say anything.

"I go up there," said Ash. "I lie on the rock. It's cold. I know it's cold, I know there's nothing. I go up anyway." He laughed, short. "Four years of somebody always being glad I'd come. Always an answer. I miss it the way you'd miss a tooth that hurt." He looked at his hands, which were cold now, like anybody's. "That's a terrible thing to say."

"Yes," said Wren.

"Will you tell them?"

"No."

He nodded, and they sat on the step, and after a while he leaned his shoulder against hers, and she let him, and that was all it was.

Her mother left at the end of summer, on the first cool morning, with the satchel.

She'd packed it at the start of summer, the week of the empty meeting. It had stood by the bed, ready, all through the Hearthdeep and the fire and the burying, and on the morning she went she simply picked it up, the way you pick up a basket to go to market, and came downstairs. She had a stick. It was a plain ash stick from Hob's yard, and her right forefinger lay along it grey to the second knuckle, as Wren's lay along her own staff grey to the root. Neither of them would warm again. They'd talked about that once, in the summer, at the desk, briefly, like two people comparing a bill.

"Stillhollow, then over," said Isolde, at the door. "Sorrel Kett's taking a train in the autumn. I'll be ahead of it."

"You don't have to go."

"No." Her mother looked down the lane, at the doors, every one with somebody behind it now. Twenty-three of them with a child behind it who'd sat at a table she'd voted to lay. "I've been a list in this town. I'll not be a list at every door for the rest of my life. And they'd be kind about it, Wren. That's worse." She put her hand on Wren's face, once, the stone finger cold against her cheek. "You'll have the house."

"And the wall," said Wren, "and the cat."

Isolde Hale almost smiled.

Wren walked her to the top of the town, where the drovers' road went off toward Thistledown and the pass, and stopped there, because that was where she'd decided to stop. Her mother didn't ask her to come further. She went up the road with the satchel and the stick, a straight-backed woman in a grey coat, getting smaller.

At the bend she stopped and looked back.

Not at Wren. Wren saw it, and saw where her mother's eyes went: past her, down the hill, to the timber yard at the bottom of the mill road, where a big man was standing in the yard gate with a small girl on his shoulders in a shawl that went round her three times. Hob Marsh didn't wave. He didn't move at all. He stood there with Nell on his shoulders and watched Isolde Hale go round the bend in the road, and when she'd gone he stood there a while longer.

Wren did the sum.

Hep Lowe's big fellow who'd carried the books up to the scriptorium every spring. *He would.* A desk made in a night, unasked, with dovetails you couldn't slide a hair into. Her mother's door, which she'd always meant to move because it faced the wind. *H.M. — T., N.*, in her mother's hand, the first of the four, before Jory, before Odile.

She stood at the top of the town with the answer and looked at it, and found she wasn't angry, and wasn't even surprised, and that she'd known the shape of it for a long time without the figures.

When she got home Tobin was splitting wood in her yard, the way he did, and looked up. "She go all right?"

"Yes," said Wren.

She didn't tell him. It wasn't hers.

She put it in a box of its own, beside the other one, and left the lid open, and went in and made him tea.

### 7.17

*Hollowmere square, then Wren's house. Autumn, PY 61.*

The first harvest without a deal was thin, and it came in on time, and Tobin had never been so proud of anything in his life.

It was short. Short sheaves, blighted rows, a whole field of Silas's lower orchard gone to scab, the barley round the lake standing no higher than Nell. The kind of harvest the old people remembered from before the Bargain, and hadn't wanted to. There'd be a hungry winter. Everybody knew it. They brought it in anyway, every town in the Vale, by hand, and on the evening of the harvest they brought it to Hollowmere's square.

They came by every road. Saltwake's carts, with salt fish in barrels and Teague Pellow weeping into his hat already. Cinderholt's, with plough-irons from Hilde's foundry, and Dagny Ferrule driving, and people moving away from her at the gate, and her driving anyway. Thistledown's honey, and Clemency's bees, and the novices singing something in the orchard that wasn't a hymn. Stillhollow's mules down the pass road in a long string, with Sorrel Kett at the head, and Edda Quill on the second mule leading the Provost's by the bridle. The Provost sat very straight with his eyes on nothing. He'd held the lens on it after Edda told him to stop, and long after. Edda talked to him the whole way down, crossly, about the lens, and so did the rest of Stillhollow now. The Hearth's charcoal, and Gerda Brand, who'd brought no oath, and Linnet, who'd brought bread that had nothing off the top of anybody.

It wasn't enough. It went on the long tables in the square anyway, all of it, under lanterns that Wynn Cobb lit by hand an hour before dusk, every one, with a word to each. Nobody wrote a name anywhere. Nobody weighed anything.

Before anyone ate, Amos Gill rang the bell.

He rang it late and much too loud, the way he always did now, and the whole Vale stood still in the square till it was done. Then the children sat down. Ninety-three of them, at the ends of the long tables on the little chairs, and they were the loudest people there. They'd been hungry since noon of the waking. They'd been hungry, Tobin thought, for eleven years, and now they had a real reason, and they shouted about it.

Halfway through, Hollis Rye climbed onto a bench with a cup in his hand and confessed. He'd been working up to it all summer. He told the whole square, at the top of his voice, that for the last eight years before the Long Night he'd chalked the feast loaves, a spoon of chalk in every sack, to make the flour go further and keep a sack back for himself. He stood there afterward with his face red, waiting.

Nobody cared. Marta said, "Sit down, Hollis," and somebody passed him the salt fish.

He sat down. He was furious about it for a week.

Marta fed Tobin first. She came down the table with the pot before anyone else and put a ladle of stew on his plate, and stood there till he ate it, the way she'd stood over Nell on the feast night. She didn't say anything. The cleaver wasn't in her apron. Tobin noticed that, and ate the stew.

Nell sat on his knee for most of it, because he'd said he'd carry her, and she'd taken him at his word, and he'd carried her everywhere since: to the pump, to the yard, up the lamp lane in the early light to watch Wynn. She hadn't forgiven him. He could tell. She would. He could tell that too. His father sat across the table from them with a cup in front of him that he didn't drink much of, and didn't look up the pass road once all evening, which Tobin noticed, and didn't understand, and didn't ask about.

He'd slept till noon, the week before. Once. Woken in the light with nobody shouting and nothing to carry, and lain there, frightened, waiting for something to have died. Nothing had.

Afterward they went up to Wren's.

The wall was a memorial now: every name ticked, one painted out, one in a black ring. The lamp was in the window. Wren lit it with a spill from the fire, the ordinary way, cupping her good hand round it. There'd been a little round glow over that table once, the colour of a lantern seen through fog, that had stayed lit with nothing feeding it. It wasn't there. It wouldn't be again.

Tuppence lay on the cost book, a scruffy grey tom with one torn ear and a collar that was a collar. His fur lay flat. It had lain flat since the evening of the waking, the first time since Tobin had known him, and he seemed to resent it bitterly, and to blame Wren.

Pip had her mended shield on the floor by her chair. Ash had his crooked nose and his ordinary cold hands and Brother's Due on the hook by the door, beside Wren's staff, which had black and white bands on it, for measuring fields.

"So what do we do now?" said Pip.

"Eat," said Tobin. "Then plant."

Wren didn't say anything. She was looking at the wall.

"It'll be a hard winter," said Ash.

"We've had worse," said Tobin.

And for once nobody made a joke. They sat round the table in the lamplight, the four of them and the cat, with the names behind them and the door shut and nobody outside it who hadn't come home, and nobody said anything for a long time, and it was all right.

"Same time tomorrow," said Wren.

"Where else would we go," said Pip.
